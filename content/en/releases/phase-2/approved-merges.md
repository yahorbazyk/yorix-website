```markdown
## Home apnea monitors and consumer “SIDS monitors”

Consumer gadgets that claim to watch a baby’s breathing or to “prevent SIDS” are not a substitute for the safer-sleep steps already on this page. HealthyChildren.org’s page on home apnea monitors is direct: **research has not shown a clear link between ordinary apnea and SIDS**, and **home apnea monitors give little or no protection from SIDS**. The American Academy of Pediatrics’ 2022 safe-sleep policy, already the backbone of this article, **does not recommend** using commercial cardiorespiratory monitors as a strategy to reduce the risk of SIDS. Devices marketed to reduce SIDS are not a safer-sleep plan. Lullaby Trust and Red Nose similarly caution families not to treat a gadget as protection.

In **rare** cases a doctor may prescribe a home monitor — HealthyChildren.org names examples such as a baby who needs **home oxygen** or who has **serious breathing problems**. If that happens, use the device as prescribed and still keep the crib rules on this page. Do not buy a consumer sock, camera, or pad in place of back-sleeping, a firm flat surface, a clear crib, and room-sharing.
```

```markdown
HealthyChildren.org’s “Stages of Newborn Sleep” describes **active (REM) sleep** as a restless stretch: the baby may twitch, and **the eyes move under closed lids**. Nightmares are scary dreams. As the table above already says, the child usually **wakes**, often later in the night, can often **remember** the dream, and wants comfort. ## If the pediatrician mentions a sleep study, or tonsils
```

```markdown
A **sleep study** (polysomnogram) is an overnight test, usually in a hospital or sleep center, that records how a child actually sleeps (KidsHealth, reviewed November 2021). Sensors can track breathing, oxygen, carbon dioxide, heart rate, brain waves, eye movements, snoring, and body movement. One parent can usually stay. Other precautions on that page: place the bunk in a corner so two sides are walled; the top mattress must fit snugly and cannot ride over the edge; attach a ladder and use a night-light so the child can see it; install a top-bunk guardrail with a gap **no wider than 3½ inches**, and check the child cannot roll under it when the mattress compresses; mattress supports should be fastened slats or wires, not a loose frame; no jumping or roughhousing on either bunk. If you separate the bunks into twins, remove leftover connectors.
```

```markdown
**Bed rails.** Use only a rail made for that bed, installed as the maker says, if a clinician or the bed’s age-and-design instructions call for one. Do not improvise with pillows or a gap that can trap a head or chest. This is not a shopping review; if the child is still in a crib, rails on an adult bed are the wrong product.

**Crib tents and “safety” nets over a crib.** Do not use them. They are not a recommended way to keep a climber in. Mesh or fabric over a crib can loosen, trap, or become a climbing-and-fall hazard, and they fight the bare-crib rule already on [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies). If a child is climbing out, that is a reason to plan the toddler-bed move on this page, not to zip the crib shut. HealthyChildren.org’s lists of sleep products to avoid include add-ons that make a crib less bare or less safe; a tent is in that family, not in the “helpful accessory” family.
```

```markdown
## Periodic breathing (a normal newborn pattern, not a home diagnosis)

**Periodic breathing** is a common newborn pattern: a **brief pause**, then several faster “catch-up” breaths, then a steadier rhythm, often in sleep, often repeating a few times. HealthyChildren.org’s “Stages of Newborn Sleep” describes pauses of **5 to 10 seconds** in active sleep, then a burst of faster breathing (they mention 50 to 60 breaths a minute for 10 to 15 seconds), then regular breathing again. The baby’s **color does not change**. Cleveland Clinic (updated 12 February 2025) uses the same idea: pauses usually last **5 to 10 seconds**, the next breaths are often fast, and the cycle may repeat three or more times before settling.

Sources do **not** use one “it stops at” age, and we will not average them. Cleveland Clinic: most likely at **2 to 4 weeks**, and usually considered normal **up to 6 months**; tell the pediatrician if it continues after that. HealthyChildren.org’s stages page: babies generally outgrow it by about the **middle of the first year**. An older HealthyChildren.org symptom-checker note says this pattern **usually resolves by 1 month**. We report all three clocks. The NHS Scotland leaflet already on this page uses a slightly tighter “normal pause” picture (up to about 5 seconds; should not stop longer than 10 seconds). Use the leaflet’s home-check steps in the next section rather than timing pauses from memory at 3 a.m.

Periodic breathing is **not** the same as a diagnosis of **apnea** (Cleveland Clinic: a pause of 20 seconds or longer, or a shorter pause with a slow heart rate or low oxygen). It is also not a reason to buy a consumer “SIDS monitor” — see [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies).
```
