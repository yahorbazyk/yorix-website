# Night Weaning: When Night Feeds Can Stop, and How to Ease Them Out

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

**Night weaning** means gradually dropping the feeds your baby still takes between bedtime and morning, once those calories are no longer needed for growth. It is not the same as a [dream feed](/dream-feed), which adds a late-evening feed, and it is not the same as [sleep training](/sleep-training-methods-explained), though families sometimes do both. It is also not something to rush in a newborn. The right time depends on age, whether your baby is breastfed or formula-fed, weight gain, and what your pediatrician says — not on a neighbor's story.

## Short answer

Newborns need night feeds. After the first month, HealthyChildren.org says that if a baby is gaining well you often do not need to *wake* them for a feed; they may still wake and need one. For **formula-fed** babies, a HealthyChildren.org clinician Q&A states that between **2 and 4 months**, or once the baby weighs **at least 12 pounds**, most no longer need a middle-of-the-night feed. **Breastfed** babies often need night feeds longer, because breast milk is digested faster and because night nursing also supports supply. Sleep Foundation notes that by **9 to 12 months** most infants can sleep longer at night without a feed. If you do night-wean, do it gradually, offer more calories by day, and stop and call your pediatrician if weight gain slows, wet diapers fall off, or your baby seems unwell.

## Night weaning is optional — and it is a medical conversation first

A baby who still wakes to eat at 7 or 8 months may be hungry, thirsty, in the habit of feeding to sleep, teething, or all of those at once. The only way to know whether night calories can go is to look at **daytime intake, growth, and health** with a clinician. Do not night-wean, or delay needed feeds, if:

- your baby was premature, has a medical condition, or is not back to birth weight in the early weeks
- weight gain has slowed, or your pediatrician has you on a feeding plan
- wet diapers have dropped off (HealthyChildren.org mentions at least four wet diapers a day as one of the "do not worry about waking them" signs for a growing baby)
- your baby is ill, in a growth spurt, or newly started on a change such as [starting solids](/when-to-start-solids)

If those boxes are not a concern and your pediatrician is comfortable with a plan, you can decide whether *you* want to night-wean. Plenty of families keep one night feed for months because it is easy, or because they are breastfeeding and want to protect supply. That is a valid choice.

## Breast milk and formula are not the same clock

Formula-fed babies often stretch at night earlier. The HealthyChildren.org figure — most no longer need a middle-of-the-night bottle between 2 and 4 months, or from about 12 pounds — is specifically about formula. It does not automatically transfer to breastfeeding.

Breastfed babies commonly continue night feeds past that point. Breast milk leaves the stomach faster, night nursing helps maintain supply, and the AAP recommends continued breastfeeding well beyond the age when many formula-fed babies have dropped the night bottle. If you are exclusively breastfeeding and you cut night feeds sharply, you may become uncomfortably full, and your supply may dip unless you express or add a day feed. A gradual approach is kinder to both of you. HealthyChildren.org's general weaning advice (drop a feeding every few days, or even once a week) is a reasonable pace to borrow for nights, even though that page is about weaning from the breast overall.

A [dream feed](/dream-feed) is sometimes used as a bridge: one last feed at the adult's bedtime, then a longer stretch. The evidence for dream feeds is thin; we walk through it on that page. It is not required for night weaning.

## A gradual way to drop a night feed

There is no single AAP step-by-step protocol titled "night weaning." What follows is a cautious, commonly used approach that stays consistent with gradual-weaning advice and with "feed more by day." It is not a medical protocol.

1. **Confirm the plan** with your pediatrician, including how many night feeds you are dropping and over what period.
2. **Watch daytime intake for a week.** If days are light and nights are heavy, fix days first — that pattern is sometimes called reverse cycling, and cutting nights alone often backfires.
3. **Pick one feed to change**, usually the one at which your baby takes the least or the one closest to morning, unless a clinician has told you otherwise.
4. **Shrink it slowly** if you bottle-feed (a little less milk every two or three nights) or **shorten it** if you breastfeed (a few minutes less, or one side instead of two). Jumping from a full feed to nothing overnight is more likely to mean a very distressed baby and, if you are lactating, sore fullness.
5. **Offer comfort in other ways** once the feed is no longer the whole visit: a brief hold, a back rub, a [pacifier](/pacifier-and-sleep) if you use one, or a simple check-in. This is where night weaning can overlap with the methods on our sleep-training pages; you do not have to use a formal method.
6. **Give it several nights** before you judge it, unless your baby is clearly not coping or growth becomes a concern.
7. **Protect bedtime and the first stretch of the night.** An [overtired](/overtired-baby) baby and a very late bedtime make every remaining waking harder.

Night comfort that is a bottle of milk left in the crib is not a safe shortcut. HealthyChildren.org warns that bottles in bed raise the risk of tooth decay and can contribute to ear infections.

## What if my baby still wakes after the feed is gone?

That is common. Hunger was one reason they woke; habit, a short sleep cycle, a wet diaper, or a bump in the [sleep regression](/sleep-regression) calendar may still be there. Give the new pattern a week or two. If wakings get more frequent and daytime eating drops, or if you are worried about growth, go back to your pediatrician and be willing to put a feed back for a while.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Talk **before** you start if your baby is under 6 months, was premature, is breastfed and you are unsure about supply, or you simply want a green light. Talk **during** the process if weight checks slip, diapers change, or your baby seems unusually lethargic or thirsty. If you have symptoms of mastitis or blocked ducts after dropping night feeds, that is a reason to seek care too.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can I night-wean a breastfed baby?**  
There is no single age. Many breastfed babies still need night calories after the 2–4 month window that applies more often to formula. Nine to 12 months is when Sleep Foundation says most infants can go longer without a night feed, and some families wait longer. Ask your pediatrician.

**When can I night-wean a formula-fed baby?**  
The HealthyChildren.org clinician answer points to 2–4 months or about 12 pounds for *no longer needing* a middle-of-the-night formula feed, if the baby is growing well. "No longer needing" is not a command to stop on that day.

**Does night weaning mean sleep training?**  
No. You can drop a feed and still respond at once. You can also combine a gradual cut-down with a settling method you have already chosen. They are separate decisions.

**Will starting solids make night feeds stop?**  
Not reliably. HealthyChildren.org notes that solids do not increase sleeping through the night for breastfed infants. Solids add daytime nutrition from around 6 months; they are not a night-weaning tool on their own.

**Can I night-wean during a regression or an illness?**  
It is usually a poor time. Wait for a calmer week.

**Can a 6 month old (or 4, 8, 12 month old) sleep through the night without feeding?**
“Through the night” at 1–3 months often means about **5–6 hours** (KidsHealth), not a 12-hour stretch. Whether a later baby can go longer without a night feed is a weight-gain and feeding question for your pediatrician, not a search-result rule. Use this page, [night weaning](/night-weaning), and [should you wake a sleeping baby](/should-you-wake-a-sleeping-baby). Do not drop a needed night feed because a blog said “6 months.”
