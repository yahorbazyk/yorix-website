# When Do Toddlers Stop Napping?

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Giving up the last daytime nap is a preschool project, not a first-birthday one. Our [nap schedule](/baby-nap-schedule-by-age) and [2-to-1 transition](/2-to-1-nap-transition) pages cover the earlier drops. The [2-year sleep regression](/sleep-regression/2-years) covers a nap *strike* during a bump, which is not the same as outgrowing naps. This page is about **when the afternoon nap ends for good**.

## Short answer

The AASM (AAP-endorsed) range for ages 3 to 5 is **10 to 13 hours in 24 hours, which may include a nap**. When that nap disappears, those hours should move into the night — Cleveland Clinic and Sleep Foundation both say to **offer an earlier bedtime**, not to simply subtract sleep. Timing varies. Mayo Clinic: until about age 3, most children still sleep 1 to 2 hours in the afternoon; **by age 5, most have stopped**. Sleep Foundation, citing Weissbluth's 1995 study, gives a slower curve: **almost all 3-year-olds still nap**, **60% of 4-year-olds**, **fewer than 30% of 5-year-olds**, fewer than 10% at 6, and nearly all have stopped by 7. Those two pictures agree that age 5 is a common finish line; they disagree about how unusual a non-napping 3-year-old is. We show both. KidsHealth: you cannot force a child to sleep; use **quiet time** if they will not nap.

## Signs they may be ready

Sleep Foundation's list, in our own words:

- They consistently **cannot fall asleep** at nap time, but are in an even mood on days without a nap.
- The nap is making **bedtime later** or mornings earlier, and shortening the nap has not fixed it.
- On days the nap is skipped, they are **not** falling apart in the evening.
- They no longer fall asleep at all during the usual rest, and they are not fighting sleepiness — they are simply done.

**Signs they are not ready:** evenings get much worse, they are struggling to stay awake by day even after a decent night, or the "nap refusal" is really a one-week blip after [travel](/baby-sleep-and-travel), illness, or a schedule change. In that case, keep the nap and read [baby won't nap](/baby-wont-nap).

Cleveland Clinic describes the drop as often **gradual**: naps on some days and not others, or a few days of napping then a few without. That mixed pattern is expected.

## How to make the change

There is no official week-by-week medical protocol. A sourced, practical approach:

1. **Do not quit overnight** if you can help it. Sleep Foundation suggests reducing how often or how long the nap happens, rather than ending it on a Monday and hoping.
2. **Replace nap time with quiet time** (Sleep Foundation; KidsHealth). Same place, same stretch of the afternoon, with books, puzzles, or coloring — not a car ride or a show that just makes them drowsy at the wrong hour. Some days they will still fall asleep. That is fine.
3. **Move bedtime earlier** so the 10–13 hour total still happens at night. Sleep Foundation notes that, because preschoolers may need up to 13 hours, bedtime may need to be quite early depending on the morning wake. Use your child's actual wake time; we are not setting a universal 6 p.m. rule.
4. **Keep mornings consistent.** A drifting wake time makes it harder to see whether the nap is still needed.
5. **Allow occasional naps** after growth spurts, late nights, or busy days (Sleep Foundation). One nap on a weekend is not a failure of the plan.

If preschool still has a rest period, you may not control it. Protect bedtime at home anyway.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if your child is still taking a long daily nap at 6 or 7, if nights are very short but they "can't" drop the nap, or if you worry about snoring, pauses, or unusual daytime sleepiness.

## Frequently asked questions

**When do toddlers stop napping?**  
Often between about 3 and 5 years, with most children finished by 5 (Mayo Clinic) and a slower curve in the Weissbluth figures Sleep Foundation cites. The birthday is not a switch.

**Should I drop the nap if bedtime is a battle at 2?**  
Usually not. First shorten or cap the nap. Two-year-olds typically still need it. See the [2-year sleep schedule](/2-year-old-sleep-schedule).

**What if they nap at daycare but not at home?**  
Common. Keep a consistent home bedtime and a quiet afternoon when you are in charge.

**Will they sleep less overall?**  
They should not have to. Move the hours to night.

**What is “quiet time” if they will not nap?**  
It is the same afternoon slot, used for rest rather than sleep: books, puzzles, or a calm room, not a new show or a late car nap (Sleep Foundation; KidsHealth; already in “How to make the change” above). Some days they will still fall asleep; that is all right. The preschool timetable, including quiet time on no-nap days, also lives on [4 year old sleep schedule](/4-year-old-sleep-schedule).

**My 18 month old or 2 year old won't nap. Is it time to drop it?**
Not automatically. Many children still need a nap at 18 months, and plenty still need one at two. Use the readiness signs already on this page, plus the month view: [18 month old sleep schedule](/18-month-old-sleep-schedule) or [2 year old sleep schedule](/2-year-old-sleep-schedule). If daycare has already dropped the nap and home days have fallen apart, use [daycare and baby sleep](/daycare-and-baby-sleep).
