# Sleep Training Methods Explained: A Side-by-Side Comparison

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"Sleep training" covers a family of approaches, not one technique. They differ in how much crying is involved, how close the parent stays, and how quickly families tend to see change. This guide lays them out together, so you can choose the one that fits your baby's temperament and your own limits.

## Short answer

Sleep training means helping a baby learn to fall asleep and resettle without being fed, rocked, or held all the way to sleep. Sleep Foundation says most babies are **not ready before 4–6 months**, and many families start around 6 months. In a review of 52 studies for the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, behavioral approaches were reported effective in **94% of studies**, with over 80% of children improving. Follow-up trials found **no evidence of harm** to emotions, behavior, stress, or attachment. No single method is "best": the right one is the approach you can carry out consistently.

## Is your baby ready?

Sleep Foundation suggests looking for these signs:

- Your baby is **at least 4–6 months old** (before this, the circadian rhythm that supports longer sleep is still forming).
- Your baby **sometimes falls asleep on their own** at bedtime, even if they still wake later.
- Your pediatrician has no concerns about **growth, feeding, or health**.

Sleep Foundation notes that some families wait until **9 months**, when most babies no longer need to feed at night. Ask your pediatrician whether your baby still needs a night feed before you begin, and build any feeds into the plan. For age-by-age readiness and self-soothing, see our full guide on [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training).

## Sleep training methods at a glance

| Method | Also called | Parent's role | Crying | How fast | Research support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extinction | Cry it out (CIO), full extinction | Leaves after bedtime and doesn't return until morning (except for safety or illness) | Most, for a short time | Often fastest | Strong support (AASM review) |
| Graduated extinction | Ferber method, interval method, progressive waiting | Brief check-ins at lengthening intervals | Moderate | A few nights to a week | Supported (AASM review; 2016 and 2012 trials) |
| Check and console | Variation of Ferber | Checks in before crying builds, then stretches the gaps to about 15 minutes | Less | Up to a week | Variant of graduated extinction |
| Chair method / fading | Camping out | Sits by the crib, moves a little farther away each night | Some, with the parent in sight | Slower | Limited direct trials |
| Pick up / put down | — | Picks up to calm, puts down drowsy, repeats | Less | Slow, demanding for parents | Limited direct trials |
| Bedtime fading | — | Temporarily moves bedtime later, then 15 minutes earlier every few days | Minimal if done correctly | Gradual | Supported (AASM review; 2016 trial) |
| No-cry / gentle | Gentle sleep training | Consistent routine, quick responses, slow changes to sleep associations | Least | Slowest | Limited direct trials |

Sources: Sleep Foundation (sleep training and Ferber guides), Cleveland Clinic, KidsHealth New Zealand (bedtime fading), Mindell et al. 2006, Gradisar et al. 2016, Price et al. 2012. "Limited direct trials" means we found no trial of that method in the sources we checked, not that it fails to work.

## Each method in more detail

### Extinction (cry it out)

After a calm bedtime routine and a goodnight cuddle, the parent leaves and doesn't go back for crying. Sleep Foundation says supporters find it works quickly, while many parents find it very hard to listen to. In the AASM review, unmodified extinction had strong evidence behind it. Always go in if your baby may be sick, hurt, or unsafe. Our [cry it out guide](/cry-it-out-method) covers the age limits, newborns, the stress-hormone and attachment studies, and the "fuss it out" variation.

### Graduated extinction (Ferber)

This is the most familiar middle path. You leave the room and return for very short, calm check-ins, waiting a little longer each time and a little longer each night. Sleep Foundation recommends check-ins of under one to two minutes, without picking up or feeding. The full 7-night interval chart, plus what to do with night wakings, is in our dedicated [Ferber method guide](/ferber-method-baby-sleep).

### Check and console

A softer version of Ferber. In Sleep Foundation's description, the parent leaves and comes back after a minute or two to offer a few loving words or a light pat, before crying escalates. The gaps then grow to around 15 minutes. It can take up to a week and demands more of parents. Some babies get more upset with each visit, and if that happens, Sleep Foundation suggests another method may suit better.

### Chair method (fading, camping out)

The parent stays in the room, first standing or sitting beside the crib, then moving a little farther away each night until they are outside the door. Sleep Foundation notes that crying is still common, that some parents find it harder because they can see their baby, and that it usually takes longer than CIO or Ferber.

### Pick up / put down

Place your baby down drowsy but awake. If they cry, pick them up to calm them, then lay them down again before they fall asleep, and repeat as needed. Sleep Foundation says it suits babies of about 4–8 months best and requires a lot of patience.

### Bedtime fading

This method works on timing rather than on crying. KidsHealth New Zealand describes starting with a temporary bedtime close to when your child usually falls asleep, then, once they fall asleep quickly, moving bedtime 15 minutes earlier every few days. In the 2016 randomized trial by Gradisar and colleagues, bedtime fading improved sleep compared with a control group, as did graduated extinction.

### No-cry or gentle methods

These lean heavily on a consistent routine and on slowly changing how your baby falls asleep, such as feeding or rocking a little less each night. Sleep Foundation says they are less stressful but take longer, and that parents may lose more sleep along the way.

## What the research says about safety

Concerns about crying and attachment are common and reasonable. The studies we checked found:

- **AASM task force review (2006):** across 52 studies, behavioral treatments produced reliable, lasting improvements in bedtime problems and night waking.
- **Randomized trial (2016):** in 43 babies aged 6–16 months, neither graduated extinction nor bedtime fading caused adverse stress responses, and there were no effects on attachment, emotions, or behavior a year later.
- **Five-year follow-up (2012):** in 225 families, children whose parents learned sleep techniques at 8–10 months were no different at age 6 from other children on mental health, stress, closeness to parents, or maternal depression.

These findings apply to healthy, typically developing babies. Sleep Foundation adds that behavioral methods alone are not enough for children with breathing problems such as heavy snoring, or for some neurodevelopmental conditions.

## How to choose a method

| If this sounds like you | Consider |
| --- | --- |
| You want the quickest change and can tolerate crying | Extinction or Ferber |
| You need to see your baby is OK during crying | Ferber or check and console |
| You want to stay in the room | Chair method |
| Your baby fights bedtime but sleeps fine once asleep | Bedtime fading |
| You'd rather go slowly with as little crying as possible | Pick up / put down or a no-cry approach |

## Before you start: a short checklist

1. **Health first.** No illness, ear pain, or feeding concerns. Check with your pediatrician if unsure.
2. **A predictable routine.** Sleep Foundation calls the bedtime routine a key part of every method.
3. **A sensible schedule.** An overtired baby will find any method harder. Our [wake windows guide](/wake-windows-by-age) can help.
4. **Everyone agrees.** All caregivers use the same responses.
5. **The right week.** Avoid travel, a new childcare setting, or a nap transition.
6. **Safe sleep basics.** Back to sleep, a bare crib, and room-sharing without bed-sharing (HealthyChildren.org / AAP).

## What can make sleep training harder

- **Inconsistency,** such as rocking to sleep on some nights and not others.
- **Starting too young,** before about 4–6 months.
- **Switching methods every few nights** before any one has had a fair trial.
- **Ignoring signs of illness or pain** during crying.

Sleep Foundation also reminds parents that rough nights happen, especially with travel, illness, or teething, and that keeping a sleep diary can help you stay on track.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Talk to your pediatrician before starting if your baby was premature, has a medical condition, snores loudly, or has feeding or weight concerns. Also check in if a method isn't helping after a fair trial, or if you are feeling low, hopeless, or unable to cope.

## Frequently asked questions

**What age can you start sleep training?**  
Sleep Foundation says most babies aren't ready before 4–6 months.

**Which sleep training method is best?**  
None is best for everyone. Extinction and graduated extinction have the strongest research base; gentler methods take longer but involve less crying.

**Does sleep training harm attachment?**  
Studies we checked found no harm to attachment, stress, emotions, or behavior, including at a five-year follow-up.

**How long does sleep training take?**  
It depends on the method. Ferber often works within a week (Sleep Foundation); chair, pick up/put down, and no-cry methods usually take longer.

**Do you have to sleep train?**  
No. Sleep Foundation notes that some babies learn to sleep through without formal training.

**Can I sleep train for naps too?**  
Many families start with bedtime and nights first, then apply the same responses to naps once nights improve.
