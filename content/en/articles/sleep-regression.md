# Sleep Regression Ages: A Stage-by-Stage Guide From Birth to Age 2

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"Sleep regression" is not a medical diagnosis. It is a parenting term for a stretch of worse sleep that follows a better one. Online charts often list neat ages (4, 6, 8, 12, 18 months, 2 years), as if every baby passes through each one on schedule. The research is less tidy than that. This page is the overview: what each commonly named age is about, how strong the evidence is, and which page to read next.

## Short answer

The most commonly named **sleep regression ages** are around **3–4 months, 6 months, 8–10 months, 12 months, 18 months, and 2 years**. Only some have reasonable evidence behind them. Cleveland Clinic notes that research hasn't shown regressions arriving like clockwork at a set age for every baby, and Sleep Foundation describes them as phases that *can* happen during development rather than events every child is destined to have. Studies do link disrupted sleep to **new motor and language skills**, such as crawling, pulling to stand, walking, and first words, which helps explain why many babies have rough patches at roughly similar ages. Most episodes last **a week or two to a few weeks** (Cleveland Clinic; Sleep Foundation).

## What "sleep regression" means

Sleep Foundation describes a sleep regression as a pause or a step backward in a baby's progress toward consistent sleep. Cleveland Clinic calls it a common phase in which a child slides backward after a period of steadier sleep. In practice, parents usually notice some mix of:

- more night waking, or wakings that are harder to settle;
- a baby who fights sleep at bedtime or naps;
- shorter or skipped naps;
- more fussiness during the day.

None of these is specific. The same signs appear with illness, teething, hunger, a schedule that no longer fits, or a change at home. Cleveland Clinic advises not to assume that every bad patch is a regression. One nursing researcher's webinar slides, published by Public Health Ontario, go further and list "sleep regression" as a popularized term that does not exist in the scientific literature.

## What the research actually shows

The evidence for **age-specific** regressions is thin. The evidence that **development and sleep interact** is stronger:

| Study | Who was studied | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scher & Cohen, 2015 | 28 infants followed from 5 to 11 months with actigraphy | Alongside overall sleep improvement, periods of more long night wakings were linked in time to the onset of crawling |
| Atun-Einy & Scher, 2016 | 20 infants followed from 7 to 11–12 months | In babies who pulled to stand early (by 8 months), the milestone coincided with a period of disrupted sleep |
| Waugh & Berger, 2026 | Two cohorts tracked with an app and baby monitor | Sleep disruptions occurred around first crawling, first walking steps, walking mastery, and several language milestones |
| Mindell et al., 2016 | 841 children aged 0–36 months, app data | Sleep patterns changed constantly in the first three years, with wide individual variability |
| Pennestri et al., 2018 | 388 infants at 6 and 12 months | 27.9% to 57.0% did not sleep through the night (6- or 8-hour definitions), with no link to development or maternal mood |

Two practical conclusions follow. First, disrupted sleep tends to follow **your baby's developmental timetable**, not the calendar. An early crawler may have a "regression" at 7 months, a late one at 10. Second, many babies who wake at night are not regressing. They simply haven't consolidated their sleep yet, and that is within the normal range.

## Is there a newborn sleep regression?

We found no health source that describes a newborn sleep regression. Newborn sleep is irregular from the start. HealthyChildren.org (AAP) says newborns sleep about 16–17 hours a day but often only 1–2 hours at a time, and regular sleep cycles don't appear until about 4 months. Sleep Foundation notes that schedules tend to become more predictable from around 2 months.

What parents sometimes call a "newborn regression" is usually one of these:

- **Day and night still mixed up.** The NHS suggests teaching the difference from the start with light, play, and everyday noise by day, and dim, quiet feeds at night.
- **Hunger.** Breastfed newborns usually feed about every 2 hours (HealthyChildren.org), so a "bad night" may simply be a hungry one.
- **Overtiredness.** KidsHealth notes that overtired infants often have more trouble sleeping at night.

If a newborn is suddenly sleepier than usual, harder to wake, or feeding poorly, that is not a regression. Call your pediatrician (HealthyChildren.org).

## How long does a sleep regression last?

- **Cleveland Clinic:** typically a week or two.
- **Sleep Foundation:** 12- and 18-month regressions rarely last more than a few weeks, and 6-month problems often resolve within days or weeks.

Patterns that **outlast a few weeks** usually have another cause, such as a habit that formed during the rough patch (for example, being rocked to sleep at every waking), a schedule that needs adjusting, or a health issue. Then it is worth looking at routines, and talking to your pediatrician if you are unsure.

## What helps at any age

The specific pages cover age-appropriate details. The principles are the same across all of them:

1. **Rule out the basics:** illness, ear pain, teething discomfort, hunger, a room that is too hot or cold.
2. **Keep the routine steady.** Sleep Foundation's regression pages all stress a consistent bedtime routine and schedule.
3. **Make room for new skills in the day.** Lots of floor time to practice rolling, crawling, standing, or walking.
4. **Check the timing.** A nap that ends too late, or awake periods that no longer match your baby's age, can look like a regression. See [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age) and our [nap schedule by age](/baby-nap-schedule-by-age).
5. **Respond calmly and consistently** to night wakings, without introducing new habits you don't want to keep.
6. **Look after yourself.** Our guide to [night wakings](/survive-night-wakings-new-parents) covers sharing nights and getting help.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Contact your pediatrician if poor sleep comes with fever or other signs of illness, pain, breathing pauses or loud snoring, poor feeding, or fewer wet diapers. Also call if sleep problems last more than a few weeks or leave you struggling to cope.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the sleep regression ages?**  
The ages most often named are about 3–4, 6, 8–10, 12, and 18 months, plus 2 years. Evidence is strongest for the changes around 3–4 months and around crawling and standing (often 8–10 months).

**Are sleep regressions real?**  
Bad patches are real and common. The idea that every baby has one at fixed ages is not supported: Cleveland Clinic says research hasn't shown them happening like clockwork for every baby.

**Can a newborn have a sleep regression?**  
We found no source describing one. Newborn sleep is irregular by nature.

**How long do sleep regressions last?**  
Usually one to a few weeks (Cleveland Clinic; Sleep Foundation).

**Do all babies go through sleep regressions?**  
No. Sleep Foundation notes that many babies don't have a 6-, 8-, 12-, or 18-month regression, and some sleep better at those ages.
