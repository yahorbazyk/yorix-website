# Baby Feeding Schedule by Age: How Often and How Much, Birth to 12 Months

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Feeding is the part of baby care that most often brings parents to a search bar: is my baby eating enough, too often, too much? The honest answer from pediatric sources is that babies vary, and that following your baby's hunger and fullness signals matters more than any chart. Still, typical ranges help you know what to expect. Here they are, by age, with the sources for every number.

## Short answer

Most healthy, full-term babies are **fed on demand** rather than by the clock. HealthyChildren.org (AAP) says breastfed newborns usually nurse **about every 2 hours, 10–12 times in 24 hours**, while formula-fed newborns eat **every 2–3 hours, at least 8 times a day**, taking about **1–2 ounces** per feed after the first days. By the end of the first month, many formula-fed babies take **3–4 ounces every 3–4 hours**. The AAP recommends **breast milk only for about the first 6 months**, then adding solid foods while continuing breast milk or formula. Your pediatrician's growth checks are the real measure of whether feeding is going well.

### Key numbers at a glance

| Question | Answer | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breastfeeds per day, newborn | About 10–12 (roughly every 2 hours) | HealthyChildren.org (AAP) |
| Formula feeds per day, newborn | At least 8 (every 2–3 hours) | HealthyChildren.org (AAP) |
| Formula per feed, end of month 1 | 3–4 oz (90–120 ml), every 3–4 hours | HealthyChildren.org (AAP) |
| Formula per day, maximum | About 32 oz (960 ml) | HealthyChildren.org (AAP) |
| Wet diapers after day 4–5 | At least 5–6 a day | HealthyChildren.org (AAP) |
| Start of solid foods | About 6 months | AAP; Cleveland Clinic |

## Baby feeding chart by age

These are typical ranges, not targets. Every baby's needs are different.

| Age | Breastfed | Formula-fed | Solid foods |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First days | Colostrum, about a teaspoon per feed; may want to feed about every hour at first (NHS) | About ½ oz per feed in the first day or two (HealthyChildren.org) | None |
| Weeks 1–4 | About every 2 hours, 10–12 feeds a day (HealthyChildren.org); 8–12 feeds (Cleveland Clinic) | Every 2–3 hours, at least 8 feeds, about 1–2 oz each (HealthyChildren.org); 6–10 feeds (Cleveland Clinic) | None |
| End of month 1 | Feeds slowly space out | 3–4 oz about every 3–4 hours (HealthyChildren.org) | None |
| 1–6 months | On demand; total daily intake stays similar from 4 weeks to 6 months (Cleveland Clinic) | 3–4 oz per feed, about 24–30 oz a day (Cleveland Clinic); no more than about 32 oz a day (HealthyChildren.org) | None. Exclusive breast milk for about 6 months (AAP) |
| About 6 months | Continue | 6–8 oz at each of 4–5 feeds (HealthyChildren.org); Cleveland Clinic lists 3–4 oz per feed, totals vary | Start solids when ready |
| 6–8 months | Continue | Continue | 2–3 meals a day (Cleveland Clinic) |
| 9–12 months | Continue | Continue | 3–4 meals plus 1–2 snacks a day (Cleveland Clinic) |

**Note on differences:** HealthyChildren.org and Cleveland Clinic give different per-feed amounts at about 6 months. Both say babies regulate their own intake. Ask your pediatrician what fits your baby's growth.

HealthyChildren.org also offers a rough guide for formula of about **2½ ounces per pound of body weight per day**, within the 32-ounce upper limit.

## Newborn feeding: the first weeks

- **Feed early and often.** The NHS explains that frequent breastfeeding in the first days stimulates milk supply, and feeds become fewer but longer once mature milk comes in.
- **Don't let a newborn go too long.** HealthyChildren.org advises waking a formula-fed newborn who sleeps longer than 4–5 hours in the first weeks and starts missing feeds. KidsHealth says newborns should be woken to feed every 3–4 hours until they are gaining weight well.
- **Watch the diapers.** HealthyChildren.org says 2–3 wet diapers a day in the first few days, then **at least 5–6 a day** after day 4–5, suggests your baby is getting enough.

For how feeding and sleep fit together in these weeks, see our [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule).

## Breastfed vs formula-fed: why schedules differ

Breast milk digests quickly, so breastfed babies usually feed more often, in smaller amounts. HealthyChildren.org notes that formula-fed babies often settle into a more regular pattern, such as every 3–4 hours. Neither pattern is better; they are just different.

For bottle feeding, HealthyChildren.org suggests:

- **Start on demand** and let a routine emerge from your baby's own timetable.
- **Let your baby decide when they're done.** If they get fidgety or distracted, they're probably full. If they finish the bottle and keep smacking their lips, they may want a little more.
- **Talk to your pediatrician** if your baby consistently seems to want much more or much less than the usual range.

## Hunger and fullness cues

| Early hunger cues | Fullness cues |
| --- | --- |
| Licking lips, sticking tongue out | Slowing down or stopping sucking |
| Rooting (turning toward the breast) | Getting fidgety or easily distracted |
| Hands to mouth repeatedly | Relaxed hands and body |
| Opening mouth, fussing | Turning away from the breast or bottle |

Source: HealthyChildren.org (hunger cues; bottle fullness signs). HealthyChildren.org stresses that **crying is a late hunger sign**, and that babies also suck for comfort, so not every cry or suck means hunger. It also notes that some babies keep sucking even when full, so pausing during a feed can help them notice.

## Night feeds and feeding schedules

Night feeds are normal in the early months. HealthyChildren.org says that between **2 and 4 months**, or when they weigh more than about 12 pounds, most formula-fed babies no longer need a middle-of-the-night feed, although this varies widely. Breastfed babies often feed at night for longer.

## Starting solid foods

The AAP recommends breast milk alone for about the first 6 months, then continuing breastfeeding alongside foods for as long as you and your child wish, for 2 years or beyond. Cleveland Clinic also places the start at around 6 months.

**Signs of readiness** (HealthyChildren.org; Cleveland Clinic):

- Good head control and able to sit in a high chair or feeding seat.
- Watching you eat, reaching for food, opening their mouth for a spoon.
- Moving food from a spoon to the back of the mouth rather than pushing it out.

**Key points:**

- **Don't add cereal to a bottle.** Cleveland Clinic advises spoon-feeding instead.
- **Common allergens early.** HealthyChildren.org says there is no evidence that waiting to introduce foods like eggs, dairy, soy, peanut products, or fish beyond 4–6 months prevents allergy. Cleveland Clinic suggests introducing them at about six months.
- **No juice before 12 months** (HealthyChildren.org).
- **Water:** healthy babies don't need extra water before solids. Once solids start, a little water from a cup is fine, up to about 1 cup (8 oz) a day (HealthyChildren.org).

## What can make feeding harder

- **Watching the clock instead of your baby,** especially in the newborn weeks.
- **Pushing a baby to finish every bottle,** which can override their fullness signals.
- **Feeding to sleep every time,** if you later want your baby to settle without a feed. This is a sleep habit, not a feeding problem.
- **Starting solids before your baby is ready.**

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Contact your pediatrician promptly if your baby (HealthyChildren.org symptom checker):

- isn't feeding well, can't finish feeds, or has to be woken for every feed (HealthyChildren.org calls feeding "the one reliable measure" of a newborn's wellbeing);
- has fewer wet diapers than the ranges above;
- is sleeping much more than normal or is hard to wake;
- vomits green (bile-colored) fluid;
- has a fever, especially if under 12 weeks old;
- isn't gaining weight, or you are worried about how much they eat.

Breastfeeding problems such as pain or poor latch are common and fixable. Ask your midwife, health visitor, or a lactation consultant for help.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should a newborn eat?**  
Breastfed newborns usually feed about every 2 hours (10–12 times a day). Formula-fed newborns feed every 2–3 hours, at least 8 times a day (HealthyChildren.org).

**How much formula should a 2 month old drink?**  
Many babies take 3–4 oz per feed from the end of the first month, about every 3–4 hours (HealthyChildren.org). Your baby may take more or less.

**When can I stop night feeds?**  
HealthyChildren.org says most formula-fed babies no longer need a middle-of-the-night feed between 2 and 4 months, but this varies. Check with your pediatrician.

**When should my baby start solids?**  
At about 6 months, when they show signs of readiness (AAP; Cleveland Clinic).

**Is it OK to feed on demand instead of a schedule?**  
Yes. For most healthy, full-term babies, HealthyChildren.org recommends following your baby's hunger cues.
