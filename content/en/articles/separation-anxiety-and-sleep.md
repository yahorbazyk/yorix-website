# Separation Anxiety and Sleep: A Longer Arc Than One Regression

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Separation anxiety is a normal part of emotional development, and it often shows up at bedtime and in the middle of the night. This page is about **that longer arc** — from late in the first year into the toddler years — and what a consistent, loving response can look like. It is not a rewrite of [8-month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/8-months). That article owns a time-limited bump and the skills that cluster around it (sitting, crawling, standing, new awareness). Separation protest can be one ingredient there; it is not the whole story, and it does not end on a birthday.

## Short answer

HealthyChildren says separation anxiety can disrupt sleep **beginning in the second half of the first year**, may last for several months, and **usually fades around the second birthday**. In that stage a child may wake several times and cry anxiously for a parent, often with a strong preference for one person. Until it eases, the child may need reassurance **several times, night after night**.

KidsHealth places a related wave of not wanting a parent to leave — at daycare or at bedtime — at about **10 months to 2 years**. The exact week varies. The theme is the same: the child now understands that you can go, and has not yet built a solid feeling that you will return.

This is not a trick or a spoiling problem. It is also not automatically a reason to start a formal sleep-training method the same week. If you do later choose a plan, use [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training) and, if you want a stay-close approach, [gentle sleep training](/gentle-sleep-training). The response below can stand on its own.

## What it looks like at night

The picture HealthyChildren describes is specific: repeated anxious waking, crying for one or both parents, sometimes with a clear favorite. By day you may also see clinging at handovers, or tears when you leave the room — even to go to the bathroom. KidsHealth's advice for everyday separations is to keep goodbyes short and honest, and to let the child learn that you come back.

At bedtime the same idea applies. A long, uncertain goodbye in a dark room often makes the feeling bigger. A predictable [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby), a clear "I'll see you in the morning," and the same person doing the last step when you can, tend to travel better than a new speech every night.

A comfort object can help once it is safe — after 12 months, and not in an infant's sleep space. See [when can a baby sleep with a blanket](/when-can-baby-sleep-with-a-blanket) for the timing. A dim light for you and the child is fine if it helps; this is not a color-of-light article.

## A consistent way to respond

HealthyChildren's wider advice on this stage is simple and firm in tone: tell the child when you are leaving, even for a minute ("I'll be right back"); when you go out, use a familiar sitter if you can, and have a new sitter arrive before bedtime so they are not a stranger in a dark room; keep goodbyes brief. That daytime practice supports nights. Peekaboo and other short hide-and-return games, when the child is in the mood, give the same lesson in play.

For the wakings themselves:

- Go in, be present, keep the lights low, and keep the visit boring and kind. The goal is reassurance, not a second evening of play.
- If one parent is the preferred person, you do not have to make the other parent disappear; you may need a stretch of nights where the preferred parent does the checks, then gradually share them, rather than turning 2 a.m. into a new conflict.
- Avoid creating a brand-new habit you do not want to keep every night — for example, a long feed that is no longer needed, or relocating to the sofa — unless you are making that choice on purpose. Comfort is allowed. A complete change of sleep place has its own safety rules if the child is still under 1 year: [bed-sharing](/bed-sharing-and-co-sleeping).
- Try not to sneak out at bedtime. KidsHealth's "say you'll be back, hug, and go" pattern is clearer than disappearing.

If nights are also a regression plus teething plus a new skill, treat the causes in parallel. Do not stack a new formal method, a room change, and dropping a feed in the same week.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if the anxiety seems extreme for the child's age, is getting worse after the second birthday instead of easing, comes with poor daytime function, or if you are not sure whether a night feed is still needed. Seek urgent care if breathing, color, or unresponsiveness worries you — those are not separation symptoms.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is night waking at 8 or 9 months always separation anxiety?**  
No. It can be part of it, or it can be practice of new skills, a schedule change, or something medical. The [8-month regression](/sleep-regression/8-months) page is the place for that cluster.

**Will this last until my child is 2?**  
HealthyChildren says the stage usually fades around the second birthday, and that it can last several months. Some children have a shorter wave; some have more than one.

**Should I ignore the crying so they learn I am still there?**  
HealthyChildren's own framing is reassurance, repeated if needed, in a consistent way — not disappearance. A formal method is a separate family decision, not a requirement of this stage.

**Why does my child only want one parent at night?**  
That preference is common in this phase (HealthyChildren). It is inconvenient, not a verdict on the other parent.
