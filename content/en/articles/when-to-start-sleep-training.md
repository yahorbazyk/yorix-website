# When to Start Sleep Training: Age, Readiness Signs, and Self-Soothing

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"When should we start?" is often the first sleep training question, and it gets very different answers: 3 months, 4 months, 6 months, "whenever you're ready," or "never." The spread isn't just opinion. It reflects how quickly babies' sleep changes in the first year, and how little research there is on the youngest ages. This guide collects what pediatricians and studies say about the right age, the signs that a baby is ready, and when babies can actually self-soothe. It doesn't cover how each method works; for that, see [sleep training methods explained](/sleep-training-methods-explained).

## Short answer

Most pediatric sources put the **earliest start at about 4–6 months**, with **around 6 months** the most common choice. Cleveland Clinic says many babies are ready at about 4 months, Sleep Foundation says babies aren't ready before 4–6 months, and the Child Mind Institute calls about 6 months typical. **Newborns shouldn't be sleep trained.** Before you start, ask your pediatrician whether your baby is growing well and still needs night feeds. There is **no proven deadline**: older babies and toddlers can learn too, so start when your baby is ready *and* your family can be consistent.

The pattern is clear: nobody we found recommends sleep training before about 3–4 months, and most land between 4 and 6 months.

## Signs your baby may be ready

You don't need every sign, but most of them should apply:

- **Age:** at least 4 months (adjusted age if your baby was born early), ideally closer to 6.
- **Healthy growth:** your pediatrician is happy with weight gain and feeding.
- **Night feeds discussed:** you know which feeds, if any, your baby still needs.
- **Some independent sleep:** Sleep Foundation notes that a baby who sometimes falls asleep on their own at night is likely ready.
- **Longer night stretches already appearing:** a sign the body clock is maturing.
- **A predictable routine:** a regular bedtime and similar steps each night.
- **You're ready:** all caregivers agree on a plan and can stick with it for one to two weeks.

## Signs to wait a little longer

- Your baby is unwell, teething hard, or recently vaccinated and uncomfortable.
- You're about to travel, move house, change childcare, or return to work.
- Your baby has reflux, feeding difficulties, or slow weight gain.
- Your baby snores loudly or seems to struggle to breathe during sleep. Sleep Foundation's Ferber guide notes that behavioral methods alone aren't enough when excessive snoring may point to a breathing disorder such as sleep apnea.
- You're not sure you can stay consistent right now. A half-done plan can make crying last longer.

## When can babies self-soothe?

Self-soothing is a baby's growing ability to calm down and settle without immediate help. It develops **after** babies have had lots of experience being soothed by a caregiver.

- **First months:** Huckleberry (app example) says some babies show early signs, such as bringing hands to the mouth or sucking a pacifier, but not consistently.
- **3–6 months:** Texas Children's says babies aren't cognitively ready to self-soothe until 3 to 6 months; from then, families can give them a chance to settle when they wake.
- **Around 6 months:** Huckleberry (app example) describes most babies as developmentally capable of falling and staying asleep more independently, as long as hunger and other needs are met.

Two clarifications help:

1. **Self-soothing isn't the same as sleeping through the night.** Dr. Schwartz at Cleveland Clinic points out that a sleep-trained baby has learned to settle but may still wake; a baby who sleeps through without training hasn't necessarily learned to settle.
2. **Waking is normal.** HealthyChildren.org (AAP) describes a good sleeper at this age as a baby who wakes often but gets back to sleep, not one who never wakes.

Comfort objects should wait: the AAP advises keeping soft toys and loose items out of the crib for the first year, as Huckleberry (app example) also notes.

## Sleep training by age: what to expect

### Before 4 months: build foundations instead

Babies' self-regulated sleep changes fastest in this period. In a study of 75 infants, Henderson and colleagues found the biggest increase in self-regulated night sleep between **1 and 4 months**. Many babies lengthen their night stretch on their own. Instead of training, focus on:

- dark, quiet nights and bright, social days;
- a short bedtime routine;
- sometimes putting your baby down drowsy but awake;
- responding to crying and feeding on demand.

A 2013 review by Douglas and Hill found that behavioral sleep programs in the first 6 months had not been shown to reduce crying, prevent later sleep problems, or protect against postnatal depression, and flagged risks such as earlier weaning from breastfeeding.

### 4–6 months: the earliest common window

Sleep becomes more adult-like around 4 months, which is also when many families meet the [4 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/4-months). Gentle approaches (bedtime fading, pick up/put down, chair method) are popular here. Check feeding plans with your pediatrician first, because many babies this age still feed at night.

### 6–12 months: the most studied window

The trials we checked enrolled babies from about 6 months up. A 2016 randomized trial included babies aged 6–16 months, and an Australian community trial (Hiscock et al., 2007) reached mothers of 7-month-olds with sleep problems; it reduced reported sleep problems and maternal depression symptoms at 10 and 12 months. Dartmouth Health suggests encouraging self-soothing a little earlier, between 4 and 6 months, before separation anxiety develops.

### After 12 months: still possible

Henderson's study found that at 12 months, 21 of 75 babies still weren't sleeping from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. Toddlers can learn new sleep habits too. For toddlers who resist bedtime, Huckleberry (app example) describes pairing a warm, consistent routine with a temporarily later bedtime, then moving it earlier. Macall Gordon, writing in Psychology Today in 2025, notes there's no proof that waiting makes sleep training harder later.

## Is there a "best" age?

No study has identified one best age. A reasonable way to decide:

| If... | Consider... |
| --- | --- |
| Baby is under 4 months | Foundations only; no formal training |
| Baby is 4–6 months, healthy, and your pediatrician agrees | A gentle method, or wait |
| Baby is 6 months or older, and sleep problems are affecting the family | Any method you can follow consistently |
| You're happy with how things are | No need to train at all |

The Child Mind Institute's expert, Wendy Nash, MD, suggests looking for trends over weeks rather than reacting to single bad nights. Short disruptions from growth spurts or teething can pass on their own; if they last more than a week or two, sleep training becomes a reasonable option.

## Frequently asked questions

**What age is best to start sleep training?**  
Most sources point to 4–6 months at the earliest, and around 6 months is the most common choice. There's no single best age.

**Can you sleep train at 3 months?**  
Formal training isn't usually recommended this early. Gentle habits (routine, putting down drowsy) are fine.

**What age do babies self-soothe?**  
Early signs appear in the first months, but self-soothing becomes more reliable between about 3 and 6 months, and most babies are more capable by around 6 months.

**Is it too late to sleep train at 1 year or older?**  
No. There's no evidence of a closing window; toddlers can learn new sleep habits.

**Do I have to sleep train at all?**  
No. Sleep training is optional. Many babies consolidate sleep with time.

**How do I know it's working?**  
Track settling time and night wakings for a week. If there's no progress after one to two weeks of consistency, pause and reassess.
