# 8 Month Sleep Regression: Why Sleep Gets Bumpy Between 8 and 10 Months

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

If your baby has just learned to crawl or haul themselves up on the furniture, and nights have suddenly fallen apart, you are in good company. The second half of the first year is a busy time for the brain and body, and sleep often takes a temporary hit. Parents call it the 8 month, 9 month, or 10 month sleep regression. They are usually describing the same window.

Looking for a normal day plan rather than help with a rough patch? Our [8 month old sleep schedule](/8-month-old-sleep-schedule) and [9 month old sleep schedule](/9-month-old-sleep-schedule) cover naps, wake windows, and bedtimes.

## Short answer

The so-called **8 month sleep regression** usually shows up somewhere between **8 and 10 months**. Of all the "regression ages", this one has some of the better evidence: small studies using sleep trackers found **disrupted sleep around the onset of crawling** and, in early achievers, **around pulling to stand**. Sleep Foundation adds **teething, separation anxiety, and overstimulation** as contributors, and notes that **not every baby goes through it**. Two NHS services describe night waking often returning at **8–9 months**. It is usually temporary, and calm consistency helps most.

## 8, 9, or 10 month regression: same thing?

Mostly, yes. Development doesn't follow birthdays. Newcastle Hospitals NHS says some babies begin waking more often again at 8–9 months, and Sleep Foundation's wake-windows page says disturbances may be more common around 9 months. A baby who crawls at 7 months may have a bumpy patch then; a baby who pulls to stand at 10 months may hit it later. Huckleberry (app example) frames the whole stretch as 7–10 months.

## What the research says

This is one of the few "regressions" with direct research behind it, although the studies are small:

- **Crawling.** Scher and Cohen followed 28 infants from 5 to 11 months with actigraphy. While sleep generally improved over that time, periods with more long night wakings lined up with the onset of crawling.
- **Pulling to stand.** Atun-Einy and Scher followed 20 infants from 7 to 11–12 months. Among babies who pulled to stand early (by 8 months), the milestone was time-linked to disrupted sleep.
- **Walking and language.** A 2026 study by Waugh and Berger found sleep disruptions around several locomotor and language milestones, and suggests skill learning can affect sleep even before the new skill shows.

These findings support the idea of a **developmental** sleep disruption, linked to skills rather than to an exact age.

## Common signs

Sleep Foundation describes:

- trouble falling asleep, and more fussing or agitation around bedtime;
- more night wakings;
- longer naps during the day with less sleep at night.

Parents also often notice a baby who pops up to sitting or standing as soon as they are laid down, or who cries the moment a parent leaves.

## Why it happens

| Factor | What it looks like | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New motor skills | Crawling, sitting up, pulling to stand in the crib | Sleep Foundation; Scher studies |
| Separation anxiety | Crying when a parent leaves; calling out at night | KidsHealth; Sleep Foundation |
| Teething | Gum discomfort that wakes the baby | Sleep Foundation |
| Overstimulation | A busy, exciting day that makes winding down hard | Sleep Foundation |
| Nap transition | Third nap disappearing; awake periods lengthening | Mayo Clinic; Huckleberry (app example) |

## How long does it last?

Sleep Foundation describes regressions at this age as normally short-lived, and Cleveland Clinic says regressions in general typically last a week or two. Because the trigger is often a new skill, things tend to settle as the skill becomes routine. If disrupted sleep goes on for weeks, check whether new habits have formed during the rough patch.

## What helps

### Give new skills plenty of daytime practice

Lots of floor time for crawling, standing, and cruising means the crib is less of a training ground. Sleep Foundation also recommends daytime activity with natural light to support the body clock.

### Keep the routine predictable

Sleep Foundation suggests a consistent schedule, including naps, and the same bedtime steps every night, with a well-fed baby and a calm wind-down without too much stimulation.

### Lay your baby down drowsy

Putting your baby in the crib while drowsy, rather than already asleep, helps them associate the crib with falling asleep (Sleep Foundation).

### Handle separation protests calmly

Sleep Foundation advises comforting your baby without lifting them out of the crib when they cry because you are leaving. KidsHealth suggests reassuring quietly, a soothing pat, and a quick exit. Long cuddles, feeding, or chatting at night may encourage more waking.

### Ease teething discomfort

For teething pain, Sleep Foundation suggests gently rubbing the gums with a clean finger or offering a teething toy before bed. Ask your pediatrician before using any pain relief.

### Watch the nap transition

Mayo Clinic says most babies drop the third nap at about 9 months. If late-afternoon naps are pushing bedtime later, or your baby fights the third nap, a two-nap day may help. Cleveland Clinic suggests 2.5–4.5 hours of awake time between sleeps at 7–10 months.

## What can make it worse

- **Starting new habits you don't want to keep,** such as feeding to sleep at every waking.
- **Dropping the third nap before your baby can manage longer awake periods.**
- **Stimulating play close to bedtime.**
- **Lifting your baby out at every protest,** which can turn bedtime into a game.

## Safe sleep at 8–10 months

- Remove anything reachable from sitting or standing, such as mobiles, cords, and wall hangings (KidsHealth).
- Keep the crib bare: no pillows, bumpers, or blankets in the first year (HealthyChildren.org / AAP).

## Standing up in the crib at night

Pulling to stand is a common reason an 8- to 10-month-old suddenly seems "worse" at night: they can get up and cannot always get down. This is a skill problem as much as a "regression." Practice sitting down during the day, keep the crib clear of cords and mobiles, put the mattress at the correct height, and use a short, boring response when you lay them back down. Step-by-step wording is on the [8 month old sleep schedule](/8-month-old-sleep-schedule) and the [10 month old sleep schedule](/10-month-old-sleep-schedule). Do not add loose bedding to soften the crib.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Sleep Foundation advises talking to your child's doctor if sleep problems continue. Call sooner if your baby has a fever, pulls at an ear and seems in pain, snores loudly or pauses in breathing, or is eating much less.

## Frequently asked questions

**When does the 8 month sleep regression start?**  
Usually somewhere between 8 and 10 months, often around a new skill such as crawling or pulling to stand.

**Is there a 9 month sleep regression?**  
It is usually the same developmental window. NHS services describe more waking at 8–9 months, and Sleep Foundation notes disturbances around 9 months.

**How long does the 8 month sleep regression last?**  
Typically one to a few weeks (Cleveland Clinic; Sleep Foundation).

**Should I drop to two naps during the 8 month regression?**  
Possibly. Mayo Clinic says the third nap usually goes around 9 months. Look for a fought or skipped late nap.

**Does every baby have an 8 month sleep regression?**  
No. Sleep Foundation says not all babies do.

**My 8 month old is waking every hour. Is that the regression?**
It can be part of the same stretch. The hourly-waking plan is on [baby waking every hour](/baby-waking-every-hour). This page covers the 8–10 month named wobble.
