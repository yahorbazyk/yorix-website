# When to Start Solids: Readiness Signs, First Foods, and a Simple Plan

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If your baby was born early, has a medical condition, has severe eczema, or has had a reaction to food, talk to your pediatrician before starting solids.*

"When can my baby eat food?" is one of the most searched baby questions, and the answer you hear depends on whom you ask: grandparents may say 3 months, an app might say 4, your pediatrician probably says 6. This guide explains what U.S. and international health bodies actually recommend, the signs that matter more than the date on the calendar, how to choose between infant cereal and purees, why rice shouldn't be the only cereal, and how the first few weeks can look. If you're interested in skipping purees and offering finger foods from the start, read our separate [baby-led weaning](/baby-led-weaning) guide.

## Short answer

Start solids at **about 6 months**, when your baby shows the **signs of readiness**: sitting up with little or no support and steady head control, interest in food, opening the mouth for a spoon, and swallowing rather than pushing food back out. The AAP, CDC, WHO, and NHS all point to around 6 months; **never before 4 months** (CDC, KidsHealth). First foods can be **iron-rich**: iron-fortified infant cereal (oat, barley, or multigrain, not only rice) or pureed meat, plus vegetables and fruits in any order. Start with a teaspoon or two once a day, offer one new single-ingredient food at a time, and include common allergens early. **No honey, cow's milk as a drink, or juice before 12 months**, and nothing that is a choking hazard. Breast milk or formula stays the main food for the first year.

## When can babies start eating food?

- **AAP:** breast milk only for about the first 6 months, then foods alongside continued breastfeeding.
- **CDC and the Dietary Guidelines for Americans:** foods other than breast milk or formula at about 6 months; not before 4 months.
- **WHO:** around 6 months, a baby's needs for energy and nutrients start to exceed what breast milk provides; starting much earlier or later may affect growth.
- **NHS (UK):** around 6 months, when three signs appear together.
- **Mayo Clinic and KidsHealth:** most babies are ready somewhere between 4 and 6 months; KidsHealth adds that at about 6 months babies need the extra iron and zinc that foods provide.

So "4–6 months" is the window in which readiness typically appears, and "about 6 months" is the official target. If you're thinking of starting before 6 months, talk to your pediatrician first. If your baby was born prematurely, ask about timing (NHS).

## Signs your baby is ready for solids

Look for these together, not just one of them (CDC, AAP, NHS):

1. **Sits up** alone or with a little support and **holds the head steady**.
2. **Shows interest:** watches you eat, reaches for food, opens the mouth when food comes close.
3. **Swallows** food instead of pushing it back out onto the chin. The tongue-thrust reflex fades between about 4 and 6 months (KidsHealth).
4. **Hand-to-mouth coordination:** picks things up and brings them to the mouth (CDC, NHS).

The AAP also mentions size: babies who have roughly doubled their birth weight and weigh about 13 pounds or more may be ready.

**Not reliable signs** (NHS): chewing fists, waking more at night, or wanting extra milk feeds. These are normal at this age. The NHS states plainly that starting solids won't make a baby more likely to sleep through the night.

## Purees or baby-led weaning?

Both are acceptable ways to start. With **spoon-feeding**, you offer smooth purees and mashed foods and move to lumpier textures and finger foods over the following months. With **baby-led weaning (BLW)**, your baby feeds themselves soft finger foods from the start. Many families mix the two. This page covers timing and first foods for any approach; for BLW-specific safety and food shapes, see our [baby-led weaning guide](/baby-led-weaning).

Whatever you choose, the NHS and CDC both encourage moving on from smooth textures **as soon as your baby can manage**, because practice with lumps and soft pieces helps chewing.

## First foods: infant cereal, purees, and meat

The AAP says your baby's first foods are **your choice**, and CDC notes most children don't need foods in a set order. What matters most:

- **Iron and zinc.** Include iron-fortified infant cereal or pureed meat early (AAP). CDC explains that breastfed babies especially need iron from foods from about 6 months; iron-fortified formula covers needs during the first year.
- **Single ingredients at first,** so you can spot a reaction (AAP, CDC).
- **Vegetables or fruit first?** The AAP says there's no evidence that offering fruit first makes babies dislike vegetables.
- **No salt or sugar added** (NHS, KidsHealth), and limit adult processed foods (AAP).

### Infant cereal: when and how

- **When:** once your baby shows readiness signs, usually around 6 months. There's no need to start cereal early or to make it the first food.
- **How:** mix iron-fortified infant cereal with breast milk, formula, or water; start runny and thicken it over time (AAP, Mayo Clinic, KidsHealth).
- **Never in a bottle.** Cereal in a bottle can cause choking and overfeeding (AAP, KidsHealth). The only exception the AAP mentions is a doctor's advice for reflux.

### Rice cereal and arsenic

Rice can contain arsenic (Mayo Clinic). Since 2020 the FDA has set a **100 ppb action level** for inorganic arsenic in infant rice cereal; in its 2018 testing, 76% of samples met that level. CDC recommends a **variety** of fortified cereals (oat, barley, multigrain) instead of only rice, and Mayo Clinic adds that rice milk and brown rice syrup should be avoided for babies. Rice cereal isn't banned, but it shouldn't be the only grain.

### Purees for a 6 month old: ideas

Smooth, single-ingredient purees that are easy to start with (examples drawn from AAP, CDC, and KidsHealth lists): pureed meat or poultry, iron-fortified oat or barley cereal, cooked vegetables such as sweet potato, squash, peas, or carrots, mashed ripe banana or avocado, cooked apple or pear, prunes, lentils or beans, and plain whole-milk yogurt. Cook most fruits and vegetables until soft; ripe banana can be mashed raw (AAP).

## How to start: the first two weeks

**Example only.** Your baby's appetite and pace will vary.

| Days | What to try |
| --- | --- |
| 1–3 | Once a day, at a time your baby is alert and not starving. A little milk first, then ½–1 teaspoon of one food, then more milk if they want it (AAP). |
| 4–7 | If that went well, a second single-ingredient food. Slightly thicker texture. |
| 8–14 | 1–2 tablespoons per meal if your baby is keen. New foods every few days, including iron-rich ones and a first allergen. |
| From ~3–4 weeks | Building toward 2–3 small meals a day by 8 months (WHO), with mashed and lumpy textures and soft finger foods. |

Tips from AAP, NHS, and CDC:

- **Follow your baby's lead.** Wait for an open mouth before offering the spoon; stop when they close their lips, turn away, or push food away (NHS, CDC).
- **Expect faces and mess.** Rejection of a new taste is normal. The NHS says it may take 10 or more tries, KidsHealth says 8–10.
- **Sit together** without screens (NHS).
- **Upright and supervised**, always, in a high chair (CDC).

## Allergens: start them early

Waiting to introduce eggs, dairy, peanut, soy, wheat, fish, or sesame doesn't prevent allergies (AAP, KidsHealth, CDC). Introduce them **alongside** other first foods, in baby-safe forms (for example thinned smooth peanut butter, never whole nuts). If your baby has **severe eczema or an egg allergy**, talk to your pediatrician first. Details are in our [introducing allergens](/introducing-allergens) guide.

## Drinks with solids

- **Water:** not needed before solids. After solids start, small sips from an open, sippy, or straw cup are fine, up to 8 oz a day (AAP).
- **Juice:** none before 12 months (AAP, KidsHealth).
- **Cow's milk or plant milk as a drink:** not before 12 months; yogurt and cheese are fine (CDC, KidsHealth).

## Foods to avoid in the first year

- **Honey** (including honey pacifiers): risk of infant botulism (CDC).
- **Unpasteurized** milk, cheese, yogurt, or juice (KidsHealth).
- **Added salt and sugar**, salty processed foods, and no-calorie sweeteners (NHS, KidsHealth).
- **Choking hazards:** whole grapes, whole nuts and seeds, popcorn, hot dogs and meat sticks, chunks of meat or cheese, raw carrot or apple, globs of peanut butter, hard or sticky candy (AAP, CDC, NHS). Cut round foods into small pieces and cook hard vegetables until they mash easily (CDC).

## What changes after starting solids

Expect firmer, smellier, and more colorful stools, and sometimes bits of undigested food (AAP). If stools become very loose, watery, or full of mucus, slow down and talk to your pediatrician if it continues (AAP).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

- Before starting if your baby was **premature**, has a medical condition, **severe eczema**, or a known food allergy.
- If your baby shows signs of a reaction after a new food, such as a rash, vomiting, or diarrhea, stop that food and call your pediatrician (AAP). **Call emergency services** for swelling of the lips or tongue, trouble breathing, repeated vomiting, or sudden paleness or floppiness.
- If your baby still pushes all food out or shows no interest by about 7–8 months *(practical threshold, not an official one)*, or isn't gaining weight.
- If you're unsure about **vitamin D or iron** supplements; the AAP suggests asking during the first year.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can babies start eating baby food?**  
At about 6 months, once they sit with support, hold their head steady, show interest, and can swallow food. Not before 4 months.

**Can I start solids at 4 months?**  
Only after talking to your pediatrician. Some babies show readiness between 4 and 6 months, but official guidance targets about 6 months.

**When should I start rice cereal?**  
There's no need to start with rice. If you use infant cereal, start around 6 months with iron-fortified oat, barley, or multigrain cereal, and don't make rice the only grain.

**What purees are best for a 6 month old?**  
Smooth, single-ingredient purees, including iron-rich choices like meat or iron-fortified cereal, plus vegetables and fruits in any order.

**Will starting solids help my baby sleep through the night?**  
No. The NHS says starting solids won't make a baby more likely to sleep through.

**How much solid food should a 6 month old eat?**  
At first just a teaspoon or two once a day. Milk is still the main food; follow your baby's hunger and fullness cues.

**Can starting solids affect sleep?**
Solids often start around the same weeks as other 6-month changes, so nights can look bumpier at the same time. We do not have an official AAP figure for hours of sleep gained or lost from the first spoons. Do not drop night milk feeds just because solids have started, unless your pediatrician has already agreed a plan. See [6 month old sleep schedule](/6-month-old-sleep-schedule).
