# Fear of the Dark and Other Nighttime Fears

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Fear of the dark is a **waking** problem. The child is aware, can usually tell you something is scary, and will remember it in the morning. That is the opposite of a [night terror](/night-terrors), in which the child is not fully awake, may not know you, and typically has no memory of the episode. Nightmares — bad dreams the child can recall — sit closer to this page than to night terrors (HealthyChildren; KidsHealth).

This is also not [separation anxiety and sleep](/separation-anxiety-and-sleep), though the two can overlap at bedtime. Separation anxiety is "don't leave me." Fear of the dark is "this room is not safe." A child can have both. We will not retell the regression articles, and we will not write a science essay on night-light color — that remains a possible later topic.

## Short answer

Nighttime fears are common. KidsHealth notes that younger children may struggle to tell imagination from reality, and that many preschool-age children are afraid of the dark and of what might be under the bed or in a closet. Cincinnati Children's says it is normal for young children to worry about safety and security at bedtime, and that most children have nighttime fears at some point.

What helps, according to those sources and to KidsHealth's preschool-sleep page, is a calm, predictable routine, a quiet comfortable room, a **dim night light** if it helps the child feel safe, a comfort object when age-safe, and brief, serious reassurance. What does not help is teasing the fear, or building a nightly theater of "monster spray" and closet checks that teach the child you also believe something is in the room (Cincinnati Children's). KidsHealth's preschool advice is to avoid lying with the child until they fall asleep as a new every-night pattern, because it can make solo settling harder; Cincinnati Children's says an occasional stay until they fall asleep can be all right if fear is intense, but not night after night. We treat that as a **nuance, not a fight**: comfort is allowed; a brand-new all-night presence can become the only way the child will sleep.

Screens and scary stories before bed make this worse (KidsHealth, Cincinnati Children's). Turn screens off at least an hour before bedtime and keep them out of the bedroom (KidsHealth preschool sleep).

### Fear of the dark versus night terrors versus nightmares

| | Fear of the dark / bedtime fear | Nightmare | Night terror |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is the child awake? | Yes, at bedtime or after a waking | Yes, after a dream; they can usually be comforted | No — they may look terrified but are not fully awake |
| Will they remember it? | Yes | Often yes | Typically no |
| When? | Settling, or after waking in the night | More often in the second half of the night | Often in the first part of the night |
| What to do | Reassure, keep the plan boring and kind | Comfort, keep the visit short, back to bed | Keep them safe, do not try hard to wake them. See [night terrors](/night-terrors) |

## What to do in the evening, and after a scare

By day, Cincinnati Children's suggests giving the child a chance to talk about the fear, without forcing the conversation, and without agreeing that a monster is real. You can say the room is safe and that you are there to keep it that way. You do not need a ritual that "clears" the closet.

At bedtime:

- Keep the [routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby) the same length on easy nights and scared nights, as much as you can, so fear does not earn an hour of extra entertainment.
- A dim night light or a door left slightly open is a reasonable comfort (Cincinnati Children's, KidsHealth). Bright light that turns the room into daytime is less useful. We are not claiming a special color of light unless a later, sourced article can support it.
- A lovey or blanket belongs only in a sleep space that is old enough for it.
- If the child calls out, Cincinnati Children's suggests going back, asking what is wrong, and answering with safety ("we are here to make sure you are safe in your own bed") rather than starting a hunt.
- If they come to your room, take them back calmly. The goal is for the bed to feel like the safe place, not for the dark hall to become the path to your mattress every night — unless you have chosen a family-bed arrangement on purpose, with the safety limits that still apply for younger children.

After a nightmare, the same short-comfort rule holds (KidsHealth preschool): soothe, return them to their bed, keep the visit brief. If nightmares are frequent or the child seems distressed by day, talk with the pediatrician.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if fears are taking over daytime life, if the child cannot sleep in their room at all for a long stretch, if you cannot tell night terrors from nightmares, or if snoring, pauses, or unusual daytime sleepiness are also in the picture (see [baby snoring and mouth breathing](/baby-snoring-and-mouth-breathing)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a night light all right?**  
Yes, if it is dim and the child sleeps with it. KidsHealth and Cincinnati Children's both treat it as a reasonable comfort. This page does not recommend a color.

**Should I check the closet for monsters?**  
Cincinnati Children's advises against building rituals that treat imagined figures as real (including "monster spray"). Take the fear seriously; do not join the story.

**How is this different from a night terror?**  
The child with a night terror is not truly awake and usually will not remember it. Details and first aid are on [night terrors](/night-terrors).

**My 2-year-old suddenly will not go to bed. Is it fear or a regression?**  
It can be either, or both. Use [2-year sleep regression](/sleep-regression/2-years) for a time-limited bump, [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles) for ongoing stalling, and this page if the child can tell you they are scared.
