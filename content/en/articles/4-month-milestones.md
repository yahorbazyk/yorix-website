# 4 Month Milestones: What Most Babies Can Do and When to Talk to Your Doctor

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's development, hearing, vision, or feeding, contact your pediatrician.*

Around four months, many babies "wake up" socially: they hold your gaze longer, coo back at you, and start to giggle. This page lists the skills that most babies show by this age, explains where that list comes from and why it may differ from what you read elsewhere, and suggests simple everyday play. Looking for naps and bedtime instead? See our [4 month old sleep schedule](/4-month-old-sleep-schedule).

## Short answer

By 4 months, most babies **smile to get your attention, chuckle, coo ("ooo", "aah"), turn toward your voice, hold their head steady while held, bring their hands to the mouth, swing at toys, and prop up on their forearms during tummy time.** This list follows the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) checklist revised with the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) in 2022, which includes skills that **at least 75% of babies** have by this age. So if your baby is missing one, it is worth raising with your pediatrician rather than waiting. Rolling over is **not** on the 4-month list; CDC places rolling from tummy to back at 6 months.

## Why this list may look different from older lists

Before 2022, many milestone lists, including CDC's own, used ages at which about half of babies had a skill. The revision, described by Zubler and colleagues in *Pediatrics* (2022), moved to the **75th percentile**: a skill appears on the checklist at the age by which three out of four babies are expected to have it. The authors explain why: a 50% list sends too many healthy babies into "is something wrong?" territory, while also encouraging a wait-and-see habit. The total number of milestones shrank by about a quarter, roughly 4 in 10 of the final milestones were new, and a third of the milestones that were kept moved to a different age, most often an **older** one.

What this means for you: many babies do these things earlier than 4 months. A baby who does them by 4 months is on track. A baby who doesn't yet do one of them isn't necessarily delayed, but it is exactly the situation in which CDC and AAP want parents to speak up.

## 4 month milestones checklist (CDC, in our words)

| Area | Most babies by 4 months… |
| --- | --- |
| Social and emotional | Smile on their own to catch your attention; give a little chuckle (not yet a full laugh) when you play; look at you, wiggle, or make noises to get you to keep interacting |
| Language and communication | Coo with vowel-like sounds; answer your talking with sounds of their own; turn their head toward your voice |
| Thinking and learning | Open their mouth at the sight of breast or bottle when hungry; study their own hands with interest |
| Movement and physical | Keep their head steady without support while held; grasp a toy you place in their hand; swipe an arm at a toy; bring hands to the mouth; prop up on elbows or forearms on their tummy |

Source: CDC "Milestones by 4 Months" (updated May 2026). Nemours KidsHealth uses the same set.

## What these skills look like at home

- **The social smile becomes a tool.** At 2 months, CDC lists smiling *back* when you smile. By 4 months, babies start smiling first, to start the "conversation" themselves.
- **"Talking" back and forth.** Pause after you speak. Many 4-month-olds fill the gap with a coo, which is early turn-taking.
- **Hands are fascinating.** Long looks at their own fingers and bringing both hands to the mouth are normal and expected at this age, not a sign of hunger every time.
- **Stronger neck and upper body.** When you hold your baby upright, the head should no longer wobble much. On the floor, babies start resting on their forearms with the chest lifted. Regular [tummy time](/tummy-time) helps.
- **Batting at toys.** Babies swing at a hanging toy before they can grab it precisely. Grabbing on purpose (reaching for a toy they want) moves to the 6-month checklist.

## Skills that often come soon after 4 months

Some babies do these at 4 months and many don't; CDC places them at 6 months:

- rolling from tummy to back;
- pushing up on straight arms during tummy time;
- leaning on their hands while sitting with support;
- laughing out loud and blowing "raspberries".

The AAP's corrected-age article describes rolling from stomach to back as something that "often" appears around 4 months in term babies. That is not a contradiction: it describes a typical age, while CDC lists the age by which most babies (75%) do it.

## Play ideas for a 4 month old

These ideas are based on CDC's tips for this age:

1. **Answer every sound.** Smile, sound excited, and copy your baby's coos so they learn turn-taking.
2. **Floor time every day** on a blanket or mat, with a few safe toys within kicking and swiping distance.
3. **Mouthing is learning.** Let your baby explore safe objects with their mouth, as long as nothing is sharp, hot, or small enough to choke on.
4. **Track a toy.** With your baby on their back, move a bright toy slowly side to side and up and down.
5. **Read, sing, talk** about what you're doing during diaper changes and walks.
6. **Limit time in seats.** CDC suggests not keeping babies in swings, strollers, or bouncy seats for long stretches.
7. **No screens** except video calls with family; CDC does not recommend screen time under 2 years.

CDC also reminds parents that at this age babies need **only breast milk or formula**, with no other foods, water, or drinks, for about the first 6 months. If you're wondering about solids, see [when to start solids](/when-to-start-solids).

## How sleep and feeding fit in

Four months is also a time of big sleep changes for many families. That is a separate topic, covered in detail in our [4 month old sleep schedule](/4-month-old-sleep-schedule) and [4 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/4-months) pages. One link between the two: new motor skills such as rolling can temporarily disrupt sleep, and once a baby shows signs of starting to roll, swaddling needs to stop (see the safe-sleep notes on the schedule page).

## Babies born early: use corrected age

If your baby was born before 37 weeks, compare their skills with their **corrected age**, not their birthday. The AAP explains: take your baby's age in weeks and subtract how many weeks early they were born. Example: a baby born 8 weeks early who is now 16 weeks old has a corrected age of about 2 months, so the 2-month checklist is the fairer comparison. The AAP suggests using corrected age for the first 2 years.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

CDC's message is simple: **don't wait**. Contact your pediatrician and ask about developmental screening if:

- your baby isn't doing one or more of the skills above by 4 months (corrected age if born early);
- your baby has **lost** a skill they used to have;
- your baby doesn't react to loud sounds or doesn't seem to watch you as you move (both are already on CDC's 2-month list);
- your baby seems very floppy or very stiff, or uses one side of the body much more than the other;
- you simply have a gut feeling that something is off. The AAP notes that parents' concerns are often right.

If you and your doctor are still concerned, CDC suggests asking for a referral to a specialist and contacting your state's early intervention program (find it at cdc.gov/FindEI). In the U.S., an evaluation through early intervention is free for children under 3.

*Note: "very floppy or very stiff" is not a milestone. The 2022 review mentions "stiff or tight muscles" as an older CDC warning sign that is better picked up during a doctor's examination, so we list it as a reason to call, not as something to check off.*

## Frequently asked questions

**What should a 4 month old be doing?**  
Most babies this age smile to get attention, chuckle, coo, turn toward voices, hold their head steady when held, swing at toys, bring hands to the mouth, and push up on forearms on their tummy.

**Is it normal if my 4 month old isn't rolling over?**  
Yes. CDC lists rolling from tummy to back as a 6-month milestone. Keep up floor play and mention it at the 6-month visit if it hasn't happened.

**My baby doesn't laugh yet at 4 months. Should I worry?**  
A chuckle is on the 4-month list; a full laugh is on the 6-month list. If there's no chuckle or social smiling at all by 4 months, talk to your pediatrician.

**Should a 4 month old be sitting up?**  
Not independently. Sitting without support is on CDC's 9-month checklist.

**Do I use actual or corrected age for a premature baby?**  
Corrected age, for the first 2 years, according to the AAP.
