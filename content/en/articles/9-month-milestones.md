# 9 Month Milestones: Development Checklist for a 9 Month Old

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's development, hearing, vision, or feeding, contact your pediatrician.*

Nine months is when many babies discover that the world keeps existing when they can't see it. A dropped spoon is searched for, a parent who walks out is protested, and a stranger may be met with a suspicious stare. This guide lists the skills most babies show by 9 months, explains the one milestone that surprises parents most (crawling isn't on the list), and covers the developmental screening your pediatrician will do at this visit. For naps, bedtimes, and dropping the third nap, see our [9 month old sleep schedule](/9-month-old-sleep-schedule).

## Short answer

By 9 months, most babies **are wary of strangers, show a range of facial expressions, look up when you call their name, react when you leave, enjoy peek-a-boo, babble strings like "mamama" and "bababa", raise their arms to be picked up, look for things that drop out of sight, bang two objects together, get into a sitting position alone, sit without support, pass toys from hand to hand, and "rake" food toward themselves with their fingers.** These come from CDC's 2022 checklist, which only includes skills that **75% or more** of babies have by this age. **Crawling is not on it.** The AAP recommends a standardized developmental screening for every baby at the **9-month** checkup.

## 9 month milestones checklist (CDC, in our words)

| Area | Most babies by 9 months… |
| --- | --- |
| Social and emotional | Act shy, clingy, or afraid around unfamiliar people; show different faces (happy, sad, angry, surprised); turn when you say their name; react (look, reach, or cry) when you leave; smile or laugh at peek-a-boo |
| Language and communication | Make lots of different repeated sounds such as "mamama" and "bababa"; lift their arms to ask to be picked up |
| Thinking and learning | Search for an object that falls out of view; bang two things together |
| Movement and physical | Sit up from lying down without help; sit steadily without support; pass an object from one hand to the other; use fingers to drag food closer |

Source: CDC "Milestones by 9 Months" (updated May 2026); Nemours KidsHealth shows the same list.

## Why crawling isn't on the checklist

Parents often expect crawling to be *the* 9-month milestone, but CDC's checklists up to 1 year don't include it. CDC's milestone pages don't explain this omission. A likely reason is that the 2022 revision kept only skills that at least three in four babies reliably show by a given visit, and crawling varies a lot *(our interpretation)*. Cleveland Clinic says most babies crawl between 7 and 10 months, that one baby may start at 7 months and another closer to 11, and that both are fine. Babies also get around in different ways first: rolling across the room, belly ("commando") crawling, bottom scooting, or bear crawling on hands and feet.

What matters more at 9 months is that your baby is **moving with purpose and using both sides of the body**: sitting steadily, getting into sitting alone, reaching and transferring toys. If your baby isn't moving across the floor in any way and you're worried, mention it.

## Stranger shyness and "where did you go?"

The social part of the 9-month list can look like a step backward: a baby who happily went to anyone at 5 months may now cling and cry. It's the opposite of a problem. KidsHealth explains that between 4 and 7 months babies develop **object permanence**, the understanding that people and things still exist out of sight, and that between 8 months and 1 year many become more upset about being separated from a parent.

What helps (CDC and KidsHealth):

- Say a quick, cheerful goodbye instead of slipping out, so your baby learns what to expect, and greet them when you come back.
- Give new caregivers time; let your baby warm up while you're still in the room.
- Stay close as your baby explores, so they can check you're there.
- Play hiding games (a toy under a cloth, peek-a-boo) that practice "gone and back".

Clinginess often spills into bedtime; our [9 month old sleep schedule](/9-month-old-sleep-schedule) page covers that side.

## Hands, food, and the road to self-feeding

Raking food toward the body with the fingers is on the 9-month list. The neat thumb-and-pointer-finger pinch for small bits of food comes later, on the 1-year list. CDC suggests letting your baby practice feeding themselves with their fingers and drinking from a cup with a small amount of water, sitting with them at meals, and offering foods that are smooth, mashed, or finely chopped. Learn which foods are choking risks before offering finger foods; our [baby-led weaning](/baby-led-weaning) guide lists them.

## The 9-month checkup

The AAP asks pediatricians to check development at every well visit and to use a **standardized screening questionnaire at 9, 18, and 30 months**, with autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months. If you've filled in CDC's checklist (in the free Milestone Tracker app or on paper), bring it along. The AAP also suggests using the checklist for the younger age if your child is between two checklist ages.

## Play ideas for a 9 month old

Based on CDC's tips for this age:

1. **Turn sounds into words.** When your baby says "bababa", repeat it, then add a word: "ba-ba... ball!"
2. **Toys a little out of reach** on the floor to encourage crawling, scooting, or rolling, and cheer when they get there.
3. **Wave "bye-bye"** every time you leave, and try simple signs for "more" or "all done".
4. **Fill and dump.** Put blocks in a container, tip them out, and fill it again together.
5. **My turn, your turn.** Pass a toy back and forth.
6. **Find it.** Hide a toy under a blanket while your baby watches.
7. **Say what you want to see:** "time to sit" instead of "don't stand".
8. **Baby-proof** as your baby gets mobile: sharp and breakable things out of reach, medicines and cleaners locked away, and the Poison Help number (800-222-1222 in the U.S.) saved in your phone.

## Premature babies

Use **corrected age** for the first 2 years. A 9-month-old who was born 2 months early is usually compared with the 6-month list (AAP).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Don't wait for the next visit if:

- by 9 months (corrected age), your baby isn't doing one or more items on the checklist, especially **sitting without support**, **babbling with consonants**, or **responding to their name**;
- your baby has lost skills they used to have;
- your baby doesn't react to sounds or doesn't seem to see well;
- your baby uses one hand or one side of the body much more than the other, or seems unusually stiff or floppy *(clinical warning signs, not CDC milestones)*;
- you have any other concern; the AAP notes parents' concerns are often right.

In the U.S. you can also call your state's early intervention program for a free evaluation for children under 3 (HealthyChildren.org; find the number at cdc.gov/FindEI).

## Frequently asked questions

**What should a 9 month old be doing?**  
Most can sit alone and get into sitting by themselves, pass toys between hands, rake food with their fingers, babble "mamama" or "bababa", look when called by name, enjoy peek-a-boo, and look for dropped toys.

**My 9 month old isn't crawling. Should I worry?**  
Not by itself. Crawling isn't on CDC's checklist, and Cleveland Clinic notes that most babies crawl between 7 and 10 months, with some starting closer to 11. Talk to your pediatrician if your baby isn't sitting alone or isn't moving around in any way.

**Should a 9 month old be pulling up to stand?**  
Many do, but pulling up to stand is on CDC's 1-year checklist, not the 9-month one.

**Is stranger anxiety normal at 9 months?**  
Yes. Being shy, clingy, or fearful around strangers is one of CDC's 9-month milestones.

**Should a 9 month old say words?**  
Not necessarily. Babbling repeated syllables is expected; calling a parent "mama" or "dada" is on the 1-year list.
