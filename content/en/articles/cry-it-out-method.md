# The Cry It Out Method: What It Is, When It's Used, and What the Research Says

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"Cry it out" (CIO) may be the most argued-about phrase in parenting. Some parents credit it with saving their sanity; others feel it goes against every instinct they have. The research is calmer than the online debate, but it also has real gaps, especially for young babies. This page focuses on CIO itself: what researchers mean by it, the age questions, what studies on crying, stress, and attachment actually found, the "fuss it out" middle ground, and options with less crying. For a side-by-side table of every method, see [sleep training methods explained](/sleep-training-methods-explained); for check-in schedules, see [the Ferber method](/ferber-method-baby-sleep).

## Short answer

In research, cry it out is called **unmodified extinction**: after a bedtime routine, a baby is put down awake and parents don't go back in until a set morning time, except to check for illness, injury, or danger. A 2006 review for the American Academy of Sleep Medicine found extinction-based methods effective, and later follow-ups found **no long-term harm** in healthy babies who were sleep trained. But **CIO is not for newborns**: experts quoted in the research say extinction shouldn't be used under **6 months**, and there are essentially no studies of full CIO in babies that young. If you'd rather avoid long crying, graduated check-ins, "fuss it out," the chair method, or bedtime fading are reasonable alternatives.

## What "cry it out" actually means

The term gets used loosely, so it helps to be precise. Researchers describe three related approaches:

| Name | What parents do | Crying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Full extinction (classic CIO)** | Put baby down awake, leave, don't return until morning except for safety or illness | Usually the most at first, often for the shortest number of nights |
| **Graduated extinction (Ferber, "controlled crying")** | Leave, then return for brief checks at growing intervals | Moderate |
| **Extinction with parental presence** | Stay in the room but don't interact while the baby falls asleep | Varies; some parents find it easier to stay consistent |

Some websites call all three "cry it out." In this article, CIO means full extinction. Cleveland Clinic pediatrician Noah Schwartz, MD, describes it as "almost like going cold turkey": you put your baby down, say goodnight, and close the door, but you still pay close attention.

## Why CIO is not for newborns

Searches for "letting a newborn cry it out" are common, so to be clear: **the sources we checked don't recommend CIO in the newborn months.**

- **Newborns can't self-settle yet.** Huckleberry (app example) says sleep training is not recommended for newborns because they don't yet have the ability to self-soothe and need help to fall and stay asleep.
- **They still need night feeds.** Frequent waking in the first months is expected and protects feeding.
- **The evidence isn't there.** Writing in Psychology Today in 2025, Macall Gordon, M.A., who reviews infant sleep research, points out that there are no studies of full extinction in babies under 6 months, and few studies of any extinction method that report results for young infants separately.
- **Early behavioral programs haven't shown big benefits.** A 2013 systematic review by Douglas and Hill concluded that behavioral sleep interventions in the first 6 months had not been shown to reduce crying, prevent later sleep problems, or protect against postnatal depression, and could carry risks such as earlier weaning from breastfeeding and more maternal anxiety.

In the early weeks, responding to crying is part of the job. What you can do instead is build foundations: a predictable bedtime routine, a dark room at night, and sometimes putting your baby down drowsy. See [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training).

## What age is cry it out used?

There is no single official age, and sources disagree slightly:

- **Cleveland Clinic:** around **4–6 months**, when many babies can begin to fall asleep on their own.
- **Huckleberry (app example):** at least **5–6 months** for bedtime, and **6 months** before using it in the middle of the night, after checking with your pediatrician whether your baby still needs night feeds.
- **Blunden et al. (2016):** many professionals agree extinction shouldn't be used under **6 months**, and some argue for waiting until **8–12 months**.

A practical reading: not before 6 months for full CIO, only with a healthy, growing baby, and only after night feeds have been discussed with your pediatrician.

## What the research says

### Does CIO work?

Yes, for many children. The 2006 AASM review (Mindell and colleagues) concluded that extinction and related behavioral methods reliably improve bedtime problems and night waking. A 2018 evidence summary for family doctors (Korownyk and Lindblad) estimated that sleep training helps about **1 in 4 to 1 in 10** families compared with no sleep training, and noted that letting a baby cry it out was similarly effective, although parents often find it more stressful.

### Does it harm attachment or behavior?

- **Bilgin & Wolke, 2020 (UK, 178 infants):** parents reported how often they left their baby to cry it out at birth, 3, and 6 months. At **18 months**, there were **no adverse effects** on mother–infant attachment or behavioral development. Babies left to cry out a few times at birth and often at 3 months had *shorter* crying durations at 18 months. This was an observational study: it shows no link with harm, but it can't prove cause and effect, and it measured everyday parenting rather than a structured CIO program.
- **Sleep training trial with 5-year follow-up:** the family-doctor summary reports that a trial of a tailored sleep intervention that included sleep training, started at about 7 months, found **no differences in any of 20 outcomes** at age 5, including behavior, the parent–child relationship, and maternal mental health.

### What about stress hormones?

The most-quoted worry comes from **Middlemiss et al., 2012**. In a 5-day inpatient sleep program, 25 babies aged 4–10 months learned to settle through extinction. By day 3 the babies had stopped crying at bedtime, but their cortisol (a stress hormone) was still raised, while their mothers' cortisol had fallen. The authors described this as a mismatch between mother and baby. The study was small, its abstract describes no comparison group, and it measured only a few days, so it can't tell us about long-term effects. A 2016 trial by Gradisar and colleagues, testing graduated extinction rather than full CIO, found small *decreases* in babies' cortisol and no effect on attachment a year later.

### The honest summary

For healthy babies over 6 months, the studies we found show CIO and related methods improve sleep, and none found long-term harm. The evidence is thinner for full CIO than for graduated methods, and almost absent for young babies. Parents who decide against CIO aren't ignoring the science, and parents who use it at the right age aren't harming their child.

## If you choose CIO: a safety-first checklist

1. **Get the go-ahead.** Ask your pediatrician whether your baby is growing well and still needs night feeds.
2. **Pick a calm week.** Avoid starting during illness, teething flare-ups, travel, or a move.
3. **Agree as a team.** Cleveland Clinic stresses that everyone who does bedtime must follow the same plan.
4. **Meet every need first.** Fed, clean diaper, right temperature, a predictable routine, put down awake but sleepy.
5. **Keep sleep safe.** Back to sleep, on a firm, flat surface with nothing else in the crib. The AAP recommends room-sharing (without bed-sharing) for at least the first 6 months.
6. **Listen, even if you don't go in.** Dr. Schwartz advises checking on your child if the pitch of the crying changes or it goes on for a long time. Always go in for vomiting, illness, or anything that sounds different.
7. **Expect an "extinction burst."** The AASM review warns that crying can come back briefly after a few good nights. Responding inconsistently at this point teaches longer crying.
8. **Know your stopping point.** Child Mind Institute's expert suggests that if you see no progress after one to two weeks of consistent effort, take a break and try again later or choose a different approach.

How long will the crying last? Cleveland Clinic notes that some babies cry for a few minutes and others for hours. Huckleberry (app example) says some babies fuss for under 10 minutes, while persistent babies may cry for hours on the first nights, and most families see improvement within several days.

## "Fuss it out": the middle ground

"Fuss it out" (FIO) comes from Alexis Dubief's book *Precious Little Sleep*. You put your baby down awake and leave. Low-level fussing and protest are allowed; if the crying becomes intense, you go in and comfort briefly. The Swedish sleep resource Somnfakta notes that FIO hasn't been studied as a separate method and that the line between fussing and real crying is hard to define, but it shares principles with graduated extinction and is used as a compromise.

FIO tends to suit parents who:

- can tell their baby's "winding down" grumble from a distressed cry;
- want to give their baby a few minutes to try before stepping in;
- don't want to follow a fixed timer.

## Sleep training without cry it out

If CIO isn't right for your family, you have options:

- **Graduated check-ins (Ferber).** Brief visits at lengthening intervals. See [the Ferber method](/ferber-method-baby-sleep).
- **Chair method / camping out.** Sit by the crib and move a little farther away every few nights.
- **Pick up, put down.** Lift your baby to calm them, then lay them back down while still awake. Child Mind Institute lists this among the "no-cry" approaches and notes they can take longer.
- **Bedtime fading.** Temporarily move bedtime later so your baby falls asleep quickly, then bring it earlier in small steps. In the 2016 trial it shortened settling time without adverse effects.
- **Simply not sleep training.** Many babies consolidate sleep on their own with time; sleep training is a choice, not a requirement.

Child Mind Institute's expert points out that for some children, check-ins actually make them more worked up, while for others they're soothing. Your baby's temperament matters as much as the method's name.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the cry it out method harmful?**  
In the studies we found of healthy babies over 6 months, no long-term harm to attachment, behavior, or mental health was seen. One small study found babies' stress hormones stayed raised for a few days while learning to settle.

**At what age can you let a baby cry it out?**  
Not before 6 months for full extinction, according to the expert consensus quoted in Blunden et al. (2016). Some suggest waiting until 8–12 months. Talk to your pediatrician first.

**Can you let a newborn cry it out?**  
No. Newborns need help to settle and frequent night feeds, and full extinction hasn't been studied at that age.

**How long does cry it out take to work?**  
Often within several days to a week if you're consistent. If nothing improves after one to two weeks, pause and reassess.

**What's the difference between cry it out and Ferber?**  
With CIO you don't go back in until morning (except for safety). With Ferber you return for brief check-ins at set, growing intervals.

**What is the fuss it out method?**  
A looser approach: allow fussing, go in for real distress. It hasn't been studied on its own.
