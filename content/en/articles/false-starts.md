# False Starts: Why Your Baby Wakes Soon After Bedtime and How to Fix It

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Bedtime went smoothly, you've just sat down, and then the monitor crackles. Your baby is awake again, 40 minutes after falling asleep. Sleep coaches call this a **false start**: a full waking at the end of the first sleep cycle of the night. It isn't a medical diagnosis, and it doesn't mean anything is wrong with your baby. It usually points to bedtime timing, comfort, or the way your baby falls asleep, and those are things you can adjust. Below we explain how to work out which one it is.

## Short answer

A false start is when a baby **wakes fully within the first hour after bedtime**, often **30–45 minutes** in, around the end of the first sleep cycle. Huckleberry (app example) notes the term comes from sleep consultants rather than medical textbooks. The usual causes are being **undertired** (bedtime too soon after the last nap), **overtired** (last wake window too long), needing the **same help** used at bedtime to drift back off, **hunger**, or **discomfort**. Most families fix it by moving the last wake window by **about 15 minutes** in the right direction for a few days and keeping the bedtime routine and room the same.

## Why false starts happen

At the end of every sleep cycle, babies come up into lighter sleep. Cleveland Clinic describes infant cycles of about 45–50 minutes in the first 3–4 months, which is why the first waking so often lands 30–50 minutes after lights out. Most of the time babies drift into the next cycle. After the first cycle of the night, a few things make that harder.

### Undertired: not enough sleep pressure

If the last nap ended too close to bedtime or day sleep ran long, your baby may treat bedtime like another nap. Sleep Wonders notes that undertired babies often fall asleep easily and then **wake happily** 30–60 minutes later. Little Sleep Space lists other clues: taking **more than about 20 minutes** to fall asleep, or being playful and wide awake during the routine.

### Overtired: too much sleep pressure

A baby kept awake past their comfort zone can fall asleep quickly but struggle to slip into deep sleep. Little Sleep Space describes a frantic protest during the routine and falling asleep in **under 5 minutes** as typical overtired signs. Overtired false starts tend to be **upset** rather than happy wakings.

### The sleep conditions changed

If your baby fell asleep feeding, being rocked, or held, and then wakes in a still crib, the change can be enough to wake them fully at the end of the first cycle. Little Sleep Space points out that this isn't true for every baby who's rocked to sleep, but it's worth considering. Huckleberry (app example) adds that this becomes more relevant from about 3 months.

### Hunger

Sleep Wonders highlights babies who fall asleep before finishing the bedtime feed. A sleepy, partial feed may not carry them past the first cycle.

### Discomfort, new skills, or the room

Teething, a stuffy nose, tummy troubles, or illness can all show up as an early waking. So can new skills, such as rolling onto the tummy at bedtime and not being able to roll back, according to Little Sleep Space. Light leaking in or a sudden household noise (a shower next door, a door closing) can also break that first transition.

## Overtired or undertired? A quick check

| Clue | Points to undertired | Points to overtired |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to fall asleep | Long (20+ minutes), chatty | Very quick (under 5 minutes) |
| Mood during routine | Playful, alert | Crying, arching, frantic |
| Mood at the false start | Content or babbling | Upset, hard to settle |
| Last nap | Long, or ended late | Short, or ended early |
| Adjustment | Lengthen last wake window ~15 minutes | Shorten last wake window ~15 minutes |

These clues come from Little Sleep Space and Sleep Wonders. Not every baby fits neatly, so test one change at a time.

## How to stop false starts: a 5-step plan

1. **Log a week of evenings.** Note when the last nap ended, how long your baby was awake before bed, how fast they fell asleep, and when the false start came.
2. **Adjust the final wake window by about 15 minutes.** Little Sleep Space suggests lengthening it for undertired babies and shortening it for overtired ones, changing every 2–3 days. Use [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age) as a starting range.
3. **Offer a full feed earlier in the routine.** Feeding before bath or books, rather than as the last step, gives your baby a complete feed while awake.
4. **Keep the routine and room identical.** Same steps, dark room, and if you use white noise, keep it on all night so the sound doesn't change after your baby falls asleep.
5. **Respond the same way each time.** If you usually rock or feed back to sleep, do that; if your baby normally settles alone, offer brief reassurance and give them space. Little Sleep Space stresses consistency over any particular technique.

If false starts keep happening because your baby needs the same help to resettle every time, and your baby is old enough, practicing falling asleep in the crib at bedtime can help. See [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training).

## What to do during a false start

- **Don't treat it as the end of the night.** Keep lights low and voices quiet.
- **Avoid restarting the whole routine.** Resettle the way you normally would.
- **Check for an obvious problem first:** dirty diaper, a lost pacifier, stuck on the tummy, too hot or cold.
- **If your baby is truly hungry**, feed them, then note it. A pattern of hungry false starts suggests moving the bedtime feed earlier or making it fuller.
- **It's fine to simply accept it for now.** Little Sleep Space notes some families decide not to change anything and just wait for the phase to pass.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Huckleberry (app example) and Little Sleep Space suggest checking with a doctor if you see:

- false starts together with fever, congestion, or a persistent cough;
- noisy breathing, snoring, gasping, or pauses in breathing during sleep;
- false starts with **waking every hour every night**;
- fewer wet diapers or a change in feeding;
- repeated stiffening, jerking, or twitching that doesn't look like a normal startle;
- no improvement after a few weeks of schedule changes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my baby wake up 30 minutes after bedtime?**  
Usually because the first sleep cycle ended and something made it hard to go into the next one: being under- or overtired, needing help to resettle, hunger, or discomfort.

**Are false starts normal?**  
Yes. They're common enough that many families meet them at some point, and they're often temporary.

**Should I make bedtime earlier or later to fix false starts?**  
It depends. Later (a longer last wake window) for undertired babies, earlier for overtired babies, in steps of about 15 minutes.

**Do false starts happen at naps?**  
No. A 30–45 minute daytime nap is called a short nap. "False start" refers to bedtime.

**At what age do false starts happen?**  
They can happen at any age once sleep is organized into cycles, and Huckleberry (app example) says sleep associations become more relevant from about 3 months.
