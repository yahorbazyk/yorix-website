# 1 Month Old Sleep Schedule: Weeks 4 to 7, Wake Windows, and a First Routine

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

By about four weeks, the blur of the first days has usually eased a little. Feeding is more practiced, you've seen a few longer sleeps, and you may be wondering whether it's time for a "schedule." This page covers that narrow stretch from roughly **4 to 7 weeks**: what a realistic day looks like, how long a 4-week-old can stay awake, and how to start a first, simple bedtime routine. For the bigger picture of the first weeks (why newborns can't follow a clock, safe sleep, and red flags), see our [newborn sleep schedule guide](/newborn-sleep-schedule).

## Short answer

At 1 month there is **no fixed schedule** to aim for. Sleep Foundation says newborns don't follow a predictable day-night pattern for roughly the first two months. What you can expect: **about 14–17 hours of sleep in 24 hours** (National Sleep Foundation panel; KidsHealth) in pieces that follow feeds, with **wake windows of about 30–90 minutes**. Breastfed babies feed about every 2–3 hours and bottle-fed babies about every 3–4 hours (KidsHealth). The body clock begins to develop around **4–6 weeks** (Pregnancy, Birth and Baby), so this is a good time to start a short, repeatable bedtime routine. Expect it to work as a cue, not a switch.

## How much does a 1 month old sleep?

Mayo Clinic says babies sleep about 16 hours a day in the first month. The National Sleep Foundation panel recommends 14–17 hours for 0–3 months. The AASM has no figure under 4 months, and Cleveland Clinic explains why: research hasn't tied a specific amount of sleep to health benefits at this age, so "normal" is very wide.

**App example (not clinical guidance):** Huckleberry suggests aiming for about 15.5 hours at 1 month.

Sleep usually comes in pieces. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says babies under 3 months may wake after one or two sleep cycles, so every 1 to 3 hours. Cleveland Clinic describes sleeps of about 30 minutes to 3 hours in the first two months.

## 4 week old wake window (and weeks 5–7)

Four weeks sits right on the line between two age bands in most charts, which is why numbers you find online jump around:

| Source | Up to 1 month | From 1 month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sleep Foundation | 0.5–1.5 hours | 1–3 hours (1–4 months) |
| Cleveland Clinic | 0.5–1 hour | 1–2 hours (1–3 months) |
| Napper (app example) | 35–60 minutes (0–4 weeks) | 60–90 minutes (4–12 weeks) |
| Huckleberry (app example) | 30–90 minutes at 1 month | — |

**In practice:** most 4–7 week olds manage somewhere around **45 to 75 minutes**, feed included, which is where all four sources overlap *(our reading of the table, not an official figure)*. Mayo Clinic puts it simply: after a newborn has been awake for 1 to 2 hours, they most often need to sleep again. Sleep Foundation adds that there's no need to stretch a newborn's wake windows to get longer sleeps, because most babies lengthen them on their own.

Watch the baby more than the clock. Sleep Foundation lists yawning, eye rubbing, blinking more often, staring off to the side, and crying as signs of tiredness. It suggests laying your baby down a few minutes before they get too tired or upset.

## Example day for a 1 month old (example only)

At this age a "schedule" is really a repeating loop of **feed, a short awake time, then sleep**. This is **an example only**. Your baby's loops will be longer or shorter, and no two days will match.

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 7:00 a.m. | Feed; open the curtains |
| 7:50 a.m. | Asleep (about 50 minutes awake) |
| 9:30 a.m. | Feed, diaper, a few minutes of tummy time |
| 10:30 a.m. | Asleep |
| 12:15 p.m. | Feed, cuddle, short walk outside |
| 1:10 p.m. | Asleep (maybe in the stroller) |
| 3:00 p.m. | Feed and play |
| 3:50 p.m. | Asleep |
| 5:30 p.m. | Feed |
| 6:30–8:00 p.m. | Short naps and feeds as your baby needs them |
| About 9:00–10:30 p.m. | Bedtime routine and a feed before the longest stretch |
| Night | Feeds roughly every 2–4 hours, kept dark and quiet |

**Why such a late bedtime?** Huckleberry (app example) notes that at 1 month the longest stretch often doesn't start until about 10 p.m. or later, and that bedtime usually moves earlier over the next couple of months.

## Feeding sets the pace

- **How often:** KidsHealth says breastfed babies feed about every 2–3 hours and bottle-fed babies about every 3–4 hours.
- **Long sleepers:** KidsHealth advises waking a baby every 3–4 hours to feed until they show good weight gain, which usually happens in the first couple of weeks. After that, HealthyChildren.org says healthy, growing babies usually don't need to be woken to feed. Check with your pediatrician if growth, feeding, or diapers aren't on track.

**App example:** Huckleberry describes an "eat, play, sleep" pattern for the early months and cautions that it may need adjusting by 3–5 months, as wake windows grow.

## A first bedtime routine for a 1 month old

You don't need a long ritual. The goal is a short, repeatable set of signals. The NHS lists ideas such as a bath, night clothes and a fresh diaper, dim lights, a lullaby, and a goodnight cuddle. Sleep Foundation describes cuddling, rocking, walking, and feeding as part of a relaxing newborn routine. A simple version:

1. Dim the lights about 15 minutes before the last evening feed.
2. Change the diaper and put on a clean sleepsuit or sleep sack.
3. Feed in a quiet, low-lit room.
4. Sing the same short song.
5. Lay your baby on their back in the bassinet.

KidsHealth says it may take a few weeks for a baby's brain to learn the difference between night and day, and there are no tricks to speed this up. Do the routine anyway: you're building the cue for later.

**Help the body clock along.** The NHS suggests opening curtains, playing, and not worrying about everyday noise during daytime naps. At night, keep lights low, talk little, and change diapers only when needed.

## Holding, crying, and "bad habits"

At 1 month, comforting is not spoiling. The CDC says you can't spoil a baby by holding or responding to them, and Mayo Clinic advises not letting babies cry alone in the first month. Cleveland Clinic says babies younger than 3 months can't yet regulate their emotions and rely on you to soothe them back to sleep. HealthyChildren.org suggests helping your baby fall asleep with soothing sensations like rocking or sucking. Our [contact naps guide](/contact-naps) covers safe ways to handle a baby who only sleeps on you.

The CDC also notes that infant crying is often worse in the first few months and gets better. If you feel overwhelmed, put your baby down somewhere safe and step away for a few minutes.

## Sleep safety reminders

Every sleep, day or night: on the back, on a firm, flat surface with only a fitted sheet, in your room but not in your bed (KidsHealth; HealthyChildren.org). If your baby falls asleep in a car seat, swing, or on a feeding pillow, move them to their sleep space. KidsHealth warns against weighted blankets, sleepers, or swaddles. If you use a pacifier while breastfeeding, the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network suggests waiting until about 4–6 weeks.

## What can make it harder

- **Keeping your baby awake** in the day to "save" sleep for night. KidsHealth says overtired infants sleep worse.
- **Bright, lively night feeds.** Cleveland Clinic suggests avoiding talking or playing during night feeds.
- **Comparing with a friend's baby.** The range of normal is wide.
- **Skipping sleep yourself.** Share night duties if you can (NHS).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Call your doctor right away for a fever in a baby under 12 weeks, a baby who is unusually hard to wake or too sleepy to feed, fewer wet diapers than usual, breathing that looks like hard work, or if you are worried (HealthyChildren.org symptom checker). Also speak up if you are feeling low or anxious.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I put my 1 month old on a sleep schedule?**  
Not a clock-based one. Sleep Foundation says newborns don't follow a predictable pattern for about the first two months. A feed-play-sleep loop and an evening routine are enough.

**How long should a 4 week old be awake?**  
Roughly 30–90 minutes, often about 45–75 minutes including the feed (Sleep Foundation; Cleveland Clinic; app charts).

**How much should a 1 month old sleep?**  
About 14–17 hours in 24 hours (National Sleep Foundation panel); Mayo Clinic says about 16 hours in the first month.

**When should a 1 month old go to bed?**  
Often late, around 9–10:30 p.m., at the start of the longest stretch. Bedtime tends to move earlier over the next months.

**Should I start a bedtime routine at 1 month?**  
Yes, if you'd like. Keep it short and calm; it becomes more useful as the body clock develops.
