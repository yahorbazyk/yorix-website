# Reflux and Baby Sleep: Why the Back Still Comes First

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Spitting up is common in young babies. It is usually gastroesophageal reflux (GER), not a reason to change the sleep surface. This page is about **how reflux and sleep fit together**: the difference between ordinary spit-up and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), what you can try while your baby is awake, and the sleep rule that does not change. The full safe-sleep list lives on [safe sleep for babies](/safe-sleep-for-babies). This is not a second copy of that hub, and it is not a [colic](/colic) article — reflux and crying can overlap, but they are not the same diagnosis.

## Short answer

Most infants who spit up have GER, not GERD. The U.S. National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) reports that **about 70 to 85 percent of infants have daily regurgitation by 2 months**, and that **most no longer have GER symptoms by 12 to 14 months**. NHS advice is similar: reflux is common and babies usually grow out of it by around 1 year. Mayo Clinic notes that it is **unusual for infant reflux to continue after 18 months** — a slightly later endpoint than the NHS and NIDDK figures, not a number we should average.

For sleep, the instruction is the same whether the issue is ordinary spit-up or medically diagnosed GERD: **place the baby on the back, on a firm, flat, non-inclined surface, for every sleep**. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says babies with gastroesophageal reflux should still be placed on their backs. There is no evidence that back-sleeping increases choking in these infants. The AAP, quoting the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN), advises **against positional therapy** — head elevation, side-lying, or tummy-down — to treat GERD in sleeping infants. The NHS says the same: sleep them flat on the back; **do not raise the head of the cot or Moses basket**. Inclined sleepers, wedges, rolled towels, and overnight time in a car seat or swing are not a reflux workaround.

## GER is common; GERD is a different conversation

GER is stomach contents coming back up the esophagus. In babies it often looks like effortless spit-up or a "wet burp." NIDDK lists other everyday names parents hear: acid reflux, regurgitation, or simply spitting up. Lying flat much of the day, a still-maturing lower esophageal sphincter, and a mostly liquid diet all make this more likely in the first months (NIDDK, Mayo Clinic). As babies spend more time upright and take more solids, GER typically eases.

**GERD** is GER that causes repeated, bothersome symptoms or complications — for example, poor weight gain, painful esophagitis, feeding refusal, or breathing problems (NIDDK). Experts are not sure how common GERD is in infants because it is hard to diagnose. It is more often discussed in babies born prematurely and in babies with certain lung, neurologic, or esophageal conditions (NIDDK, Mayo Clinic). NHS pages also describe **silent reflux**, in which milk travels back up and is swallowed again, so there may be little visible spit-up. That label is not a license to change the sleep position; it is a reason to talk with the pediatrician if the baby seems uncomfortable, feeds poorly, or is not gaining.

Many other problems can look like GERD (NIDDK, Mayo Clinic). Arching, crying, or poor feeding is not a home diagnosis. Only a clinician can sort reflux from a milk-protein allergy, a blockage such as pyloric stenosis, or something else.

## The sleep rule does not change

Parents often hear that a reflux baby will "choke" on the back, or will be more comfortable on a wedge or in a reclined seat. The AAP addresses this directly: the infant airway and gag reflex protect against aspiration on the back, and **the supine position on a flat surface does not increase the risk of choking**. HealthyChildren restates it in one sentence: babies with gastroesophageal reflux should still be placed on their backs.

Elevating the mattress or using a pillow, wedge, or positioner under or over it is **ineffective for reducing GER** and is not recommended (AAP 2022). Infants also tend to slide down an incline, which can compromise breathing, or roll to the side or front. The AAP adds that sleep surfaces with inclines of more than 10 degrees are unsafe. Michigan's public-health summary of the same AAP and NASPGHAN advice is blunt: do not elevate the head of the crib, and do not have the baby sleep in a car seat or swing as reflux treatment.

If your baby falls asleep in your arms after a feed, move them to their own firm, flat surface on the back when you can do so safely — the same rule as on our [bed-sharing](/bed-sharing-and-co-sleeping) and [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) pages. Being held upright **after a feed, while awake and supervised**, is a different activity from overnight sleep.

## What may help while your baby is awake

These steps are for feeds and the stretch afterward, not for propping the crib:

- **Hold the baby upright** during feeding and for about 20 to 30 minutes afterward, if practical (NIDDK; HealthyChildren says at least 20 minutes and to supervise closely). Limit bouncing or pressing on the belly right after a meal (HealthyChildren).
- **Burp during and after feeds** (NIDDK, NHS, HealthyChildren, KidsHealth).
- **Avoid overfeeding.** Smaller, more frequent feeds may help; follow your pediatrician's volumes so total intake stays adequate (NIDDK, NHS, HealthyChildren).
- **Keep the baby smoke-free.** NIDDK lists avoiding secondhand smoke as a lifestyle measure for GER or GERD.
- **Do not start thickeners, special formulas, or a parental elimination diet on your own.** NIDDK and HealthyChildren describe these as doctor-directed options. If thickening is advised, the AAP has recommended oatmeal rather than rice cereal because of arsenic concerns in rice — only under clinical advice, and never as a reason to prop a bottle in the crib.

Tummy time still matters; HealthyChildren simply suggests postponing it for a while after meals. Upright, supervised time in your arms is not the same as leaving a baby to sleep in a swing or seat. For sitting-device safety, see [baby sleeping in a car seat](/baby-sleeping-in-car-seat).

Medicines such as proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers are reserved for esophagitis or bothersome GERD that has not improved with lifestyle changes, and they are a clinician's decision. NIDDK notes that infants on these medicines may have a higher chance of certain infections. Do not start them from a website.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Call promptly, or seek emergency care if the situation looks urgent, if your baby (NIDDK, Mayo Clinic, NHS):

- is not gaining weight, or is losing weight;
- vomits forcefully (projectile vomiting), or vomits green or yellow fluid, blood, or material that looks like coffee grounds;
- has blood in the stool;
- refuses feeds, or has trouble swallowing, choking, or breathing problems, including a cough that will not go away;
- is extremely irritable, especially with feeds, or has much less energy than usual;
- shows signs of dehydration, such as no wet diaper for 3 hours or more (NIDDK);
- begins forceful vomiting or new regurgitation under 2 weeks of age or at 6 months or older (NIDDK; Mayo Clinic also flags spit-up that begins at 6 months or older).

In a baby 3 months or younger, a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher is also a reason to call right away — the same fever rule we use on other illness pages, not a reflux-specific number.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should a baby with reflux sleep on their back?**  
Yes. The AAP, NASPGHAN, NHS, and NIDDK all say the back, on a flat surface, is the sleep position — including for GER and GERD.

**Can I raise the head of the crib or use a wedge?**  
No. Elevation does not reliably reduce GER and can make the sleep space less safe (AAP, NHS).

**Is silent reflux a reason to use a reclined sleeper?**  
No. Silent reflux is a descriptive label for reflux with little visible spit-up (NHS). It does not change the flat, on-the-back rule. Discuss symptoms with the pediatrician.

**When does baby reflux go away?**  
Often by about 12 to 14 months (NIDDK) or around the first birthday (NHS). Mayo Clinic says it is unusual after 18 months. If symptoms are severe, or they start late, get them checked rather than waiting for a birthday.

**Is reflux the same as colic?**  
No. Ordinary spit-up is often painless. Persistent, hard-to-soothe crying has its own page: [colic](/colic). Some babies have both issues; only a clinician can sort the cause.
