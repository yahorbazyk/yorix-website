# Baby Twitching in Sleep: What Is Normal, and What Is Not

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Tiny jerks in a sleeping newborn are one of the most Googled, and most often harmless, sleep sights. This page separates **ordinary active-sleep twitches**, a condition called **benign neonatal sleep myoclonus (BNSM)**, and **signs that need a clinician**. It is not a diagnosis, and it is not a second [crying in sleep](/baby-crying-in-sleep) or [grunting](/newborn-grunting-in-sleep) article.

## Short answer

Brief twitches, smiles, and startles are common in [active sleep](/baby-sleep-cycles). That is not automatically a seizure. **Benign neonatal sleep myoclonus** is a specific, usually harmless pattern: repetitive, involuntary jerks of the arms, legs, or trunk that happen **only in sleep** and **stop immediately when the baby wakes**. Sleep Foundation, citing the literature and AASM classification, says symptoms usually start in the first month, often peak between 2 and 5 weeks, resolve before 3 months in about two-thirds of infants, and are gone by 6 months in **95%** (97% by the first birthday). The same article, citing AASM, calls formal BNSM uncommon — just under **4 in 10,000** live births — and about twice as common in males. Ordinary "sleep wiggles" are much more common than that formal diagnosis. **See a doctor** for any seizure-like movements you are unsure about, and urgently if twitching continues when the baby is awake, or comes with a color change or a change in breathing.

## Ordinary twitches versus BNSM

**Ordinary active-sleep movement** looks like small startles, finger flexes, irregular breathing, or a brief smile. It comes and goes in light sleep and is part of normal newborn [cycles](/baby-sleep-cycles).

**BNSM** is more repetitive and dramatic — clusters of jerks that can involve both sides, the trunk, or sometimes the whole body. Sleep Foundation lists four diagnosis criteria clinicians use: repetitive myoclonus of the trunk or limbs (or the whole body); a young infant, usually from birth to 6 months; movements **only during sleep** that **always stop at once** on waking; and no other explanation such as medication, a neurological condition, or another sleep disorder. Brain-wave studies in BNSM do not look like seizures, but you cannot see brain waves at home. That is why a pediatrician, not a parent with a search result, should label it.

Cleveland Clinic's parent page on infant leg tremors makes the same practical point: benign sleep myoclonus stops as soon as the baby wakes, and breathing and color stay normal.

## Red flags — do not wait and hope

This page cannot tell a harmless jerk from a seizure. Call emergency services or go to urgent care if:

- The movements **continue after the baby is clearly awake**, or start while they are awake.
- There is a **change in color**, especially blue or gray lips or face, or **breathing looks hard**.
- The baby is unusually floppy, hard to rouse, or not feeding as usual.
- Jerks come with eye deviation, long stiffening, or something that feels "different from sleep wiggles" to you.

See the pediatrician promptly (or send a video from a well-lit, safe recording) for any repetitive seizure-like movements, even if they seem to stop when the baby wakes. Sleep Foundation is explicit: talk to a doctor to rule out epilepsy or another condition. Families whose baby was exposed to opioids in pregnancy should also mention twitching, because withdrawal care can be different.

## What not to do

Sleep Foundation notes that trying to pin the limbs or giving anticonvulsant drugs can **worsen** true BNSM, and that treatment is usually unnecessary once the diagnosis is made. Do not start or stop medicines on the basis of this article. Do not put anything in the crib to "hold them still." Keep the sleep space [safe](/safe-sleep-for-babies): back, firm, flat, clear.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Any time you are not sure. Bring a video if you have one, and note whether the movements stop when the baby wakes, how long they last, and whether color and breathing stay normal. That information is more useful than a label you found online.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal for a baby to twitch in their sleep?**  
Brief twitches in active sleep are common. Repetitive, dramatic jerks still deserve a doctor look, even if they may turn out to be BNSM.

**How can I tell twitching from a seizure?**  
The most useful home clue is whether it **stops when they wake**. That is not a complete test. Leave the distinction to a clinician.

**Does benign neonatal sleep myoclonus need treatment?**  
Usually no, if that is truly the diagnosis. It typically fades over the first months.

**Will this raise their risk of epilepsy later?**  
Sleep Foundation reports that children are not generally thought to be at higher risk of seizures from BNSM itself, and development is typically normal. That is not a reason to skip an exam.
