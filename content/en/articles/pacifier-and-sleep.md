# Pacifier and Sleep: When to Start, How It Relates to SIDS, and When to Stop

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A pacifier (a dummy, in UK advice) is one of the few sleep items the American Academy of Pediatrics actually encourages at nap time and bedtime. The reason is not that it is a magic sleep trainer. It is that pacifier use during sleep is associated with a **lower risk of SIDS**. Whether you offer one is still your decision. This page covers when to start, how to use one safely, and why the "right" age to wean depends on which expert body you read.

## Short answer

Yes, babies can sleep with a pacifier. If you are breastfeeding, the AAP and Lullaby Trust ask you to wait until breastfeeding is going well, often around **3 to 4 weeks**. If you are formula-feeding, you can offer one earlier. Offer it at **every sleep**, do not force it, and do not put it back if it falls out after your baby is asleep. Never tie it to the crib, the neck, or clothing. On when to stop, sources disagree: Lullaby Trust often suggests easing off between **6 and 12 months**; AAP and AAFP mention reducing use in that window to limit ear infections; the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry often talks about stopping by age **3** to protect the bite; HealthyChildren.org discusses mouth and teeth effects if strong sucking continues at **2 to 4 years**. There is no single official "correct" birthday.

## Why a pacifier at sleep time is in the safe-sleep list

The AAP's 2022 safe-sleep advice includes offering a pacifier at naps and bedtime because it is associated with a lower SIDS risk, even if it falls out after the baby is asleep. Lullaby Trust and NHS say the same in slightly different words: think about using a dummy; some research suggests it can lower SIDS risk. Sleep Foundation, reviewing the literature, notes that researchers still do not fully understand the mechanism. Ideas that have been suggested include a slightly more open airway, a change in arousal, or an effect on sleep position; none of those is settled. A Cochrane review (Psaila and colleagues, 2017), cited by Sleep Foundation, is part of that cautious evidence base.

Two practical implications follow:

- You should not skip the rest of [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) because a pacifier is in use. A pacifier is an extra, not a substitute for back sleeping, a clear crib, and a smoke-free room.
- You should not force a baby who refuses. Plenty of babies never take one and can still be slept safely.

The protective association has been observed even in less-than-ideal sleep environments, according to Sleep Foundation's summary, but that is not permission to use a pacifier as a way to make an unsafe setup "OK."

## When and how to introduce one

**If you are breastfeeding.** Wait until the latch is comfortable, milk supply is in, and weight gain is on track. HealthyChildren.org and Sleep Foundation put that at about 3 to 4 weeks. Lullaby Trust says around four weeks, or whenever breastfeeding is well established. "Nipple confusion" is a phrase you will hear; Sleep Foundation notes that evidence for it is mixed, and that the AAP still prefers the wait. In special situations (preterm babies, procedures, certain NICU care), staff may recommend a pacifier earlier; follow that clinical advice.

**If you are formula-feeding.** Sleep Foundation says a pacifier can be offered from birth.

**How to offer it.** At the start of a nap or night sleep, when you know your baby is not hungry. Do not dip it in honey (unsafe under 1 year) or anything sweet. Lullaby Trust goes further and suggests not offering it during ordinary awake time if you are using it as part of a sleep routine, so it stays tied to sleep. That particular "sleep only" point is Lullaby Trust's, not something we found as an AAP rule, so treat it as one organization's tip.

HealthyChildren.org shopping and safety rules are specific and worth following:

- Choose a **one-piece** pacifier so it cannot come apart.
- The shield should be at least **1½ inches** across, of firm plastic, with ventilation holes, so it cannot fit entirely into the mouth.
- Do not use a bottle nipple as a pacifier; a hard suck can pull it out of the ring.
- Clean it well (HealthyChildren.org describes boiling or a dishwasher for the early months, then soap and water). Check the nipple for damage and replace it when it changes color or tears, or when it passes the maker's expiry.
- Buy a spare. They disappear at the worst moment.
- **Never tie** a pacifier to the crib, around the neck, or to a hand or clothing. Cords are a strangulation risk. The AAP repeats this in the safe-sleep policy.

Be ready for a downside in the first months: if your baby depends on the pacifier to fall asleep and cannot replace it yet, you may be up to find it. That is inconvenient. It is not a reason to clip it to the sleepsuit.

## Risks, and the weaning-age disagreement

Pacifiers are not only a SIDS topic. Sleep Foundation and HealthyChildren.org also discuss:

- **Middle-ear infections**, which appear more often in pacifier users, especially after about 12 months and in group care. AAP and AAFP have recommended *reducing* pacifier use between 6 and 12 months in that context.
- **Dental and bite changes** if strong sucking continues once the teeth are in. HealthyChildren.org says that if the habit stops before the permanent front teeth come in, the bite will often correct itself; if strong sucking goes on at 2 to 4 years, the mouth or tooth alignment may be affected. The AAPD, as quoted by Sleep Foundation, often uses age 3 as a dental stop line.
- **Thrush** (an oral yeast infection) can be a reason to replace and thoroughly clean pacifiers; ask your pediatrician if you see white patches that do not wipe away.

Lullaby Trust's consumer advice is to gently stop between 6 and 12 months to avoid longer-term problems such as misaligned teeth and ear infections. That is earlier than the dental "by age 3" line. Sleep Foundation is honest that the range in the literature runs from 6 months to 4 years, and that parents have to weigh comfort, SIDS-protection in the first 6 months, ear infections, teeth, and speech, with a pediatrician if they are unsure. We agree with that framing and are not going to pick a single Yorix age.

When you do wean, HealthyChildren.org's general advice on sucking habits is to stay kind: ignore what you can, praise stretches without the pacifier, and avoid shame or punishment. Some families stop at night last. Cold-turkey works for some toddlers; others need a gradual fade. If the habit is affecting the palate or teeth, a pediatric dentist can help.

## When to talk to your pediatrician or dentist

Ask at a well visit if you are unsure when to start, especially if breastfeeding is still being established. Ask sooner if the pacifier seems to interfere with feeding, if ear infections are stacking up, or if you see changes in the teeth or the roof of the mouth. Never use a string or clip in the crib to solve the "it fell out" problem — come up with a safer plan instead.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a newborn sleep with a pacifier?**  
A formula-fed newborn can be offered one from birth. A breastfed newborn should usually wait until breastfeeding is going well, often 3 to 4 weeks, unless a clinician has recommended otherwise.

**Does a pacifier really reduce SIDS?**  
It is associated with a lower risk. The mechanism is not fully understood, and it does not replace the other safe-sleep rules.

**Should I put the pacifier back in when it falls out?**  
No, not once your baby is already asleep.

**Will a pacifier ruin breastfeeding?**  
Used after breastfeeding is established, it usually does not, which is why the AAP uses a delay rather than a ban. If latch or supply is still fragile, wait.

**When should I wean the pacifier?**  
Sources do not agree. Many UK and ear-infection recommendations cluster at 6–12 months; dental guidance often allows use into the toddler years and aims to finish by about 3. Discuss the trade-offs with your child's clinician, especially after the first birthday.
