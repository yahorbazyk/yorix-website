# Sleep After Separation or Divorce: Two Homes, One Predictable Night

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

When a child splits time between two households, the hard part is often not "will they ever sleep again." It is that **bedtime and the going-to-bed pattern can stop matching**. HealthyChildren.org's page on sleep problems after separation or divorce (last updated 3 July 2019) is the source this short, practical article follows. It is not legal advice, not a custody guide, and not a second [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles) or [siblings sharing a room](/siblings-sharing-a-room) page. We will not expand it to adopted or foster sleep.

## Short answer

HealthyChildren.org: it can be difficult to keep consistent bedtimes and going-to-bed routines when a child divides time between two homes. Weekend or holiday visits to the other parent are often taken in stride, like other exceptions to a basic schedule. Problems are harder when the child makes **frequent changes** — a few days in one home, the rest in the other — or when the two homes have **widely different styles** and little cooperation. Sleep trouble, bedtime refusals, and night waking are common when a child feels torn between two parents, two rooms, and two different routines.

In a stressful stretch, a child may also regress for a while: thumb sucking or bedwetting may return, or appear for the first time, in a preschooler or school-age child; night fears can make it hard to settle. An older child may withdraw, push back, or try too hard to please. Insomnia or oversleeping can show up under emotional stress (HealthyChildren.org). Those are reasons for patience, not for a brand-new sleep-training program on night one in the new room.

### What actually helps, according to that page

| Practical step | Why it is on this page |
| --- | --- |
| Keep **duplicates** of especially favorite bedtime toys, books, or music in both homes | So a forgotten object does not become the whole fight (HealthyChildren.org) |
| Aim for a **similar bedtime and a similar short routine** in both places | The fight is usually irregularity, not the color of the duvet |
| Work together more closely than may feel natural | HealthyChildren.org: it takes unusually close teamwork — perhaps even closer than when you lived under one roof |
| Give regressions time once the new pattern is stable | Sleep problems and other symptoms should gradually ease over **several weeks**. If they persist, talk to the pediatrician, who may suggest counseling or a support group |

We will not write a parenting-plan clause, a holiday calendar, or advice about who is "right." If the child is stalling, leaving the bed, or afraid of the dark in *one* settled home, use the pages written for those jobs: [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles), [toddler won't stay in bed](/toddler-wont-stay-in-bed), [fear of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark), [separation anxiety at night](/separation-anxiety-and-sleep). If a new baby has arrived in one household, [sleep regression](/sleep-regression) and the age-page already cover that bump.

A comfort object, a dim [night light](/night-lights-and-baby-sleep), and the same few books at both addresses go farther than two competing "systems." Screens off before bed still applies (the AAP's at-least-one-hour rule is on the [melatonin](/melatonin-and-childrens-sleep) and preschool-schedule pages). Do not start melatonin to "get them through the transition" without the pediatrician.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

HealthyChildren.org: once the family's situation has stabilized, these sleep problems should gradually disappear over several weeks. If they do not, talk to your pediatrician. Ask sooner if the child is unsafe at night, if bedwetting is new and persistent, if you worry about mood or withdrawal, or if nights are a battleground in both homes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should both homes use the exact same bedtime?**  
As close as real life allows. The source's point is consistency and cooperation, not a legal standard.

**What if one home has no routine?**  
This page cannot referee. You can still keep your house predictable, duplicate the comfort objects, and ask the pediatrician for help if sleep does not recover after several weeks of a more stable pattern.

**Is this the same as night-time separation anxiety?**  
It can look similar. The difference is the two-home, two-routine stress. Use [separation anxiety at night](/separation-anxiety-and-sleep) when the fear is being apart from you in a settled house.
