# Should You Wake a Sleeping Baby?

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"Never wake a sleeping baby" is a saying, not a medical rule. In the first weeks it can even be the wrong advice, because some babies need to be woken to eat. Later, there are still a few reasons to end a nap or a morning lie-in. This page is about those exceptions. It is not a [night-weaning](/night-weaning) guide, and it is not a second [newborn schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule).

## Short answer

**Sometimes yes, you should wake them.** KidsHealth says that in the first weeks you should wake a newborn to feed about every 3 to 4 hours until they have good weight gain — often the first couple of weeks — and then you may be able to let a longer night stretch happen, if your pediatrician agrees. HealthyChildren.org's formula-feeding Q&A says to wake a formula-fed baby in the first month if they sleep more than 4 to 5 hours, and that many formula-fed babies no longer need a middle-of-the-night feed by about 2 to 4 months or once they reach around 12 pounds; breastfed babies often need night feeds longer. Those two timings do not line up perfectly, which is why your baby's doctor, not a proverb, should set the alarm. Other reasons to wake: a jaundice or blood-sugar plan, a long day nap that is ruining nights, or (later) a schedule shift such as [daylight saving](/daylight-saving-baby-sleep).

## When you should wake a baby to feed

Growth comes first in the newborn period. A long, impressive stretch in a 6-day-old is not a milestone if it means they miss calories, wet diapers, or a bilirubin plan.

Typical situations — always confirm with your own pediatrician:

- They are not yet back to birth weight, or they were preterm or small.
- You have been told to feed around the clock for jaundice or low blood sugar.
- They are hard to rouse, unusually floppy, or not having enough wet diapers. That is a reason to wake *and* to call the doctor, not a reason to "let them sleep it off."
- In the first month, a formula-fed baby is still asleep at the 4- to 5-hour mark (HealthyChildren.org). For a breastfed baby, KidsHealth's 3- to 4-hour early-weeks rhythm is the usual starting point unless your clinic gave you a different interval.

How to wake them: unwrap, change the diaper, dim-but-adequate light, skin-to-skin, a gentle stroke. If you cannot wake them for a planned feed, that is a medical call, not a patience test.

Once weight gain is solid, many pediatricians will tell you it is okay to let a longer stretch happen at night and to offer a feed when the baby wakes. That is the beginning of a more natural night, not [night weaning](/night-weaning) as a project.

## When you might wake them from a nap

A short nap does not usually need to be ended early. A **very long daytime sleep** in a baby who then parties all night may be worth interrupting, especially if you are working on [day-night confusion](/newborn-day-night-confusion). There is no official "maximum nap length" from the AAP that we can quote as a rule, so we are not inventing one (such as "never more than two hours"). Use your pediatrician's feeding interval in the early weeks, and later use whether the long nap is pushing bedtime impossibly late.

Other practical reasons:

- You need to leave the house for an appointment, or a car trip should not start with a baby already at the end of their wake window.
- You are shifting the schedule for [daylight saving time](/daylight-saving-baby-sleep), in small steps.
- A late-afternoon nap is bumping into bedtime and causing [false starts](/false-starts).

Waking a sleeping baby to "practice" independent sleep, or to keep a rigid timetable at 3 weeks, is not what the sources above describe.

The daytime decision is covered in [should you wake a baby from a nap](/should-you-wake-a-baby-from-a-nap).

## When you should let them sleep

Once feeding and weight are on track, a baby who has fallen asleep in a **safe** place — their own firm, flat crib or bassinet, on their back — can usually be left. Do not wake them just because a relative said you should not "spoil" a nap, and do not wake them at night for a social reason.

Do wake-and-move them if they have fallen asleep in an unsafe place: a [car seat after you have arrived](/baby-sleeping-in-car-seat), a swing, a sofa, or in your arms when you are about to fall asleep on an armchair. In those cases you are not being cruel; you are moving them to [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask, specifically, "Do I still need to wake them at night, and at what interval?" after each early checkup. Call the same day if you cannot rouse them, if they are not feeding when you do wake them, or if wet diapers drop off. Night weaning decisions come later and belong on that separate page.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should you wake a sleeping baby?**  
Yes, when a feed, a medical plan, or an unsafe sleep place requires it. No, not as a general life rule once they are gaining well and sleeping safely.

**Should I wake my newborn to feed at night?**  
Usually, in the first weeks, until your pediatrician says weight gain is good enough to let a longer stretch go.

**Should I wake my baby from a nap?**  
Only for a reason: a missed feed, a day nap that is clearly wrecking nights, a schedule shift, or because they are sleeping in the wrong place.

**What if they never wake at night anymore at 8 weeks?**  
If weight, diapers, and the doctor's last advice are all fine, a long stretch can be a gift. Mention it at the next visit rather than starting to wake them again without cause.

**Does "never wake a sleeping baby" apply to twins?**  
The same feeding and safety rules apply to each baby. See [twins and sleep](/twins-and-sleep) for logistics; do not skip a needed feed because the other twin is still asleep.

**How long can a 6 week old sleep at night without eating?**
There is no official 6-week limit. In the first weeks, KidsHealth says to wake about every 3–4 hours until weight gain is going well; HealthyChildren.org's formula Q&A says to wake a formula-fed baby in the first month if they sleep more than 4–5 hours. By 6 weeks, many babies with good weight gain are allowed a longer stretch — but only if your pediatrician has already said so. The 6-week snapshot is on [6 week old baby sleep](/6-week-old-baby-sleep).

**Can I let a newborn sleep through the night?**
Not as a default in the first days. Use the wake-to-feed rules already on this page, then ask your pediatrician when a longer stretch is all right. "Through the night" at 1–3 months often means about 5–6 hours (KidsHealth), not 12 hours.

**Can a 6 month old (or 4, 8, 12 month old) sleep through the night without feeding?**
“Through the night” at 1–3 months often means about **5–6 hours** (KidsHealth), not a 12-hour stretch. Whether a later baby can go longer without a night feed is a weight-gain and feeding question for your pediatrician, not a search-result rule. Use this page, [night weaning](/night-weaning), and [should you wake a sleeping baby](/should-you-wake-a-sleeping-baby). Do not drop a needed night feed because a blog said “6 months.”

**Should I wake my baby to change a diaper at night?**
NHS advice on helping a baby to sleep: at night, do not change them unless they need it, and do not play. “Need it” means a soiled diaper or one that is soaking through, not every slightly damp one. If you do change them, keep the lights dim and the visit boring, then put them back on their back in their own space. This is not a reason to skip a night *feed* your pediatrician still wants you to offer.
