# 4 Month Sleep Regression: Signs, How Long It Lasts, and What Helps

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

The **4 month sleep regression** — also called the **four month sleep regression** or **4th month sleep regression** — is the name many parents give to a stretch when a baby who had started sleeping in longer stretches suddenly wakes more, naps less, or takes longer to settle. It is common, usually temporary, and it does not mean you have done something wrong.

## Short answer

Around 3 to 4 months, many babies move away from newborn-style sleep and toward more mature sleep cycles. That uneven shift is why some families see more night wakings and shorter naps — the classic **signs of 4 month sleep regression**. When it does happen, it often eases over a few days to a few weeks if you keep nights calm and consistent. From 4 to 12 months, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends **12 to 16 hours of sleep per 24 hours, including naps**, a range the AASM states is also endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).

## What is the 4 month sleep regression?

A sleep regression is when sleep that had been settling seems to go backward: more night wakings, shorter naps, or a baby who suddenly protests bedtime. The phrase **four month sleep regression** is popular because this is often the first time families feel that shift.

Sleep Foundation describes it as a developmental phase in which babies who were sleeping well may start waking more often and have more trouble settling. Cleveland Clinic notes that people often talk about a change around 4 months, but research has not shown regressions happening “like clockwork” at one age for every child. Not all babies have one (Sleep Foundation). Some barely notice a **4th month sleep regression**; others feel it a little earlier or later.

## Signs of 4 month sleep regression (and when it may not be a regression)

**Signs of 4 month sleep regression** often include, according to Sleep Foundation and Cleveland Clinic, more time to fall asleep or restlessness at bedtime; more frequent nighttime awakenings, sometimes with crying; shorter or fewer daytime naps; and more irritability after broken sleep.

**It may not be a regression.** Cleveland Clinic cautions parents not to assume every hard night is one. Check first for illness (fever, a cold, ear pain, or anything that does not feel right); hunger or more frequent feeding; a wet or dirty diaper; discomfort, including teething for some babies (timing varies — ask your pediatrician rather than assuming teething is the cause); a change in the sleep environment; or being too hot or too cold. If your baby has a fever, is feeding poorly, is not wetting diapers as usual, seems to be in pain, or you are uneasy, call your pediatrician.

## When does it start, and how long does the 4 month sleep regression last?

Timing varies by child (Cleveland Clinic). Sleep Foundation places the shift around 3 to 4 months, when sleep starts to consolidate, and notes that some babies struggle at this age, a little earlier, or a little later.

**How long does the 4 month sleep regression last?** Sleep Foundation: in most cases, a **few days to a few weeks**. Cleveland Clinic: **typically a week or two**. Children's Health (Dallas), on infant and toddler regressions in general: often **two to six weeks**.

NHS services also note that night waking often returns around 4–5 months and again around 8–9 months, and that many babies continue to wake at night for at least the first year. If the pattern lasts longer, or is getting worse, check in with your pediatrician.

## Why it happens: sleep cycles are maturing

Newborns sleep in shorter bursts around the clock. They are not born with a steady day–night body clock; that clock develops over time, as Newcastle Hospitals NHS explains. Babies also wake at the end of a sleep cycle and to be fed — brief night waking is normal.

Around 3 to 4 months, Sleep Foundation describes a major transition: sleep starts to **consolidate**, with longer nighttime stretches and naps that are beginning to organize. That shift is not always smooth. Factors can include an uneven move into more consolidated sleep, a greater awareness of surroundings, a disrupted sleep environment, and, for some babies, early separation protest. Cleveland Clinic, quoting Heidi Szugye, DO, IBCLC, also places this among first-year “ebbs and flows,” sometimes alongside growth spurts and new motor skills. Your baby is not “forgetting” how to sleep — their sleep system is being rebuilt.

## A sample 4 month old sleep schedule

**How much should a 4 month old sleep?** The AASM recommends **12 to 16 hours per 24 hours, including naps**, for infants **4 to 12 months**, and states that this consensus is endorsed by the AAP. Derbyshire Community Health Services NHS similarly says that from around 4 months, babies usually sleep **12–16 hours per day**. Sleep Foundation, citing that range, also describes a *typical pattern* (not a second official standard) of about **10 to 12 hours** at night, which may still be broken, plus **three to four daytime naps**.

**Example only — not a schedule your baby must follow**

| Time | What might happen | Sleep (example) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7:00 a.m. | Wake and feed | — |
| 9:00–10:00 a.m. | Nap 1 | 1 hour |
| 10:00 a.m.–12:15 p.m. | Feed and play | — |
| 12:15–1:30 p.m. | Nap 2 | 1 hour 15 minutes |
| 1:30–3:45 p.m. | Feed and play | — |
| 3:45–4:30 p.m. | Nap 3 | 45 minutes |
| 4:30–6:30 p.m. | Late-afternoon wake | — |
| 6:30 p.m. | Short bedtime routine | — |
| About 7:00 p.m.–7:00 a.m. | Night sleep, with brief wakings and needed feeds | About 10–12 hours, not necessarily uninterrupted |

This example is a mid-teens total inside the 12–16 hour recommendation (about 3 hours of naps plus 10–12 hours at night). A baby closer to 12 or to 16 hours can still be normal if they are growing, feeding well, and reasonably content when awake.

## 4 month wake windows

A **wake window** is the time your baby is awake between one sleep and the next. There is **no official AAP or AASM minute count** for a **4 month old wake window**. The best cited range we found is from Sleep Foundation, which groups **1 to 4 months** and gives a broad typical range of **1 to 3 hours**.

Treat **4 month wake windows** as a loose guide. Watch for yawning, eye rubbing, fussiness, staring off, or your baby's own “tell,” and start a short wind-down before your baby is frantic. During a regression, wake windows can temporarily get shorter or longer (Sleep Foundation). If a numbered window and your baby's cues disagree, follow the baby.

## How to help

Keep a simple, repeatable bedtime — a feed, a clean diaper, a dim room, a song or a book — at about the same time each evening. Cleveland Clinic and Sleep Foundation both emphasize consistency.

Protect day versus night: morning light, ordinary daytime sound, and outdoor time when you can, with nights kept dark and quiet (NHS). Avoid very long daytime naps in fully darkened rooms if they blur day and night (Newcastle Hospitals NHS).

When you can, put babies 4 months and older to bed drowsy, not already deeply asleep, so they can fall asleep in their own sleep space (HealthyChildren.org / AAP).

Respond before your baby becomes very distressed (Newcastle Hospitals NHS). Sleep Foundation suggests waiting a short moment, then offering calm reassurance in a dark, quiet room. You do not have to leave a young baby to cry for a long stretch.

Be gentle with yourself. Many babies still do not sleep through the night even at 12 months (Sleep Foundation). If you are considering formal sleep training, talk with your pediatrician first.

## What can make it worse

A few patterns can accidentally stretch a hard patch:

- Treating every waking as an emergency — or ignoring a baby who may be ill or hungry.
- A stimulating night: bright lights, phones, play, or a long, chatty feed.
- A sleep environment that keeps changing (Children's Health notes that changing where your baby sleeps can make disturbances worse).
- Always falling asleep in a way that cannot be repeated at 3 a.m. Children's Health and HealthyChildren.org caution that a baby who only ever falls asleep while feeding or being held may be upset when they wake alone. You can still comfort at night and gradually offer more chances to finish falling asleep in the crib or bassinet.
- Missing tired cues, or pushing the day too long.
- Very long, late, dark daytime naps that leave your baby under-tired at bedtime (Newcastle Hospitals NHS).
- Unsafe “fixes” such as extra blankets, pillows, an inclined sleeper, or staying swaddled after your baby is trying to roll.

## Safe sleep and when to talk to a pediatrician

Through the first year, follow AAP safe-sleep guidance, as explained on HealthyChildren.org:

- Place your baby **on their back for every sleep** until 1 year. If they can roll both ways and roll after being placed on their back, you need not keep turning them — keep the crib clear.
- Use a **firm, flat, non-inclined** surface that meets safety standards and does not indent under your baby. Surfaces that incline more than 10 degrees are not considered safe for infant sleep.
- Use a **fitted sheet only**. No loose bedding, pillows, quilts, stuffed toys, or bumper pads.
- **Room-share, don't bed-share,** for at least the first six months. HealthyChildren.org: room sharing can decrease the risk of SIDS by as much as 50% and is much safer than bed sharing.
- **Stop swaddling** when your baby is trying to roll. Do not use weighted swaddles or weighted blankets.
- Avoid overheating: about one more layer than you would wear, and no indoor hats after the hospital.

If your baby falls asleep in a car seat, stroller, swing, or carrier, move them to that firm, flat surface on their back as soon as you can.

**Talk to your pediatrician** if you are worried, if growth or feeding drops or wet diapers fall (Sleep Foundation), if breathing during sleep looks or sounds abnormal, if there is a fever or your baby is unusually hard to wake, or if sleep stays very disrupted for weeks (Cleveland Clinic).

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does the 4 month sleep regression last?**  
Usually a few days to a few weeks (Sleep Foundation), typically a week or two (Cleveland Clinic), or often two to six weeks for regressions in general (Children's Health).

**What are the signs of 4 month sleep regression?**  
More night wakings, shorter or skipped naps, taking longer to fall asleep, and a fussier, more tired baby. Consider illness, hunger, discomfort, and a dirty diaper first.

**How much should a 4 month old sleep?**  
AASM recommends 12 to 16 hours per 24 hours, including naps, for infants 4 to 12 months, a range it says is endorsed by the AAP.

**What are typical 4 month wake windows?**  
Sleep Foundation gives **1 to 3 hours** for babies **1 to 4 months**. Use that as a starting point for **4 month wake windows**.

**Do all babies go through a four month sleep regression?**  
No. Not all babies have one (Sleep Foundation), and research has not shown a clockwork change at one exact age (Cleveland Clinic).

**Is there a 16 week sleep regression?**
Sixteen weeks is 4 months. Use this page, alongside the 4-month schedule. How long it lasts is already discussed above; do not invent a week-16 duration from parent forums.
