# 2 Year Old Sleep Regression: Nap Strikes, Bedtime Stalling, and the Big-Kid Bed

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Two-year-olds can talk, climb, negotiate, and remember that you gave in last night. So when sleep goes wrong at this age, it often looks less like a baby waking for comfort and more like a small person with a plan. They skip the nap, ask for "one more" everything at bedtime, appear at your door at 5 a.m., or escape the crib. Here is what is really known about the "2 year old sleep regression" and what tends to help.

## Short answer

There is **no research showing a regression that arrives at the second birthday**. Sleep Foundation says sleep regressions can happen at any age. When sleep slips around age 2, common reasons include **testing limits, separation anxiety, fears and dreams, too much stimulation before bed, changes in the family's schedule, and teething**. Most 2-year-olds still need a nap. Fewer than 2.5% of children stop napping before age 2, so a nap strike is rarely the end of naps. Toddlers need about **11–14 hours of sleep in 24 hours** (AASM; KidsHealth). Most families get through this with a predictable routine, a clear and kind limit, and a well-timed bedtime.

## How strong is the evidence for a 2-year regression?

Weak for a fixed date. Reasonable for the idea that toddler development and routines affect sleep.

- **Sleep Foundation** describes a sleep regression as a setback that can occur at any age, sometimes around certain stages of development.
- **"Sleep regression"** is described as a popular term that is not used in the scientific literature (Keys, Public Health Ontario webinar slides).
- **App example (not clinical guidance):** Huckleberry calls the idea that a regression starts the moment a child turns 2 a myth. It says some toddlers sail through this year with few changes, while others have more ups and downs.
- **What research does support** is that toddler bedtime problems respond to behavioral approaches. An American Academy of Sleep Medicine task force reviewed 52 treatment studies. It found that 94% reported behavioral interventions worked, with over 80% of children showing clear improvement that lasted 3 to 6 months (Mindell et al., 2006).

## Signs and likely causes

Signs parents describe include nap refusal, long bedtime stalling, calling out or leaving the room after lights-out, new night waking, and very early mornings.

| What might be behind it | What it can look like | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independence and testing limits | Saying no to nap or bed, negotiating | Sleep Foundation |
| Separation anxiety | Clinginess at lights-out, calling for a parent | Sleep Foundation |
| Not yet able to resettle alone | Needing a parent after every night waking | Sleep Foundation |
| Nightmares or dreams | Waking upset, fear of the dark | Sleep Foundation |
| Too much stimulation before bed | Wound up and "not tired" at bedtime | Sleep Foundation; KidsHealth NZ |
| Family schedule changes | Travel, new childcare, a new sibling | Sleep Foundation |
| Teething | Discomfort and night waking | Sleep Foundation |
| Potty training, moving to a bed too early | Extra toilet requests, leaving the bed | Huckleberry (app example) |

## When your 2 year old refuses to nap

A string of skipped naps does not usually mean your child has outgrown them. A meta-analysis by Staton and colleagues found that fewer than 2.5% of children stop napping before their second birthday. Sleep Foundation adds that about half of children stop napping by age 3, and that refusing a nap doesn't necessarily mean a child is ready to give it up.

What helps:

- **Keep offering the nap at the same time each day.** Sleep Foundation recommends a regular nap time, bedtime, and wake time, with a short calming pre-nap routine.
- **Check the timing.** Sleep Foundation suggests the nap end at least 3 to 4.5 hours before bedtime. A nap that runs too late can push bedtime back.
- **Make it quiet time if sleep doesn't come.** Huckleberry (app example) suggests that even if your child doesn't sleep, quiet rest in their room is worth keeping.
- **Don't force it if the nap is truly gone.** Sleep Foundation says that once a child has clearly given up napping, forcing a nap can disrupt night sleep.

For daycare kids, Sleep Foundation quotes pediatrician Nilong Vyas: if your child's nap is short at daycare and they seem sleepy on weekends, a weekend nap of 1.5 to 3 hours is fine.

## Bedtime stalling and calling out

Bedtime stalling is one of the most common complaints at this age. KidsHealth New Zealand suggests:

- turning off TVs, tablets, and computers at least an hour before bed;
- avoiding lively play just before bed;
- a calming routine in the same order every night, even on weekends;
- checking your child has everything they need before you leave, and gently reminding them to stay in bed.

Two ideas that can take the fight out of bedtime:

- **Offer small choices.** KidsHealth's 30-month checkup guide suggests giving toddlers a sense of independence by offering a choice between two acceptable options, such as which pajamas or which book. More than two can be overwhelming.
- **Watch the clock, not just the child.** In app data from 841 children, later bedtimes went with shorter night sleep at every age (Mindell et al., 2016). Huckleberry (app example) adds the opposite risk: a bedtime set too soon after a late nap can meet resistance because the child isn't tired yet.

## Early morning waking

KidsHealth New Zealand notes that some toddlers wake early and need an earlier bedtime. It also explains that children sleep most deeply in the first hours of the night and are more likely to wake in the second half, when sleep is lighter. Keep early-morning contact quiet and dim until your chosen wake-up time *(practical suggestion)*.

## Moving from crib to bed

Crib-to-bed questions peak around age 2, and a move at the wrong moment is a common trigger for new sleep problems.

**Signs it may be time** (Cleveland Clinic, citing the AAP for the size limits):

- your child keeps climbing out, even with the mattress at its lowest;
- they are taller than 35 inches, or the rail is at about mid-chest when they stand;
- they can fall asleep on their own and follow other house rules.

HealthyChildren.org (AAP) also says that once a toddler can climb out of the crib, it may be time for a bed.

**Signs it may be too soon** (Cleveland Clinic): your child is still content in the crib, isn't sleeping through the night, or is having a hard time with limits. A sleep sack can make climbing harder for a while, though some children get out of those too.

**How to make the switch:**

1. **Talk about it ahead of time** and explain what you expect, including the morning routine (Cleveland Clinic).
2. **Let your child choose something,** such as the sheets, a night light, or a small comfort item (Cleveland Clinic).
3. **Start with naps** in the new bed, if you can (Cleveland Clinic).
4. **Keep the bedtime routine exactly the same** (HealthyChildren.org; Cleveland Clinic).
5. **Make one big change at a time.** Avoid moving to a bed during potty training, a house move, a new daycare, or a vacation (Cleveland Clinic).
6. **Walk them back, calmly, every time.** HealthyChildren.org suggests telling your child to stay in bed until you come for them. If they get up, lead them back quietly, briefly praise them for being in bed, and leave. It warns that 20 returns in an evening is not unusual at first.
7. **Praise or reward the morning after.** Cleveland Clinic suggests a sticker chart with small rewards.

**Make the room safe.** HealthyChildren.org advises clearing away furniture and large toys that could hurt a child who climbs or falls, anchoring dressers and other furniture so they can't tip, and gating the top of the stairs. A gate at the bedroom door may be needed. Cleveland Clinic adds checking cords, unlocked windows, outlets, and access to the kitchen, bathroom, garage, or outside.

## What can make it worse

- **Dropping the nap at the first refusal.**
- **Giving in to bedroom "breakouts."** HealthyChildren.org advises against rewarding them, for example by letting your child into your bed or back with the family.
- **Changing the rules night to night,** or stacking several big changes at once.
- **Screens and rough play** in the hour before bed.
- **Switching to a bed too early** for a child who isn't ready.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Talk to your child's doctor if sleep problems last more than a few weeks or are getting worse, if your child snores loudly, gasps, or seems to stop breathing during sleep, if nightmares are frequent, if sleep problems come with pain, fever, or other signs of illness, or if daytime behavior is changing a lot. Also reach out if you are exhausted or struggling.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the 2 year old sleep regression real?**  
Sleep problems around age 2 are common, but there is no evidence for a regression that every child goes through at a set age. Sleep Foundation says regressions can happen at any age.

**How long does the 2 year sleep regression last?**  
There is no study-based answer. Huckleberry (app example) says some regressions pass in a week or two and nap strikes often last a couple of weeks. Longer stretches usually point to a habit or schedule that needs adjusting.

**My 2 year old won't nap. Should I drop the nap?**  
Usually not yet. Fewer than 2.5% of children stop napping before age 2 (Staton et al., 2020), and Sleep Foundation says about half of children have stopped napping by age 3. Keep offering the nap or quiet time.

**When should my toddler move from a crib to a bed?**  
When they can climb out, are taller than 35 inches, or the rail is at mid-chest (AAP, via Cleveland Clinic). Otherwise there's no rush. Many children move between 2 and 3 years.

**How much sleep does a 2 year old need?**  
About 11–14 hours in 24 hours, including a nap (AASM; KidsHealth).
