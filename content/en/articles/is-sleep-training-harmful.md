# Is Sleep Training Harmful? What the Trials Show — and What They Don't

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

This page answers a single question: **does research show that behavioral sleep training harms babies?** It is not [sleep training methods explained](/sleep-training-methods-explained), not [Ferber method](/ferber-method-baby-sleep), not [cry it out](/cry-it-out-method), not [gentle sleep training](/gentle-sleep-training), and not [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training). We will not paste methods or check-in schedules here.

## Short answer

The best-known randomized trials in this area did **not** find lasting harm to attachment, emotional health, or the parent–child relationship in the groups they studied. That is not the same as "every method, at every age, in every family, is proven safe," and it is not a reason to start formal training in the newborn weeks.

A 2016 *Pediatrics* randomized trial (Gradisar and colleagues) compared graduated extinction and bedtime fading with a control group in infants with sleep problems. In that trial, the behavioral groups slept better, and the authors did not find increased infant stress (including cortisol measures in the study), parent stress, or attachment differences at 12 months. A five-year follow-up of an earlier Australian randomized trial (Price and colleagues, *Pediatrics*) did not find lasting harm to child mental health, the parent–child relationship, or maternal well-being in that cohort.

Those papers have limits: ages were typically in a **6–16 month** band, not newborns; sample sizes are finite; families who enroll in a trial are not every family. We report the findings. We do not treat them as a blank cheque.

Sleep Foundation generally discusses formal training readiness around **4–6 months**. AAP safe sleep — back, firm, flat, clear, room-sharing for at least the first 6 months — still applies during any plan. This page does not tell you which method to choose.

### What the cited trials do and do not show

| Claim you might see | What the cited research actually supports |
| --- | --- |
| "Sleep training always damages attachment" | Not supported by the Gradisar 2016 RCT or the Price five-year follow-up in those cohorts |
| "Sleep training is proven harmless for every baby" | Too broad. Trials had age ranges, inclusion rules, and follow-up limits |
| "You should start in the first weeks" | Not what these trials studied. Readiness is discussed later (often 4–6 months) |
| "Crying for a minute is the same as a trial protocol" | This page will not turn a headline into a DIY protocol |

## What "harm" is being asked about

Parents usually mean attachment, stress hormones, or later behaviour. The trials above measured some of those things and did not find the feared lasting differences in their samples. Other researchers and some parent organisations remain cautious, especially about leaving a young baby to cry without a plan that a clinician has agreed is appropriate. That caution is part of the picture, not something this page should erase.

If you want *how* a method is done, use the methods pages — or, if you do not want a formal method, [responsive settling](/responsive-settling) or [gentle sleep training](/gentle-sleep-training). If the question is only "is it too early," use [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training).

## When a trial result is the wrong tool

Do not use a 12-month follow-up paper to decide what to do for a two-week-old, a sick baby, a baby with poor weight gain, or a family in crisis. Do not use it to ignore safe-sleep rules or a pediatrician's advice. If nights are unlivable, that is also a reason to talk to a clinician or a qualified local service — sleep deprivation in parents is a real safety issue, and it is not a moral test.

## Who to call, and what this page cannot do

Ask your pediatrician before you start a formal plan if the baby was premature, has a medical condition, is not gaining well, or you are not sure they are ready. If you are struggling with your own mood or with unsafe exhaustion, ask for help for you as well. This article cannot tell you that a method is right for your baby.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is sleep training bad for attachment?**  
The Gradisar 2016 trial did not find attachment differences at 12 months in that sample. That is one good trial, not a law of nature. See also the Price five-year follow-up for later outcomes in its cohort.

**Is sleep training harmful according to the NHS?**  
UK advice varies by source and is often cautious about leaving very young babies to cry. We will not invent a single NHS verdict. Ask your midwife, health visitor, or GP, and use the trial evidence above as evidence, not as a substitute for local care.

**Can I sleep-train a 3 month old because a trial said it is safe?**  
Those trials were not a licence to start at any age. Readiness is generally discussed later. See [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training).

**Does "no lasting harm" mean I have to use cry-it-out?**  
No. This page is not a method recommendation. Several approaches exist; choose with your clinician and your own threshold.

**What about melatonin or medicine instead?**  
Not a substitute, and not dosed on this page. See [melatonin and children's sleep](/melatonin-and-childrens-sleep).
