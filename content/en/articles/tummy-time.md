# Tummy Time by Age: When to Start, How Long, and How to Make It Easier

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's development, head shape, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"Tummy time" (some parents say belly time) simply means your baby spends a few awake, supervised minutes lying on their front. It sounds trivial, yet it is one of the first exercises a baby gets, and it is the daytime partner of the safe-sleep rule that babies sleep on their backs. Below: when to begin, how many minutes are reasonable at each age, which positions help a newborn who protests, and when to mention something to your pediatrician.

## Short answer

You can start **from the first days at home**. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) suggests **2–3 short sessions a day of 3–5 minutes**, building up to **15–30 minutes in total per day by about 7 weeks**. The NIH Safe to Sleep campaign gives a similar target of 15–30 minutes a day by about 2 months, and the World Health Organization (WHO) says babies who can't yet move around should get **at least 30 minutes a day**, spread out. After that, more floor time is generally better. Tummy time is **only for an awake baby with an adult watching**; if your baby dozes off, turn them onto their back.

### Key numbers at a glance

| Question | What official sources say | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When to start | From the day your baby comes home (NIH: a day or two after birth) | AAP (HealthyChildren.org); NIH Safe to Sleep |
| First sessions | 2–3 times a day, 3–5 minutes each | AAP; NIH Safe to Sleep |
| By about 7 weeks–2 months | 15–30 minutes total per day | AAP; NIH Safe to Sleep |
| Babies not yet mobile | At least 30 minutes a day on the tummy, spread through the day | WHO |
| By about 3 months | About 1 hour a day (KidsHealth); about 30 minutes (Cleveland Clinic) | Sources differ |
| After 6 months | Work up to 60–90 minutes a day until crawling | Cleveland Clinic |
| Sleep position | Always on the back, even for naps | AAP |

## What tummy time does for a baby

On their front, a baby has to lift their head against gravity. That work strengthens the neck, shoulders, arms, and upper back, which later helps with rolling, sitting, and crawling. The NIH also lists a second benefit: less pressure on the back of the head, so fewer flat spots.

The research is mostly observational. A 2020 systematic review in *Pediatrics* (16 studies, 4,237 infants) linked tummy time with better gross motor development, better ability to move on the tummy and back, rolling and crawling, and less brachycephaly (a wide, flat back of the head). Links with sitting, standing, walking, and thinking skills were unclear, and most studies were not randomized trials. So tummy time is worth doing, but it is not a shortcut to early walking.

## When to start tummy time

The AAP says you can start the day your baby comes home from the hospital. KidsHealth suggests starting a newborn on your chest or across your lap for a few minutes, two or three times a day. Cleveland Clinic advises checking the details with your pediatrician first if your baby was **born preterm, has reflux, or has a physical disability**.

## How much tummy time by age

Sources agree on how to start and roughly on the 2-month target. After that they differ, so we show the range rather than a single "correct" number.

| Age | Reasonable daily total | What it may look like | Where the number comes from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Newborn (first weeks) | A few short sessions of 3–5 minutes, 2–3 times a day | On your chest, across your lap, or on a firm floor mat | AAP; NIH; KidsHealth |
| 1 month | Building toward 20 minutes or more | Brief head lifts; head turned to one side | Cleveland Clinic (0–2 months: work up to 20+ minutes) |
| 2 months | 15–30 minutes total | Longer head lifts; resting on forearms | AAP (by 7 weeks); NIH; Cleveland Clinic |
| 3 months | About 30 minutes, up to about 1 hour | Chest coming up; looking around | Cleveland Clinic (30 min); KidsHealth (about 1 hour); WHO (at least 30 min) |
| 4–6 months | More floor play, much of it baby-led | Pushing up on straight arms, reaching, rolling | CDC milestones; Cleveland Clinic |
| 6 months until crawling | Work up to 60–90 minutes a day | Pivoting, reaching, getting ready to crawl | Cleveland Clinic |

A simple rule of thumb from a Cleveland Clinic physical therapist: about **10 minutes a day for each month of age**, so close to 30 minutes at 3 months.

**App examples (not clinical guidance).** Huckleberry publishes its own tummy time chart, and its two articles do not quite match each other: one suggests 30–60 minutes a day at 3 months and 60+ minutes at 4–5 months, the other 60 minutes at 3 months and 60–90 minutes at 4–5 months. That shows how loose these targets are. Total minutes matter less than doing a little, often.

## How to do tummy time step by step

1. **Pick the moment.** The AAP suggests after a diaper change or when your baby wakes from a nap. A baby who is overtired or very hungry will protest sooner *(practical suggestion)*.
2. **Use a low, firm surface.** A clean floor with a blanket or mat is fine. KidsHealth warns against sofas and adult beds, where a baby can roll off or sink into soft bedding.
3. **Help a very young baby.** Cleveland Clinic suggests a small rolled towel under the chest so the face doesn't end up flat on the surface and it is easier to lift the head.
4. **Give your baby something to look at.** Get down to eye level, or put a toy or baby-safe mirror in front of them. Cleveland Clinic notes that babies under 4 months see best at about 12–18 inches (30–45 cm).
5. **Stop before it becomes misery.** When your baby is clearly upset, pick them up or roll them to their back or side, and try again later.
6. **Never step away.** A young baby who slips face-down may not be able to lift their head to breathe, Cleveland Clinic warns.

## Tummy time positions for newborns

- **Chest to chest.** Recline and lay your baby on your chest. Cleveland Clinic explains that the more upright a baby is, the easier it is to lift the head, so this is a good starting point if flat floor time is too hard.
- **Across your lap.** Lay your baby face-down across your thighs and gently rub their back. KidsHealth uses a lap position for exercises when babies have neck tightness.
- **On the floor with a towel roll.** The most "classic" version and, according to Cleveland Clinic's physical therapist, the most effective once tolerated.
- **Side-lying play.** Not tummy time, but a useful partner in the first months: lying on one side with support behind the back lets a baby bring hands together and swipe at toys, and changes pressure on the head (Cleveland Clinic). Give both sides equal time.

## What if my baby hates tummy time?

Very common, and usually temporary. Ideas from AAP, Cleveland Clinic, and KidsHealth:

- **Shorter, more frequent sessions.** One minute five times a day beats one tearful 5-minute block.
- **Start more upright** (on your chest) and slowly work toward flat.
- **Add a face.** Lie down opposite your baby, sing, or let an older sibling play with them while you watch (AAP).
- **Move toys.** Put toys just out of reach or in a circle around your baby so they turn and reach (AAP).
- **Change the scenery.** A different room, mat, or mirror.

If your baby screams within seconds every time, can't lift their head at all, or always turns to the same side, mention it at the next visit. Some babies benefit from a referral to a pediatric physical therapist (Cleveland Clinic).

## Tummy time, feeding, and reflux

Many parents find tummy time on a very full stomach leads to spit-up. Huckleberry (app example) suggests waiting 20–30 minutes after a feed; we did not find this exact interval in AAP or NIH guidance, so treat it as a practical tip. If your baby has diagnosed reflux, ask your pediatrician how to adapt tummy time.

## Tummy time and flat spots on the head

Back sleeping is non-negotiable, but it can lead to a flat area on the back of the head. The AAP says these flat spots usually round out as babies grow and sit up, and suggests:

- switching which end of the crib your baby's feet point to (NIH suggests changing it week to week);
- changing positions often while your baby is awake;
- limiting time in swings, bouncers, and car seats outside the car;
- switching sides when bottle-feeding.

KidsHealth adds that tummy time is helpful for babies with **torticollis** (tight neck muscles that make it hard to turn the head) together with the exercises your doctor shows you.

## What Caring for Kids adds (and one number that does not match the table above)

Canadian Paediatric Society’s Caring for Kids page on preventing flat heads in babies who sleep on their backs agrees with the section above: a baby’s skull is soft, a preferred head turn can leave a flat spot (**positional plagiocephaly**), and **the answer is not to stop back-sleeping**. A little flattening often improves on its own. More obvious flattening may not disappear completely; Caring for Kids says it **will not affect a baby’s brain or development**. If a flat spot is still there, talk to the pediatrician rather than starting tummy sleep at night.

Caring for Kids also gives a practical crib trick already in the AAP/NIH advice above: alternate which end of the crib the baby’s head points to, so the interesting view (the room, a parent coming in) still draws the face the other way. They mention a mobile on the room-facing side for the same reason; keep it **well out of reach**, and never add loose toys to the sleep space (see [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies)).

On **how much** awake tummy time, Caring for Kids uses a single early figure: **10 to 15 minutes, at least three times a day**. That is not the same as the age-by-age range in the table earlier on this page (AAP, NIH, KidsHealth, Cleveland Clinic, WHO). We show both. We do not average them into one official daily total. Supervised floor time, started a little and often, matters more than winning a stopwatch argument. Sleep still starts on the back; see [baby sleeping on the stomach](/baby-sleeping-on-stomach).

## What tummy time is not

- **Not a sleep position.** Babies should sleep on their backs for every nap and every night (AAP). If your baby falls asleep during tummy time, roll them onto their back; Cleveland Clinic says they can stay on the floor if you keep watching.
- **Not a reason to keep a baby in "containers".** CDC suggests not keeping babies in swings, strollers, or bouncy seats for too long, and WHO advises that babies are not restrained for more than 1 hour at a time.
- **Not a race.** Crawling timing varies widely; Cleveland Clinic notes most babies crawl between about 7 and 10 months, and later can be normal. CDC's checklists up to 1 year do not list crawling as a milestone.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask your pediatrician if:

- your baby always holds or turns their head to one side, or you notice a flat spot that is getting worse;
- by 4 months your baby cannot hold their head steady when you hold them, or doesn't push up onto elbows or forearms on their tummy (both are on CDC's 4-month checklist);
- by 6 months your baby doesn't roll from tummy to back or push up with straight arms (CDC 6-month checklist);
- your baby was born early, has reflux, or has a medical condition that makes tummy time hard;
- your baby has lost a skill they used to have.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can I start tummy time with a newborn?**  
From the first days at home. Start on your chest or across your lap for a few minutes, two or three times a day.

**How long should tummy time be at 1, 2, and 3 months?**  
Start with 3–5 minute sessions. Aim for about 15–30 minutes a day in total by around 2 months and at least 30 minutes by 3 months; some sources suggest up to about an hour.

**Does tummy time on my chest count?**  
Yes. AAP and Cleveland Clinic both describe chest-to-chest as a valid way to start, especially for newborns.

**What if my baby falls asleep during tummy time?**  
Gently turn them onto their back. Babies should always sleep on their backs.

**When can I stop tummy time?**  
There is no official end date. Once your baby rolls, sits, and crawls, floor play replaces planned sessions; Cleveland Clinic suggests 60–90 minutes a day after 6 months until crawling.

**Is it OK if my baby cries during tummy time?**  
Brief fussing is common. End the session when your baby is truly upset and try again later in a shorter session.
