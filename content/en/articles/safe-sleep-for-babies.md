# Safe Sleep for Babies: How to Lower the Risk of SIDS

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Every year, about **3,500 babies in the United States die suddenly and unexpectedly while they are sleeping**, according to HealthyChildren.org, the American Academy of Pediatrics' parent site. Most of those deaths are from sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) or from accidental suffocation or strangulation. There is no guaranteed way to prevent SIDS. There is, however, a well-studied set of habits — often called **safe sleep** — that lowers the risk. The rules below come from the AAP's 2022 policy, with NHS and Lullaby Trust wording added where they differ.

## Short answer

For every sleep, day and night, until your baby's first birthday: place your baby **on their back**, on a **firm, flat** crib, bassinet, or play yard mattress, with **only a fitted sheet**. Share a **room**, not a bed, for **at least the first 6 months**. Keep the crib clear of pillows, loose blankets, bumpers, and toys. Dress your baby in about **one more layer** than you would wear, and skip hats indoors. Offer a pacifier at sleep time if your baby will take one (wait until breastfeeding is going well). Do not use weighted swaddles or blankets, incline sleepers, or positioners.

## The ABCs: back, bare crib, in your room

A useful shorthand, used by many public-health campaigns, is **ABC**: Alone, on the Back, in a bare Crib. "Alone" here means on a separate sleep surface, not that the baby should be in another room. The AAP, NHS, and Lullaby Trust all want the baby close to you, just not in your bed.

## Always start on the back — including for babies with reflux

Babies who sleep on their backs are much less likely to die suddenly than babies placed on their stomachs or sides. The AAP is clear that the **side position is just as dangerous as the stomach**, in part because a baby can roll onto their tummy more easily from the side. HealthyChildren.org also states that healthy babies, and even most babies with gastroesophageal reflux (GERD), will not choke on their backs: their airway anatomy and gag reflex protect them. They should sleep **flat** on their backs, not on a wedge or incline.

Two common follow-up questions:

- **What if my baby rolls onto their tummy?** Keep placing them on their back to start. Once they can roll comfortably both ways (back to tummy and tummy to back), you do not need to keep flipping them. You do need a clear crib, so they cannot roll into a blanket or pillow. KidsHealth and the AAP agree on this point.
- **What about preemies?** A premature baby may sleep on their stomach temporarily in the NICU. Once they are medically stable, they should be placed on their backs so they practice that position before going home.

Supervised **tummy time** while the baby is awake is still important for development and to protect the shape of the head. It is not a sleep position. See our [tummy time](/tummy-time) guide.

## A firm, flat, approved sleep surface

"Firm" means the mattress should not indent when the baby is lying on it. Use a crib, bassinet, portable crib, or play yard that meets U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) standards, with a mattress made for that product and a tight fitted sheet. Check the CPSC website for recalls, especially if the crib is not new. Do not use a crib that is missing hardware, is broken, or has no instructions.

The 2022 AAP update is strict about products that look like sleep spaces but are not: if a product does not meet federal safety standards for cribs, bassinets, portable cribs, or play yards, do not use it for sleep. That includes many inclined sleepers, hammocks, in-bed sleepers, nests, pods, and compact "travel" products that have not been certified. Feeding pillows and infant loungers (HealthyChildren.org and KidsHealth name examples such as Boppy-style pillows and Dock-a-Tot-style loungers) are not sleep products. If your baby falls asleep in a car seat, stroller, swing, or sling, move them to a firm, flat surface on their back as soon as you can.

A bedside sleeper, Moses basket, or play yard can count only if it is a product intended for infant sleep, meets current standards, and is used with a firm, flat surface and a tight fitted sheet — details on [when to move baby to a crib](/when-to-move-baby-to-crib). In-bed sleepers, nests, and docks stay in the “do not use” group already on this page.

## Crib standards, mattress fit, and recalls

The paragraph above already says to use a product that meets U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) standards and to check for recalls. This section is the checklist those sentences point to.

- **Date and drop-sides.** HealthyChildren.org's "New Crib Standards" page explains the federal update that took effect on **28 June 2011**: manufacture and sale of **drop-side** cribs were prohibited, and new cribs had to meet tougher rules on slats, mattress support, hardware, and testing. HealthyChildren's room-safety checklist (updated 14 August 2020) says to use a crib manufactured **after June 2011**. An older drop-side crib is not a bargain. The same 2011 rules also apply to resale, including garage sales and donations (HealthyChildren.org). If you already have a recalled drop-side crib, stop using it; CPSC and the AAP have long pointed families to the recall list, not to a homemade repair.
- **Slats and posts.** CPSC crib-safety tips, and HealthyChildren's checklist, both say slats should be **no more than 2 3/8 inches** (about 6 cm, or the width of a soda can) apart, with no missing or cracked slats. CPSC says corner posts should be **no more than 1/16 inch** high, so clothing cannot catch; there should be no cutouts in the headboard or footboard that could trap a head. HealthyChildren.org agrees on flush, very low posts for ordinary cribs, and flags decorative cutouts the same way.
- **Mattress fit.** The mattress should be made for that crib and sit snugly. HealthyChildren.org's test: if you can insert **more than two adult fingers** between the mattress and the sides or ends of the crib, do not use that crib-and-mattress combination. CPSC's wording is the same idea: a firm, tight-fitting mattress so a baby cannot become trapped in a gap.
- **Hardware, every week.** Missing, loose, or broken screws, brackets, or mattress-support parts are a reason to stop using the crib (CPSC; HealthyChildren.org). Use the manufacturer's parts, not a substitute from a hardware drawer.
- **Where to check recalls.** Search the crib (and any second-hand bassinet or play yard) at [SaferProducts.gov](https://www.saferproducts.gov/) and consider signing up for CPSC recall notices at [CPSC.gov](https://www.cpsc.gov/). The existing safe-sleep paragraph already tells you to do this, especially if the crib is not new.

A mesh play yard is a different product: CPSC's crib tips say the mesh should be less than 1/4 inch, with no tears or loose threads, and securely attached. It still has to be a product intended for sleep, used with only a tight fitted sheet.

Keep the crib **bare** — that rule is already in "Keep the crib bare" below. This section does not add bumpers, pillows, or blankets back in.

## Share a room, not a bed, for at least 6 months

This is one place the wording changed in 2022. The AAP now recommends that parents **avoid bed-sharing under any circumstances**, including with twins, and that the baby sleep in the parents' room, close to the bed but on a separate surface, **for at least the first 6 months**. Older AAP language said "ideally the first year." NHS and Lullaby Trust advice matches the six-month minimum.

HealthyChildren.org says room sharing can decrease SIDS risk by as much as 50% and is much safer than bed sharing. It also makes night feeds easier.

If you bring your baby into your bed to feed or comfort them, the AAP asks you to put them back in their own space before you go to sleep. If there is any chance you might doze off, first remove pillows, loose blankets, and anything that could cover the baby's face. Move the baby back as soon as you wake. **Sofas, armchairs, and cushions are especially dangerous** — the AAP cites a risk of sleep-related death up to 67 times higher when a baby sleeps with someone on a couch or soft chair.

The risk of bed-sharing is even higher when:

- someone has had alcohol, marijuana, medicines, or drugs that make it harder to wake (more than 10 times higher, per HealthyChildren.org)
- a parent smokes or vapes, or smoked during pregnancy
- the baby is younger than 4 months, was born preterm, or had a low birth weight (the AAP gives a 5-to-10-times higher risk under 4 months)

The short version on bed-sharing is the AAP's: we are not able to recommend bed-sharing, even though many families do it for breastfeeding or cultural reasons.

## Keep the crib bare

Soft objects and loose bedding raise the risk of entrapment, suffocation, and strangulation. That means no pillows, pillow-like toys, quilts, comforters, mattress toppers, non-fitted sheets, loose blankets, stuffed toys, or bumper pads. If you are worried about warmth, dress your baby in layers or use a wearable blanket (a [sleep sack](/sleep-sacks)). In general, dress them in **only one layer more** than you are wearing, and do not put a hat on them indoors once you are home from the hospital. Check for sweating, a hot chest, or flushed skin — those are signs of overheating, which is linked to a higher SIDS risk.

**Do not use weighted blankets, weighted sleepers, or weighted swaddles** on or near your baby. The AAP and KidsHealth both say this clearly.

How to wrap a newborn, and when to stop, is covered in [how to swaddle a baby](/how-to-swaddle-a-baby). Clothing and room temperature — including a disagreement between U.S. and UK numbers — are in [how to dress a baby for sleep](/how-to-dress-baby-for-sleep).

## Other steps that lower risk

A safe sleep space is the core. These additional steps also have evidence behind them:

- **Breast milk**, if you can and choose to. The AAP says human milk reduces SIDS risk, and the longer it is given the more protection it offers. Exclusive breastfeeding for about 6 months is the nutritional recommendation; any amount still helps. Families who formula-feed should still follow every other item on this list. Lullaby Trust makes the same point.
- **A pacifier at nap time and bedtime.** Pacifiers are associated with a lower SIDS risk, even if they fall out after the baby is asleep. You do not have to put it back. Do not force it, and do not clip it to clothing or tie it around the neck. If you are breastfeeding, wait until breastfeeding is well established (often around 3 to 4 weeks). Details are in [pacifier and sleep](/pacifier-and-sleep).
- **Smoke-free pregnancy and home.** Smoking in pregnancy and secondhand smoke after birth are major SIDS risk factors. This includes vaping and e-cigarettes, which contain nicotine. Keep the car and home smoke-free. If you smoke, it is especially important not to bed-share.
- **No alcohol or drugs** in pregnancy or after birth, in the sense the AAP means: alcohol, marijuana, opioids, or illicit drugs raise risk, and they make bed-sharing far more dangerous.
- **Prenatal care and well-child visits**, including routine immunizations. KidsHealth and NHS both note that vaccines are associated with a lower SIDS risk; they also protect against illnesses that can make a baby more vulnerable.
- **No gadgets that claim to prevent SIDS.** Home apnea monitors and "SIDS-prevention" positioners have not been shown to do that job, and positioners add a suffocation risk. The AAP and KidsHealth advise against them.

## Home apnea monitors and consumer “SIDS monitors”

Consumer gadgets that claim to watch a baby’s breathing or to “prevent SIDS” are not a substitute for the safer-sleep steps already on this page. HealthyChildren.org’s page on home apnea monitors is direct: **research has not shown a clear link between ordinary apnea and SIDS**, and **home apnea monitors give little or no protection from SIDS**. Brief pauses in a newborn’s breathing can be normal (see [newborn grunting in sleep](/newborn-grunting-in-sleep) for periodic breathing) and are not, by themselves, a SIDS warning. The same HealthyChildren.org page notes that these monitors also produce many **false alarms**, which can add fear and lost sleep.

The American Academy of Pediatrics’ 2022 safe-sleep policy, already the backbone of this article, **does not recommend** using commercial cardiorespiratory monitors as a strategy to reduce the risk of SIDS. Devices marketed to reduce SIDS are not a safer-sleep plan. Lullaby Trust and Red Nose similarly caution families not to treat a gadget as protection.

In **rare** cases a doctor may prescribe a home monitor — HealthyChildren.org names examples such as a baby who needs **home oxygen** or who has **serious breathing problems**. If that happens, use the device as prescribed and still keep the crib rules on this page. Do not buy a consumer sock, camera, or pad in place of back-sleeping, a firm flat surface, a clear crib, and room-sharing.

## Vaccinations do not cause SIDS

They do not. The Lullaby Trust, Red Nose, and the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Vaccine Education Center all state that **immunizations do not cause SIDS**. The Lullaby Trust goes further, in line with the research those organizations cite: babies who are **up to date with their vaccinations have a lower risk** of SIDS, not a higher one. Vaccinating on the recommended schedule is part of protecting a baby from serious illness; it is not a SIDS risk.

If a baby is sleepy or a little feverish after a vaccine, dress them for the room and follow [sick baby sleep](/sick-baby-sleep) — do not add extra blankets. For the monitors-and-gadgets question, see the section above.

## UK advice that lines up — and one number that does not

NHS and Lullaby Trust advice is very close to the AAP's: back to sleep, clear cot, firm flat mattress, same room for at least 6 months, smoke-free, avoid sofas, consider a dummy (pacifier) after breastfeeding is established, keep up with vaccinations. The NHS also describes a **feet-to-foot** position (baby's feet at the bottom of the cot) so the baby cannot wriggle under the bedding, which is a useful extra if you use light, tucked bedding as UK guidance allows.

The clear disagreement is **room temperature**. NHS and Lullaby Trust ask you to keep the room at **16–20°C** (about 61–68°F). A HealthyChildren.org page on hours of sleep mentions **68–72°F** (20–22°C). Sleep Foundation cites studies in that 68–72°F band but also notes that the 2022 AAP policy itself does not set a temperature — it tells you to dress for the room and avoid overheating. We show all three in [how to dress a baby for sleep](/how-to-dress-baby-for-sleep) rather than inventing a single "correct" number.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Call if you are unsure whether a product is safe for sleep, if your baby has a medical condition that you have been told might affect sleep position, or if you are struggling to keep your baby on a flat, back-sleeping surface (including after a cold or with reflux). If your baby has been in an unsafe sleep situation and you are worried about their breathing, seek urgent care. For grief after a sudden infant death, organizations such as First Candle (cited by KidsHealth) can help families find support.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "safe sleep" mean?**  
A set of habits that lower the risk of SIDS and accidental suffocation: back sleeping on a firm, flat, bare surface, in the same room as a caregiver, with no loose bedding, smoke, or overheating.

**Can my baby sleep on their side?**  
No, not as a starting position. The AAP treats the side as just as risky as the stomach for babies who cannot yet roll both ways.

**Is it OK to use a blanket if I tuck it in tightly?**  
The AAP prefers no loose bedding in the first year. Use layers or a wearable blanket instead. UK sources discuss light, tucked bedding and a feet-to-foot position; if you follow that tradition, keep the cot otherwise clear and the covering light.

**Do I need a baby monitor to follow safe sleep?**  
No. Monitors can be reassuring, but they do not replace a safe sleep setup and they do not prevent SIDS.

**Should twins share a sleep surface?**  
The AAP recommends against bed-sharing, including with twins. Each baby needs their own firm, flat surface. Each twin should have their own approved crib or bassinet.

**Does breastfeeding replace the other rules?**  
No. Breast milk is protective, but a breastfed baby still needs back sleeping, a clear crib, and a smoke-free room.

**Will back-sleeping flatten my baby’s head?**  
It can leave a soft spot, called positional plagiocephaly. The answer is supervised [tummy time](/tummy-time) when the baby is awake, not stomach sleep at night.

**Can I let my newborn sleep on my chest?**
For the difference between an awake hold and a safe sleep space, see [bed-sharing and co-sleeping](/bed-sharing-and-co-sleeping).
