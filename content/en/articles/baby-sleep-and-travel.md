# Baby Sleep and Travel: Planes, Hotels, and Jet Lag

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Travel scrambles days, but it does not rewrite [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies). This page is about **sleep on the way and at the destination**: jet lag, hotels, and what to do when the only nap happens in a car seat. It is not a packing list for every country, and it is not a second [car-seat safety](/baby-sleeping-in-car-seat) essay — we will link that rule, not repeat the whole airway explanation.

## Short answer

Bring the same basics you use at home: a **firm, flat, clear** crib, bassinet, or play yard, baby on their **back**, in your room if they are still in the first months. HealthyChildren.org (Claire McCarthy, MD, FAAP) says not to relax those habits in a hotel: resist putting the baby in the adult bed; ask for a portable crib or pack-and-play when you book; check that it meets current standards and is in good repair. The AAP's family-travel tips (updated 5 February 2025) add: **to reduce jet lag, adjust the child's sleep schedule 2 to 3 days before departure**, and after you arrive, use **daylight and outdoor or brightly lit time** to help the clock shift. A car-seat nap is for the journey; move them to a flat sleep space as soon as you arrive (AAP, NHS, Lullaby Trust — see our car-seat page).

We are not inventing a "one hour of shift per day" jet-lag formula. HealthyChildren.org's sourced instruction is the 2- to 3-day pre-adjustment plus daylight. For a one-hour change, our [daylight saving](/daylight-saving-baby-sleep) page (a few minutes a day for about a week) is the closer playbook.

## Hotels and rentals

McCarthy's hotel article is the practical checklist:

- Request a crib or play yard when you book. Inspect hardware, the mattress, and fit of the sheet. If it looks wrong, ask for a replacement or use your own travel play yard.
- Put the crib away from cords, lamps, TVs, and anything that could fall.
- Do a "safety crawl" on arrival for dropped pills, pens, and other small objects.
- Never leave a child alone in a hotel room, even with a monitor, including for a meal in the attached restaurant.
- Gate or block stairs if the layout has them; lock balcony doors; watch water (bathtub, toilet, pool).
- Anchor or make tip-able furniture and TVs harder to pull over, as you would at home after a [crib-to-bed](/crib-to-toddler-bed) move.

The same AAP travel-tips page warns that cribs or play yards provided when you travel internationally — and in the U.S. — may not meet current standards. If you have any doubt, ask for a replacement or choose another option.

A familiar [sleep sack](/sleep-sacks), a small [lovey if they are over 12 months](/when-can-baby-sleep-with-a-blanket), and the usual [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby) are the portable parts of home. A new apartment sofa is not a crib.

## Camping, and very hot or cold nights away from home

Safer sleep does not pause in a tent. Red Nose and the Lullaby Trust both treat sleep away from home as the same rules in a different building (or in no building): **on the back**, on a **firm, flat**, approved surface, with a clear space and no loose adult bedding. A pillow nest on an air mattress, a baby on a quilt between adults, or a car seat left at the edge of a tent is not a campsite exception.

Bring a travel crib, play yard, or other approved product that still fits the baby, and use only its own mattress. Keep the sleep space off a soggy groundsheet and away from heater vents and stove fumes. In hot weather, Red Nose’s summer advice and the Lullaby Trust’s hot-weather pages agree with [how to dress a baby for sleep](/how-to-dress-baby-for-sleep): light layers, no hat for sleep, shade, and a check of the chest — not an extra blanket “in case.” In the cold, add a layer or a well-fitting sleep bag; do not use a hot-water bottle, a heating pad, or a heavy duvet in the baby’s space, and do not bring the baby into an adult sleeping bag.

If the only option looks unsafe, it is better to change the plan than to improvise a nest. Temperature and dressing details stay on the dressing page; this section is the travel-and-camping frame.

## Planes, cars, and the "they've finally fallen asleep" problem

**Cars.** Use a correctly installed, rear-facing seat for as long as the seat allows (AAP). Stop about every two hours (HealthyChildren.org). Never leave a child alone in a car. If they sleep in the seat, that is a travel nap; when you get there, move them — the full argument is on [baby sleeping in a car seat](/baby-sleeping-in-car-seat).

**Planes.** The AAP recommends exploring ways for each child to have their own seat and to ride in an FAA-approved car safety seat, even though the FAA allows children under 2 on a lap. Booster seats cannot be used on aircraft but can usually be checked. For ear pain on descent, the same page suggests nursing or a bottle for infants, and chewing gum or a drink through a straw for older children. Talk to your pediatrician before flying with a newborn who has heart or lung problems, or within two weeks of an ear infection or ear surgery.

A stroller nap in the terminal is still a sitting-device nap. Move them to a flat, safe space when you reasonably can.

## Jet lag and time zones

Two to three days before a trip that crosses time zones, start sliding bedtime and wake time toward the destination clock (HealthyChildren.org). After you land, get daylight in the local day, and keep nights as boring and dark as you can — the same contrast idea as [day-night confusion](/newborn-day-night-confusion) and [DST](/daylight-saving-baby-sleep).

Do not skip needed feeds to "force" the new clock in a young baby. Safety and calories come first; the schedule can be messy for a few days.

If you are only changing the clock by one hour, a gentler nudge is enough. If several hours are involved, expect several off days, not a single heroic night.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask before long-haul travel with a preterm or recently discharged baby, before flying after an ear infection, or if you need vaccine or malaria-prevention advice for international trips (AAP travel tips). After you return, mention sleep that has not recovered after a couple of weeks, or any breathing concern that started on the trip.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I handle jet lag with a baby?**  
Shift the schedule 2–3 days before you go, then use local daylight. Do not invent a steeper protocol than that unless your pediatrician gives you one.

**Is a hotel crib safe?**  
It can be, if it meets current standards and is in good repair. Inspect it. If it does not look right, ask for another or use your own play yard.

**Can they sleep in the bed with us "just on vacation"?**  
The AAP still does not recommend bed-sharing. Vacation tiredness is a reason to be more careful, not less. See [bed-sharing](/bed-sharing-and-co-sleeping).

**What if the only nap is in the car?**  
Use it for the drive, then move them when you arrive. Build a crib nap at the destination when you can.

**Why won’t my baby or toddler sleep after we moved house?**
A new room, new noises, and a temporarily unpacked crib can unsettle sleep. That is a change of place, not a new official regression age. Keep the same safe-sleep rules in the new home: back, firm, flat, clear, in your room if they are still in the first months. Rebuild the same short bedtime routine you already use — see [bedtime routine for baby](/bedtime-routine-for-baby). A travel crib or play yard is fine overnight if it meets safety standards and has only a tight fitted sheet (see the pack-and-play FAQ on [when to move baby to crib](/when-to-move-baby-to-crib)). If an older child is frightened of the new dark room, use [fear of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark) and [night lights and baby sleep](/night-lights-and-baby-sleep).
