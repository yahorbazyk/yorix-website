# 6 Month Milestones: What Most Babies Do by Half a Year

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's development, hearing, vision, or feeding, contact your pediatrician.*

Half a year in, babies are busier and noisier: they roll, squeal, blow bubbles with their lips, grab whatever is within reach, and tell you with their whole face who they know. Below is the list of skills most babies have by 6 months, how it builds on the 4-month list, what is *not* expected yet, and when it is worth calling your pediatrician. For naps and bedtimes at this age, go to our [6 month old sleep schedule](/6-month-old-sleep-schedule).

## Short answer

By 6 months, most babies **recognize familiar people, enjoy their reflection, laugh, "talk" back and forth with you, blow raspberries, squeal, mouth objects, reach for a toy they want, close their lips when they've had enough food, roll from tummy to back, push up on straight arms, and lean on their hands while sitting with support.** That's the 2022 CDC checklist for this age, built so that **at least three out of four babies** can do each item. Sitting without support, crawling, and first words are **not** expected at 6 months.

## 6 month milestones checklist (CDC, in our words)

| Area | Most babies by 6 months… |
| --- | --- |
| Social and emotional | Recognize the people they see every day; like looking at themselves in a mirror; laugh |
| Language and communication | Trade sounds with you in turns; stick out the tongue and blow "raspberries"; make high squealing noises |
| Thinking and learning | Explore objects by putting them in the mouth; reach out to grab a toy they want; press their lips shut to show they don't want more food |
| Movement and physical | Roll from front to back; push the chest up on straight arms during tummy time; prop themselves on their hands when sitting with support |

Source: CDC "Milestones by 6 Months" (updated May 2026); the same set appears on Nemours KidsHealth.

## What's new compared with 4 months

The two checklists show how skills build on each other:

| Skill | By 4 months | By 6 months |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laughing | Small chuckle when you play | Real laugh |
| Sounds | Coos and answers your voice | Takes turns, squeals, blows raspberries |
| Hands | Swipes at toys, holds a toy put in the hand | Reaches on purpose for a toy they want |
| Tummy | Props on forearms | Pushes up on straight arms |
| Body | Steady head when held | Rolls tummy to back; leans on hands when supported in sitting |
| Eating cues | Opens mouth for breast or bottle | Closes lips to say "no more" |

That last row matters if you're starting solids: a baby who presses their lips together or turns away is telling you they're done. CDC lists pushing food away, closing the mouth, and turning the head as signs of fullness, and pointing, opening the mouth for a spoon, and excitement at the sight of food as signs of hunger.

## Common questions at this age

**"My baby isn't sitting up on their own."** That's expected. CDC moved independent sitting to the 9-month checklist. At 6 months, sitting *with support* while leaning on the hands is the milestone.

**"My baby rolls only one way."** CDC lists rolling from tummy to back at 6 months. Rolling from back to tummy often comes around the same time or later; keep offering floor time on both front and back.

**"Everything goes in the mouth."** CDC counts mouthing as a thinking skill at 6 months: it's how babies learn about texture and shape. Keep small objects, coins, button batteries, and anything that fits through a toilet-paper tube out of reach *(the tube test is a common practical rule, not from CDC)*.

**"Is a first tooth a milestone?"** Teeth aren't on CDC's checklists. The AAP says most babies start teething between about 4 and 7 months, but later is common and usually normal. See our [teething symptoms](/teething-symptoms) guide.

**"Should my baby be eating solids?"** CDC suggests talking to your pediatrician about when to start; breast milk or formula remain the most important food. Read [when to start solids](/when-to-start-solids) for readiness signs.

## Play ideas for a 6 month old

Adapted from CDC's suggestions for this age:

- **Copy and reply.** When your baby smiles or makes a sound, answer the same way, then pause and let them have a turn.
- **Name the world.** On walks, point to dogs, trees, and buses and say what they are.
- **Toys just out of reach.** Put a toy a little beyond your baby's hands while they're on their tummy or back to tempt a roll.
- **Supported sitting practice.** Hold your baby sitting up and put toys in front of them so they practice balance.
- **Supported standing.** Hold your baby firmly under the arms and let them take weight on their feet for a moment while you look around together.
- **The drop game.** When a toy hits the floor, hand it back. It teaches cause and effect.
- **New sounds.** Whisper, clap, and make silly noises; notice which ones your baby turns to.
- **Calming skills.** When your baby is upset, help them settle with a soft voice, rocking, a song, or a pacifier.

CDC still advises no screen time before age 2 apart from video chats with family.

## Premature babies

For a baby born early, compare skills with the checklist for their **corrected age** for the first 2 years, as the AAP recommends. For example, a 6-month-old born 2 months early is usually compared with the 4-month list.

## When to call your pediatrician

Talk to your baby's doctor and ask about developmental screening if:

- by 6 months (corrected age) your baby isn't doing one or more of the items on the checklist;
- your baby **loses** a skill they had, such as no longer babbling or no longer reaching;
- your baby doesn't respond to sounds or doesn't seem to see well;
- one side of the body moves much more than the other, or your baby feels very stiff or very floppy *(clinical warning signs, not CDC milestones)*;
- you're simply worried. CDC's advice is "don't wait".

You can also contact your state's early intervention program yourself; in the U.S., children under 3 can get a free evaluation (HealthyChildren.org). The AAP recommends formal developmental screening at the 9-month visit for every baby, but you don't have to wait for it if you have concerns.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should a 6 month old be doing?**  
Most 6-month-olds laugh, squeal, blow raspberries, take turns making sounds, recognize familiar people, reach for toys, roll from tummy to back, push up on straight arms, and lean on their hands when sitting with support.

**Should my 6 month old be sitting up alone?**  
No. Sitting without support is on CDC's 9-month checklist.

**Should a 6 month old crawl?**  
Not yet for most babies. Cleveland Clinic says most crawl between about 7 and 10 months; some start by rolling, belly-crawling, or scooting on their bottom first.

**Should a 6 month old say "mama"?**  
Not as a real word. Repeated syllables like "mamama" appear on the 9-month list; calling a parent "mama" or "dada" appears at 1 year.

**My 6 month old doesn't laugh. Is that a problem?**  
Laughing is on the 6-month checklist, so mention it to your pediatrician.
