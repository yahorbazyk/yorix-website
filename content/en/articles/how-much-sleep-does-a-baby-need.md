# How Much Sleep Does a Baby Need? A Chart From Newborn to Age 5

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"How much sleep does a baby need?" sounds as if there should be one official number. There is not. There are two widely used charts, they do not match at every age, and healthy babies sit on a wide range around them. This page is a **totals chart** — hours in 24 hours, naps included — not a daily schedule. For sample days and wake windows, use the month guides linked below and our [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age) and [nap schedule by age](/baby-nap-schedule-by-age) pages.

## Short answer

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), in a 2016 consensus statement endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, recommends **12 to 16 hours per 24 hours** for infants 4 to 12 months, **11 to 14 hours** at 1 to 2 years, and **10 to 13 hours** at 3 to 5 years, naps included. The AASM did **not** issue a recommendation for the first 3 months, because the evidence was not strong enough. The National Sleep Foundation's 2015 ranges, which Sleep Foundation and KidsHealth still quote, suggest **14 to 17 hours** from 0 to 3 months and **12 to 15 hours** from 4 to 11 months — a slightly different infant band. Cleveland Clinic, looking at the whole first year, says babies often sleep **11 to 17 hours** a day. Use the charts as a range, not a daily target your baby must hit.

### Key numbers at a glance

| Age | AASM (AAP-endorsed) | National Sleep Foundation (2015) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3 months | No official AASM number | 14–17 hours in 24 hours | Sleep Foundation also notes healthy newborns may sleep as few as 11 or as many as 19 hours |
| 4–12 months | 12–16 hours (4–12 months) | 12–15 hours (listed as 4–11 months) | The upper end differs by one hour; both include naps |
| 1–2 years | 11–14 hours | 11–14 hours | Includes naps |
| 3–5 years | 10–13 hours | 10–13 hours | Includes any remaining nap |
| 6–12 years | 9–12 hours | 9–11 hours (NSF school-age band is slightly narrower) | Shown for completeness; this page focuses on babies and toddlers |
| 13–18 years | 8–10 hours | 8–10 hours | AASM teenager range |

KidsHealth's parent summary uses 14–17 hours at 0–3 months and 12–16 hours at 4–12 months — that is, NSF at the start of infancy and AASM after 4 months. We would rather show both original charts than blend them silently.

## Why the official bodies disagree a little

The AASM consensus (Paruthi and colleagues, published in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* in 2016) reviewed hundreds of studies and then recommended ranges that "promote optimal health." The AAP endorsed those ranges. The panel started at 4 months because newborn sleep is so variable, and because high-quality studies in the first months were limited.

The National Sleep Foundation's 2015 update (Hirshkowitz and colleagues, in *Sleep Health*) is the other chart you will see. It includes a newborn band and uses 4–11 months rather than 4–12 months, with a slightly lower upper limit (15 vs 16 hours). Neither chart is "wrong." They are two expert groups drawing similar, not identical, lines.

Cleveland Clinic's first-year article (reviewed 15 June 2023) describes an even wider everyday range of 11 to 17 hours and tells parents to ask their pediatrician what is normal for their baby. That is a useful reminder: a single night or week outside a chart is not, on its own, a diagnosis.

## What "hours in 24 hours" actually includes

Every figure in the table is **total sleep**, nights plus naps. A 6-month-old who sleeps 11 hours at night and 3 hours across two or three naps is inside the AASM range. A toddler who dropped to one nap and sleeps 11 hours at night plus a 2-hour nap is inside the 1–2 year range. If you only count night sleep, you will think your baby is "short" when they are not.

Newborns are a special case. Their sleep comes in short stretches of one to two hours, around the clock, because they need frequent feeds and their body clock is still maturing. Sleep Foundation describes this pattern clearly, and NHS reminds parents that some babies sleep much more than others and that a newborn's pattern is unlikely to match yours. For week-by-week expectations, see [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule) and [when do babies sleep through the night](/when-do-babies-sleep-through-the-night).

## What happens when a child regularly gets less — or a lot more

The AASM statement links regularly sleeping in the recommended range with better attention, behavior, learning, memory, emotional regulation, and physical health. Regularly sleeping fewer hours than the range is associated with attention, behavior, and learning problems, and with higher risk of accidents, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and depression (with additional self-harm risks discussed for teenagers). HealthyChildren.org, summarizing the AAP's support for these guidelines, also lists irritability, poor concentration, headaches, and a harder time at school when children are routinely short on sleep, and notes that enough sleep supports the immune system and mental health.

"More than the range" is less often discussed and is not automatically better. A baby who is suddenly much sleepier than usual, is hard to wake, or is feeding poorly needs a medical check, not a later bedtime. Cleveland Clinic makes this point for babies who seem to sleep "too much," including the possibility of illness.

## How to use the chart without turning it into a test

- Add up a typical 24 hours, not a single night.
- Compare with your baby's mood, feeding, and growth, not only with a neighbor's baby.
- Expect totals to swing during a [sleep regression](/sleep-regression), a cold, or a nap transition. Look at the week, not the day.
- Move bedtime earlier if nights plus naps consistently come in under the range and your child looks exhausted. Do not keep a baby up late in the hope of a longer night — that often backfires; see [overtired baby](/overtired-baby).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Sleep Foundation's wake-window article, and Cleveland Clinic, both suggest checking in if your baby regularly sleeps a lot more or less than the range for their age, or if you notice snoring, long pauses in breathing, or very restless sleep. Call sooner if your baby is unusually hard to wake, is not feeding, has fewer wet diapers, or seems unwell. Charts cannot tell you whether a particular baby has a medical sleep problem.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much sleep does a newborn need?**  
There is no AASM official number. NSF suggests 14–17 hours in 24 hours, and Sleep Foundation notes a healthy range that can run from about 11 to 19 hours, in short stretches.

**How much should a 6-month-old sleep?**  
AASM: 12–16 hours in 24 hours, including naps. Details and example days are on our [6 month old sleep schedule](/6-month-old-sleep-schedule).

**Do the hours have to be in one long night block?**  
No. The recommendations are 24-hour totals. Many babies still wake at night and still meet the range; see [when do babies sleep through the night](/when-do-babies-sleep-through-the-night).

**Is 11 hours enough for a 4-month-old?**  
It is below the AASM 12–16 hour range. One short day is not a crisis; a regular pattern that low, especially if the baby is miserable, is worth a conversation with your pediatrician and a look at naps and bedtime.

**Should I wake my baby to keep them from sleeping "too much"?**  
Not to hit a chart. Newborns sometimes need to be woken for feeds — that is a nutrition question, discussed with your pediatrician, not a way to trim sleep.
