# Preemie Sleep: Use Due Date, Not Just Birthday

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A baby born before 37 weeks does not follow a standard [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule) by the calendar date of birth. This page is about **corrected age**, the safer-sleep clock that runs from the due date, and why night stretches often arrive later than they do for term babies. It is not a second copy of [safe sleep for babies](/safe-sleep-for-babies), and it is not a term-baby sample day with the word "preemie" swapped in.

## Short answer

**Corrected age** (also called adjusted age) is your baby's age from the due date, not from the birthday. Use it when you are thinking about developmental sleep expectations — for example, when longer night stretches or self-settling might realistically show up. HealthyChildren, in an AAP-sourced preemie article, says not to expect a preterm baby to sleep a longer stretch at night for many months: a term baby might manage a 6- to 8-hour stretch around 4 months, while a preemie **may not do that until 6 to 8 months or later**. That 2015 page is older than the AAP's 2022 safe-sleep policy; we use it for the timetable comparison, not as a replacement for current safety rules.

Safer-sleep advice is usually given until 12 months of age. The Lullaby Trust and the NHS say that if the baby was born early, you should **follow that advice for a year from the due date, not from the birthday**. The Lullaby Trust also advises **avoiding bed-sharing completely until one year from the due date**, and keeping the baby in your room for **at least six months past the due date**. Premature and low-birth-weight babies (under 2.5 kg or 5.5 pounds) have a higher risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) (Lullaby Trust, NHS, AAP). That is why the rules are applied for longer, not relaxed.

The sleep position itself is the same as for term babies once the baby is medically ready: **on the back, on a firm, flat surface, in their own clear sleep space**. The AAP says hospitalized preterm infants should be placed supine as soon as they are stable — often by about 32 weeks' gestational age — so that back-sleeping is modeled in the NICU before discharge. Even preemies with reflux sleep on the back, not on an incline (AAP 2022). See [reflux and baby sleep](/reflux-and-baby-sleep).

## Corrected age is for expectations, not for skipping safety

If your baby arrived 8 weeks early, then at 16 weeks after birth their corrected age is about 8 weeks. Sleepy cues, nap length, and when "sleeping a longer stretch" becomes realistic should be read against that corrected age, not against a term-baby chart printed for the birthday. The AAP-sourced HealthyChildren article also uses **corrected age** when it talks about a later spell of sleep disruption around 6 to 9 months.

What corrected age does **not** do is shorten safe-sleep practice. The Lullaby Trust and NHS clock — 12 months from the due date — is the conservative public-health rule we follow here. If your neonatologist or pediatrician has given you a personal plan (for monitors, oxygen, or positioning that was used in hospital), follow that clinical plan and ask when it changes at home.

Twins and multiples are more often early or smaller at birth, which is one reason they appear in SIDS statistics (NHS, Lullaby Trust). Sleep for two babies in one household is a separate practical article: [twins and sleep](/twins-and-sleep).

## Safer sleep after a NICU stay

The home crib or bassinet should look like the safe-sleep setup you want to keep using, not like a nest of positioning aids from the unit unless your team has prescribed a specific device and told you how long to use it. In general:

- Lie the baby on their **back** for every sleep, once they are medically stable enough for that position (AAP).
- Use a **firm, flat, waterproof mattress** in their own cot or Moses basket, kept clear of pillows, duvets, bumpers, loose bedding, toys, pods, and wedges (Lullaby Trust, NHS, AAP).
- Share a **room**, not a bed, sofa, or armchair. The Lullaby Trust is especially clear that premature or low-birth-weight babies have a much higher SIDS risk if they share a sleep space with a sleeping adult, and it advises against co-sleeping until a year from the due date. Our [bed-sharing](/bed-sharing-and-co-sleeping) page explains the AAP's broader room-share-without-bed-share stance.
- Keep the baby **smoke-free** in pregnancy and after birth (Lullaby Trust, NHS, AAP).
- The Lullaby Trust says a baby sleeping bag should fit well, is usually sized by weight, and that babies should be **8.8 pounds or 4 kg** before one is used. Treat that as UK product-and-charity guidance, and check the maker's weight range; it is not a separate AAP number.

If a hospital used side or tummy positioning for medical reasons, ask the team to show you back-sleeping before discharge. The AAP wants NICUs to model supine sleep well before going home, because the link between front-sleeping and SIDS is as strong, or stronger, in preterm and low-birth-weight infants.

A home cardiorespiratory monitor is not a substitute for a safe sleep space. If your baby was discharged with a monitor, use it as prescribed and keep the crib rules anyway.

## Apnea of prematurity — a clinical issue, not a shopping problem

Some preterm babies have **apnea of prematurity**: pauses in breathing, or shorter pauses with a slow heart rate or low oxygen, because the systems that control breathing are still maturing (Cleveland Clinic; KidsHealth). That is diagnosed and managed in hospital, often in the NICU. It is **not** the same as ordinary [periodic breathing](/newborn-grunting-in-sleep#periodic-breathing-a-normal-newborn-pattern-not-a-home-diagnosis), and it is **not** a reason to buy a consumer “SIDS monitor” on the way home (see [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies)).

If your team discharges you with a prescribed monitor, use it as they taught you, and still keep the crib rules in the section above. HealthyChildren.org’s NICU safe-sleep advice is the same direction already on this page: units should model **back-sleeping** before discharge, and positioning aids from the hospital are not souvenirs for the home crib unless a clinician has prescribed a specific device and told you how long to use it. We will not write an apnea-treatment protocol here. Ask the neonatology or pediatric team about any pause you see after discharge.

## Nights often stay broken for longer — and that can still be normal

HealthyChildren's preemie-sleep article recommends the same day-and-night contrast we describe for term newborns, only with more patience: play and talk in the daytime; keep night feeds **quiet, brief, and dimly lit**. It may take several weeks after coming home for days and nights to sort themselves out. That is a longer runway than many term-baby "day-night confusion" timelines, not a different set of tricks — see [newborn day-night confusion](/newborn-day-night-confusion) for the general idea, and do not force a term-baby schedule onto a baby who is still very young by due date.

A predictable putting-down sequence (same few steps, same sleep space) still helps, once you are home and the baby is stable. Starting early, in a small way, to let the baby practice settling in the crib or bassinet is the HealthyChildren suggestion for easing a later bump around 6 to 9 months' **corrected** age. That is not a call to start formal [sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training) in the NICU or in the first weeks at home. Readiness should be discussed with your pediatrician against corrected age, health, and feeding, not copied from a term 4-month plan.

A pacifier at sleep time, once breastfeeding is established if you are nursing, remains on the AAP's risk-reduction list for infants in general; HealthyChildren also mentions a pacifier or the baby's fist as a possible comfort. See [pacifier and sleep](/pacifier-and-sleep).

## When to talk to your pediatrician or neonatology team

Ask before you copy a term-baby schedule, if breathing or color changes worry you, if weight gain or feeding is off, or if you were sent home with positioning, oxygen, or a monitor and are unsure how that fits with back-sleeping. Call emergency services if breathing stops, the baby becomes pale, blue, or gray, or you cannot rouse them — the same urgent rules as on our breathing-sounds pages.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I use my preemie's real age or corrected age for sleep?**  
Use corrected age for developmental expectations (when longer nights or new skills might appear). Use the due-date clock for how long to keep full safer-sleep practice (Lullaby Trust, NHS: 12 months from the due date).

**When do premature babies sleep through the night?**  
There is no single official week. An older AAP-sourced HealthyChildren article says a longer 6- to 8-hour stretch may not arrive until 6 to 8 months or later, later than many term babies. "Through the night" is also a slippery phrase — see [when babies sleep through the night](/when-do-babies-sleep-through-the-night).

**Can a preemie bed-share if I am breastfeeding?**  
The Lullaby Trust advises avoiding bed-sharing completely until one year from the due date for premature or low-birth-weight babies, because the SIDS risk is higher. Discuss any exception with your baby's clinicians.

**Do preemies still have to sleep on their backs if they have reflux?**  
Yes. The AAP says even preterm infants with GER should be on the back, on a flat, firm surface, without commercial devices that elevate the head. Details are on [reflux and baby sleep](/reflux-and-baby-sleep).
