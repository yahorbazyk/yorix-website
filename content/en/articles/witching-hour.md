# The Witching Hour: When Evenings Get Fussy

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"Witching hour" is a parent phrase, not a formal diagnosis. It usually means a stretch of fussiness in the **late afternoon or evening** in an otherwise well young baby. ZERO TO THREE uses the name that way: many babies cry more at the end of the day, possibly because they are tired or have had a full day of sights and sounds, and this pattern is common in the first months and usually eases with development.

This is **not** a second [colic](/colic) article. Colic already explains the clinical "rule of threes," the Period of PURPLE Crying acronym, remedies, and medical red flags. Our colic page even has a short section that mentions the witching hour. This page's job is the everyday evening window that often **does not** meet a colic definition — what it looks like, when it tends to peak, and what is reasonable to try — plus a clear pointer back to colic when the crying is more than that.

## Short answer

The American Academy of Pediatrics describes a **regular fussy period**, often between about 6 p.m. and midnight, that **peaks at about 3 hours a day around 6 weeks** and typically falls to about 1 to 2 hours a day by 3 to 4 months. Many families call that window the witching hour, even when it is shorter than a clock-hour and even when it is not colic. ZERO TO THREE notes a broader crying curve: after a quieter start, crying often rises and **peaks around 6 to 8 weeks**, when more than two hours a day can be ordinary, and eases noticeably for most families by 3 to 4 months.

If your baby has a tough spell in the early evening, settles within a few hours, and is fairly calm the rest of the day, you are likely looking at this common pattern — not a failure of your feeding or bedtime technique. If the crying is very long, very intense, and hard to soothe across the day, read [colic](/colic) and talk with the pediatrician rather than stretching this page into a diagnosis.

## Why evenings are harder

No official body has a single proven "cause of the witching hour." The explanations parents and clinicians use are everyday ones: a day's worth of stimulation catching up, an overtired baby who is then harder to settle ([overtired baby](/overtired-baby)), a cluster of feeds in the evening, or a baby whose immature nervous system simply has a fussy shift. ZERO TO THREE links evening crying to tiredness and overstimulation after the day's input. That is a plausible description, not a lab result.

Because the name is informal, app blogs sometimes give exact start weeks (for example 2 to 3 weeks). Those timelines are popular, not a diagnosis code. We stick to the sourced crying curve: worse in the first months, often peaking near 6 weeks, usually kinder by 3 to 4 months.

## What may help, without turning into a colic-remedy list

Work through needs first: hunger, a burp, a diaper, temperature, a chance to sleep. A slightly earlier bedtime can help if the baby is falling apart from tiredness, rather than pushing through to a late "normal" hour and arriving more wound up. A dimmer, quieter room in the late afternoon reduces one source of overload. Holding, walking, and the soothing sequence on [the 5 S's](/5-s-soothing-a-crying-baby) are reasonable tools for a fussy, **awake** baby, with the same limit: side or stomach only in arms, then down on the back.

Some babies feed more often in the evening. That can be normal cluster feeding; it is not automatically a problem to fix on a sleep chart. If feeds are painful, the baby is not gaining, or you suspect reflux, use [reflux and baby sleep](/reflux-and-baby-sleep) and your clinician, not a wedge in the crib.

If nothing is working and you feel yourself getting angry, put the baby on their back in the crib or bassinet and step into another room for a few minutes. ZERO TO THREE describes this as a safe break, not neglect. **Never shake a baby.** Call someone if you can. The colic page has a longer section on looking after yourself; we will not copy it here.

## When it is more than a witching hour

Use the red-flag list on [colic](/colic) and call the pediatrician or emergency services if the crying is sudden and different, the baby has a fever (100.4°F or 38°C or higher is urgent under 3 months), is vomiting, is not feeding, is hard to wake, or has trouble breathing. ZERO TO THREE also flags a sudden change in the crying, poor feeding, unusual sleepiness, dehydration, injury, or breathing trouble. If you are low, anxious, or having thoughts that scare you, that is a reason to call your own clinician — postpartum depression is common enough that ZERO TO THREE mentions it in this context, and it is not a test you have to fail before asking for help.

## Frequently asked questions

**When does the witching hour start and end?**  
There is no official start date. The AAP's regular fussy period is often in the evening, hardest around 6 weeks, and usually lighter by 3 to 4 months. ZERO TO THREE describes a similar peak at 6 to 8 weeks and improvement by 3 to 4 months.

**Is the witching hour the same as colic?**  
Not necessarily. Evening fuss can be ordinary. Colic is the more intense, prolonged end of the spectrum and has its own definition and page.

**Is PURPLE crying the same thing?**  
The Period of PURPLE Crying is an educational framework for a normal developmental crying phase, often worse in the evening. Our [colic](/colic) page already explains the acronym. This page does not repeat it.

**Will a stricter schedule stop it?**  
A slightly earlier bedtime and a calmer late afternoon can help an overtired baby. A rigid timetable will not erase a developmental fuss window, and it is not a reason to skip feeds.
