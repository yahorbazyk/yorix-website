# How to Swaddle a Baby Safely — and When to Stop

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Swaddling is a thin wrap that holds a newborn's arms snug and can calm the startle (Moro) reflex. Many families find it useful in the first weeks. It is optional, not a requirement, and it has rules. The two that matter most: keep the wrap **hip-healthy** (legs free to bend up and out), and **stop as soon as your baby shows signs of trying to roll**, which can be as early as 2 months.

## Short answer

If you swaddle, use a light blanket or a commercial wrap, place your baby **on their back** every time, and leave enough room at the hips and knees for the legs to flex and fall open. Do not wrap the chest so tightly that breathing looks strained. Watch for overheating. Do not use a **weighted** swaddle. Stop swaddling at the first attempts to roll — Sleep Foundation notes this may start at 2 months and, for most babies, means stopping by around 3 months — and move to a [sleep sack](/sleep-sacks) with free arms if you still want a wearable layer.

## Why parents swaddle — and what it does not do

A systematic review in *Pediatrics* (van Sleuwen and colleagues, 2007), cited by the IHDI, found that swaddling can reduce crying and help some infants settle. Sleep Foundation adds that it may calm some babies, but **it is not a SIDS-prevention method**. NICHD, as quoted there, says there is no evidence that swaddling is any safer than dressing a baby in ordinary layered sleepwear. If your baby sleeps well without a wrap, you do not need to start.

Swaddling also adds heat. That is useful in a cool room and a risk in a warm one. Combine this page with [how to dress a baby for sleep](/how-to-dress-baby-for-sleep) and the [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) checklist rather than treating the wrap as a complete sleep plan.

## Hip-healthy wrapping: the rule that is easy to miss

Improper swaddling is a known risk for **developmental dysplasia of the hip**. The International Hip Dysplasia Institute's position is specific: infant hips should sit in slight flexion and abduction during swaddling. The knees should also be slightly flexed. The legs must not be tightly wrapped straight down and pressed together, as if the baby were standing at attention. That "straight down" position can loosen the joints and damage the soft cartilage of the socket.

In practice:

- After you wrap the arms and chest, leave a loose pouch for the legs so they can bend up and fall out, the way they sat in the womb.
- Commercial swaddles should have a loose sack or pouch for the legs and feet. Even some products can confine the thighs if you pull the fastening too tight — the IHDI warns about this.
- An alternative, also endorsed by the IHDI, is to swaddle only the upper body and leave the legs completely free.

The IHDI describes one common method: fold a square cloth to make a straight edge; place the baby's shoulders at that edge; wrap one side over the arm and chest and tuck; repeat on the other side; then twist or fold the bottom and tuck it behind the baby **only after** you have left the legs bent up and out. Watch a clinician or a trusted video if you are unsure — a written list is easier to get tight at the hips than you think.

## How to swaddle without making breathing or heat worse

Sleep Foundation's safety points, which track AAP advice, are:

- **Back only.** A swaddled baby who ends up face-down cannot easily roll back. That is the core danger once rolling starts, and it is why a belly-sleeping swaddle is never acceptable.
- **Not too tight across the chest.** A baby who struggles to breathe, or whose chest cannot rise easily, is wrapped too tightly.
- **Light fabric.** A thin muslin or a purpose-made wrap is enough. Thick quilts trap heat and add loose fabric if they come undone.
- **Watch for overheating:** sweating, damp hair, flushed or red cheeks, skin that is hot to the touch, or quicker breathing. If you see those signs, loosen or stop the swaddle and remove a layer. See the dressing article for more on temperature.
- **No weighted products.** The AAP, KidsHealth, and Sleep Foundation all advise against weighted swaddles and weighted blankets. Evidence on their safety is limited, and the extra weight may make it harder for a baby to breathe or may add heat.

If the wrap comes loose, it becomes loose bedding, which does not belong in a safe crib. Re-wrap or switch to a zip or velcro swaddle sack that stays put — still with a loose hip pouch.

## When to stop, and how to come out of the swaddle

Stop when your baby starts **trying to roll**, not on a birthday. Sleep Foundation says this may begin at 2 months and that for most babies swaddling should stop around 3 months. The IHDI adds that once a baby can roll onto their stomach, a swaddle increases the risk of ending up face-down.

A simple transition many families use, described across baby-sleep sites and consistent with the "arms can be in or out" safety note:

1. One arm out for a few days, then both arms out, still using a sack for the legs if you like.
2. Or go straight to a [sleep sack](/sleep-sacks) with free arms, which is the AAP's preferred wearable blanket once the wrap is gone.
3. Expect a few bumpier nights. That is a reason to change at the start of a calmer stretch of days, not during illness or a [sleep regression](/sleep-regression), if you can help it.

If your baby has started rolling and you are still swaddling, stop at the next sleep. Do not wait for a "perfect" moment.

## “Wrapping,” the startle (Moro), and arms-up sleep

In Australia and the UK, **wrapping** is the usual word for the same skill this page calls swaddling. Red Nose, Raising Children Network, and Karitane wrapping advice follow the same safety limits already here: hips free to bend, chest not tight, no hood, and **stop once the baby can roll**. Use this page’s how-to.

A newborn’s **Moro (startle) reflex** can jerk the arms and briefly wake a baby, including as they drop into light sleep. That reflex is ordinary, and it fades over the early months. Swaddling is sometimes used to make those jerks less of a full wake — it is not a treatment for a diagnosis, and it is not a reason to wrap past the rolling deadline already in the section above.

**Arms-up** (or one arm out) is a common way to come out of the wrap, already listed in the transition steps above. Some babies sleep more happily with hands free or near the face. That is a transition, not a new method. If startle-waking is really a short cycle at the end of a nap, see [baby sleep cycles](/baby-sleep-cycles) and [false starts](/false-starts) rather than tightening the wrap.

## What if my baby hates the swaddle?

Some newborns arch, cry, or work their arms free every time. That is allowed. Swaddling is a tool, not a test you have to pass. Try arms-out, a looser hip pouch, or skip the wrap and use a sleep sack or ordinary layers instead. Soothing with holding, feeding, or a [pacifier](/pacifier-and-sleep) is still available. If your baby seems in pain when the hips are moved, or one leg looks to sit differently from the other, raise that with your pediatrician — those are hip questions, not "fussy swaddle" questions.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask before you swaddle if your baby was premature, has a respiratory problem, or has a known hip concern. Ask promptly if you notice uneven thigh creases, limited hip movement, or a clicking hip, or if you are unsure whether rolling attempts have started. And if a wrap ever seems to make breathing harder, take it off and get medical advice.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is swaddling safe?**  
It can be, for a non-rolling baby on their back, in a light, hip-healthy wrap, with no extra loose bedding and no added weights. It is not safer than simple layers, and it becomes unsafe once rolling starts.

**When should I stop swaddling?**  
At the first signs that your baby is trying to roll, which can be as early as 2 months.

**Can I swaddle with arms out?**  
Yes. Sleep Foundation says arms in or out does not change safety. Arms-out is also a useful step when you are stopping.

**Can I swaddle at daycare or with a grandparent?**  
Only if every caregiver knows the back-only rule and the stop-when-rolling rule. A wrap that is safe at 6 weeks can be unsafe at 10 weeks in someone else's care if the baby has started to roll.

**Are velcro or zip swaddle sacks better than a blanket?**  
They can stay in place more reliably, which reduces loose fabric. They still need a loose hip pouch, a light fabric, back sleeping, and a hard stop at rolling.

**Can I use a swaddle and a loose blanket together?**  
No. The wrap replaces the blanket. A second covering adds the loose-bedding risk that [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) advice is trying to remove.
