# The Ferber Method: Check-In Chart, Step-by-Step Plan, and When to Stop

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

The Ferber method is one specific, structured way to sleep train. This page goes deep on that single method: the interval chart, exactly what a check-in looks like, how to handle 2 a.m. wakings, and the signs that it is not working. If you are still choosing between approaches, start with our [overview of sleep training methods](/sleep-training-methods-explained).

## Short answer

The **Ferber method** (also called graduated extinction, "Ferberizing," progressive waiting, or the interval method) means putting your baby down awake after a bedtime routine, leaving the room, and returning for **brief check-ins at gradually longer intervals**. Sleep Foundation describes check-ins of **under one to two minutes**, with no picking up or feeding, and says the method usually works **within a few nights to a week**. It suggests that **around 6 months** is a sensible starting age for many babies. If there is no improvement after **seven days**, Sleep Foundation (summarizing Dr. Ferber) advises stopping to rethink.

## Who created it and what "graduated extinction" means

Pediatrician Richard Ferber popularized the method in a 1985 book, with a revised edition in 2006 (Sleep Foundation). Cleveland Clinic calls it the best-known form of graduated extinction. The aim is to help a baby stop associating falling asleep with a parent's presence.

It sits between two extremes. Full extinction ("cry it out") involves no check-ins at all. Gentler options keep a parent in the room. Ferber keeps a fixed structure but builds in regular reassurance visits.

## The Ferber check-in chart

These are the intervals published by Sleep Foundation, in minutes of waiting before each check-in.

| Night | First wait | Second wait | Third and later waits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3 | 5 | 10 |
| 2 | 5 | 10 | 12 |
| 3 | 10 | 12 | 15 |
| 4 | 12 | 15 | 17 |
| 5 | 15 | 17 | 20 |
| 6 | 17 | 20 | 25 |
| 7 | 20 | 25 | 30 |

Two notes from the sources:

- **The numbers are flexible.** Both Sleep Foundation and Cleveland Clinic say Dr. Ferber accepted that families may adapt the times. Cleveland Clinic quotes a pediatrician whose families sometimes use 10, then 20, then 30 minutes.
- **The direction matters more than the exact minutes.** Waits should get longer within a night and from one night to the next.

## How to do the Ferber method: step by step

1. **Pick a start date with no big disruptions.** Avoid illness, travel, or a houseguest week.
2. **Agree on the plan with every adult who will do bedtime.** Write the chart down and keep a timer handy.
3. **Do a calm, predictable bedtime routine.** For example: bath, pajamas, milk, a book, a song.
4. **Put your baby in the crib awake.** Sleep Foundation stresses not letting them fall asleep in your arms or while feeding, since the goal is to fall asleep independently.
5. **Say goodnight and leave**, even if your baby starts to cry.
6. **Wait for the first interval** on the chart.
7. **Check in briefly.** Keep the lights off and your voice soft. You can pat or shush, but don't pick your baby up or feed them (Sleep Foundation; Cleveland Clinic).
8. **Leave again while they are still awake** and wait for the next, longer interval.
9. **Repeat until your baby falls asleep.** The next night, move one row down the chart.

## What to do when your baby wakes in the night

Sleep Foundation says that if your child falls asleep and then wakes later, you can **restart the intervals** for that night. Before starting the method, decide with your pediatrician whether your baby still needs a night feed. If they do, keep that feed and use the check-in pattern for other wakings.

Many parents choose a simple rule, for example: "a feed only if it has been at least X hours since the last one, otherwise check-ins." Agree on the number in advance so you are not deciding at 3 a.m.

## Right age for Ferber

- **Sleep Foundation:** around 6 months, because many babies can self-soothe and resettle by then.
- **Sleep Foundation's sleep training guide:** most babies are not ready before 4–6 months.
- **App example:** Huckleberry notes that Dr. Ferber's own lower limit is about 3–4 months, but suggests waiting until at least 6 months for this kind of method.

**Avoid it in the newborn months.** Mayo Clinic advises not leaving babies to cry alone in the first month. Ferber can also be adapted for older children, for example by making reassurance depend on staying in bed (Sleep Foundation). If you're weighing Ferber against leaving your baby without check-ins, see [the cry it out method](/cry-it-out-method).

## When the Ferber method is not a good fit

Sleep Foundation says behavioral methods like Ferber are not enough on their own for children with:

- heavy snoring, which may be a sign of a breathing disorder;
- neurodevelopmental conditions such as Down syndrome or autism spectrum disorder;
- sleep problems linked with medical, mental health, or unstable home circumstances.

Talk to your pediatrician first if any of these apply, or if your baby is unwell, has reflux symptoms, or has feeding or weight concerns.

## When to stop or pause

- **No improvement after 7 days:** stop and reassess (Sleep Foundation).
- **Illness, teething pain, or vomiting during crying:** pause and comfort your baby. Restart once they are well.
- **It feels unbearable for your family:** another method may fit better. Sleep Foundation lists the chair method, pick-up/put-down, no-cry approaches, full extinction, and scheduled awakenings as alternatives.

Expect a possible bump: Sleep Foundation describes "post-extinction bursts," where crying returns for a short time after it seemed to be over. It says these are normal and that consistency helps them pass.

## Does Ferber work, and is it harmful?

| Study | What was tested | Main finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mindell et al., 2006 (AASM review, 52 studies) | Behavioral treatments for bedtime problems and night wakings | 94% of studies reported the treatments were effective; over 80% of children improved, with gains lasting 3–6 months; graduated extinction was among the supported methods |
| Gradisar et al., 2016 (randomized trial, 43 infants aged 6–16 months) | Graduated extinction vs bedtime fading vs sleep education | Both methods improved sleep compared with control, with no adverse stress responses and no effect on attachment or child emotions and behavior 12 months later |
| Price et al., 2012 (5-year follow-up, 225 families) | Behavioral sleep techniques used at 8–10 months | No evidence of differences in child mental health, stress, parent–child relationship, or maternal wellbeing at age 6 |

These studies support the method's safety for healthy, typically developing babies. They don't prove it is the best choice for every family.

## What can make it harder

- **Starting during a nap transition, illness, or travel.**
- **Going in "just this once" outside the plan.** Sleep Foundation warns that a single lapse may reinforce the need for a parent's presence.
- **Long, stimulating check-ins** with lights on, picking up, or feeding.
- **A late, overtired bedtime** or an afternoon nap that ended too close to bed.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does the Ferber method take?**  
Sleep Foundation says it usually works within a few nights to a week.

**What age can you start the Ferber method?**  
Sleep Foundation suggests around 6 months. Don't use it for newborns.

**Can I pick up my baby during a check-in?**  
The standard method says no. Keep check-ins short, quiet, and hands-on only for a pat or a touch.

**What if my baby cries for longer than the chart allows?**  
Keep doing check-ins at the current interval for that night. If there is no improvement after a week, stop and reassess.

**Does the Ferber method work for naps?**  
The sources we checked describe it for bedtime and night wakings. Many families start with nights and leave naps more flexible.

**Is the Ferber method the same as cry it out?**  
No. Cry it out has no check-ins; Ferber adds timed reassurance visits (Cleveland Clinic).
