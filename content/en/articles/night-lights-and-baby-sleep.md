# Night Lights and Baby Sleep: Color Claims, and What We Can Source

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

This is a **light-at-night** page: how bright the room should be for a 2 a.m. feed, what we can actually cite about color, and how to treat projectors. It is not [fear of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark), which already allows a dim lamp as comfort and deliberately left color science to a later, sourced article — this one. It is not [white noise](/white-noise-for-baby-sleep). It does not retell the bedtime-routine or toddler-fear plans.

## Short answer

A **dim** lamp for a night change or feed is ordinary, practical advice. Sleep Foundation suggests using a nightlight instead of the overhead light, and, in the newborn period, a **red bulb** so the caregiver can see. That is their wording, and it is about *you* being able to work without flooding the room. It is **not** an infant clinical trial, and their claim that newborns "cannot see the red light waves" should be read as a popular-science shorthand, not as a proven law of infant vision. We mark that part as **unverified as a developmental fact**.

Color-of-light research that *is* published is mostly in **children and preschoolers, not young infants**. A 2018 study of children (mean age 8.9 years) found greater melatonin suppression under 6200 K light than under 3000 K, a difference that did not reach significance in the adult group. A 2026 laboratory paper on 25 consumer nightlights reported that, at the product itself, only 3 of 25 stayed consistently under a 5 lx melanopic-EDI threshold used as a pediatric sleep benchmark; in a typical-use setup, 14 of 25 could get under that 5 lx mark on at least one setting, usually when they were red-toned, dim, and farther from the bed. Those authors recommend dim red or amber, off the direct line of sight, and away from the bed when you can. That is a product-measurement paper, not a night-long trial in cribs.

The AAP's Center of Excellence Q&A on light projectors (December 2025) is clearer on *brightness and duration* than on a required hue: **avoid bright light as bedtime approaches**; keep a projector to a **brief** settle (they give 15–20 minutes as an example), not hours; evening bright, cool-toned artificial light can suppress melatonin. They also say there is **no research** that treats a galaxy projector as the same thing as tablet or TV screen time.

### What we can source — and what we cannot

| Claim | What we can say | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dim is better than overhead at 2 a.m. | Sleep Foundation; AAP "avoid bright light close to bedtime" | Intensity matters more than brand |
| Red or amber is "required for babies" | **Not established** as an infant rule | Child and preschool studies, plus a 2026 product paper, support *lower melanopic / warmer* light in older children; do not treat that as a crib-specific law |
| Newborns cannot see red | Sleep Foundation's phrase | **Unverified** as a vision fact on this page |
| Projectors can run all night | AAP: brief soothing, not hours | Not the same evidence base as "screen time," and they say so |
| Any night light is harmless | 2026 paper: many products are bright at the source | Placement and the dimmest setting matter |

## How to use a light, practically

- For a night feed or diaper, keep the room as dim as you can still work in. That is the Sleep Foundation night-care point, and it is enough even if you never buy a "baby" lamp.
- If you want a warmer color, red or amber at the *lowest* setting, out of the baby's direct gaze and farther from the sleep surface, matches what the 2026 product paper found was more likely to stay under their 5 lx melanopic threshold. Many of those dim settings, the authors noted, would not actually help *you* see well.
- Do not aim a bright, cool, or flashing projector at the crib as all-night scenery. If a projector helps a short wind-down, treat it like the AAP's brief-soothe example and turn it down or off for sleep.
- A comfort lamp for a toddler who is afraid of the dark still belongs on the [fear of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark) page: dim, not a second daytime. Color choice can follow the child-study caution above; it is not a different protocol.

For how dark the room itself should be, types of darkening, and cordless window-covering safety, see [how dark should a baby's room be](/how-dark-should-babys-room-be). This page stays on the lamp.

There is no CPSC or AAP product standard that certifies a night light as "safe for infant circadian biology." Check only that the object itself cannot fall into the crib or overheat on a sheet — the bare-crib rule on [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) still applies.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if you are using a bright lamp all night because the baby seems unable to settle in dim light, or if you have a concern about vision, jaundice follow-up, or a preemie's lighting plan (those are clinical questions; see [preemie sleep](/preemie-sleep)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a red night light best for a baby?**  
Warmer, dimmer light is the better-supported direction in *children*. We do not have an infant trial that makes red mandatory. Sleep Foundation's red-bulb tip is for the caregiver during newborn night cares.

**Can I leave a galaxy projector on until morning?**  
The AAP Q&A's recommendation is a short settle, not hours. Bright, cool evening light is the part they warn against.

**Will a night light stop early waking?**  
Unlikely, if the room is already dim. Early waking has its own page: [early morning waking](/early-morning-waking).
