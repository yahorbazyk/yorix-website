# Baby Sleeping on the Stomach: When It Is and Isn't Safe

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Finding your baby on their tummy at 3 a.m. is one of the more unnerving moments of the first year. **Stomach sleeping** is a major, preventable SIDS risk if you *place* a young baby that way. It is a different question once a baby can roll both ways on their own. This page answers those two situations, and the related one: can a newborn start on their side? The rest of the safe-sleep checklist is on [safe sleep for babies](/safe-sleep-for-babies).

## Short answer

Until your baby's first birthday, **start every sleep on the back** — naps and nights. The AAP treats the **side position as just as dangerous as the stomach** for babies who cannot yet roll, because they can tip onto their tummy more easily from the side. If your baby can roll comfortably **both ways** (back to tummy and tummy to back), you do not need to keep flipping them when they roll during sleep, but you should still place them on their back at the start, in a clear crib with no loose bedding. Supervised [tummy time](/tummy-time) while they are awake is encouraged; it is not a sleep position.

## Why the back is safer

Stomach sleeping has been the standout preventable SIDS risk factor since the AAP's "Back to Sleep" campaign began in 1992. KidsHealth explains one of the leading theories: on the tummy, especially on a soft surface or with bedding near the face, a baby can end up "rebreathing" exhaled air, so oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises. A well-functioning brain would then trigger a rouse; in some babies that arousal may not happen reliably.

The side is not a compromise. The AAP is explicit that a baby on their side can roll onto their stomach more easily, and that the side is as risky as the front for infants who cannot yet roll both ways. Positioning wedges and "side-sleeper" pillows that claim to keep a baby in one position are unsafe and are not a solution; see also our page on [car seats, swings, and loungers](/baby-sleeping-in-car-seat).

Reflux does not change the starting position. HealthyChildren.org says even most babies with GERD should sleep **flat on their backs**. Doctors may make rare exceptions for specific airway problems; that is a clinician's call, not a parent experiment.

## What to do when you find them on their tummy

This is the question that brings most people to this page.

1. **If they cannot yet roll both ways,** turn them back onto their back. Keep doing that. Also look at the crib: nothing soft should be there to roll into. If they are still in a [swaddle](/how-to-swaddle-a-baby), stop the wrap now — a swaddled baby who lands face-down cannot easily roll back.
2. **If they roll both ways confidently,** you do not have to keep turning them all night. The AAP, KidsHealth, and NHS all use this rule. You still *place* them on their back at the beginning of the sleep.
3. **Until the first birthday,** do not put them down on their stomach or side "because they sleep better that way." Longer stretches are not worth the added risk.

Sleep Foundation's stomach-sleeping article sometimes frames "age 1" as the point when a child can begin stomach sleeping. That is about *placing* them, not about a baby who has already learned to roll. The more precise combined reading of AAP and KidsHealth is: back to start until 12 months; rolling both ways means you can leave their chosen position during that sleep.

## Newborns and the side-sleep myth

Newborns should not be started on their side, even with a rolled towel at the back. Those props are unneeded and unsafe. After the first hour of skin-to-skin, HealthyChildren.org says the baby should be placed on their back in a bassinet with no incline. Preemies may sleep prone temporarily in the NICU; once they are medically stable they should practice back sleeping before going home.

## Tummy time is still essential — while they are awake

Back sleeping increased "flat head" (positional plagiocephaly) in some babies, which is usually preventable and treatable. The answer is not stomach sleeping at night. It is supervised tummy time when the baby is awake, varying the direction they lie in the crib, limiting long stretches in seats and carriers, and holding them upright when you can. Our [tummy time](/tummy-time) guide covers minutes by age. Sleep Foundation, citing a review, mentions a daily total of at least 30 minutes as a later goal, built up from a few minutes at a time.

For the sourced prevention steps (alternating the crib orientation, limiting long stretches in seats, and the Caring for Kids note that a flat spot does not affect brain development), see [tummy time](/tummy-time#tummy-time-and-flat-spots-on-the-head). Do not start nights on the tummy or side to “round out” a head.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if your baby has a diagnosed airway or reflux condition and you have been given different sleep-position advice, if they seem to struggle to roll back and you are not sure they are ready to be left, or if you are worried about the shape of their head. Seek urgent care if breathing looks hard, or if they are unusually hard to wake.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it okay for my baby to sleep on their stomach?**  
Not as a starting position in the first year. If they rolled there themselves and can roll both ways, in a clear crib, you can leave them.

**Can a newborn sleep on their side?**  
No. The AAP treats the side like the stomach for babies who cannot roll.

**My baby only settles on their tummy. What should I do?**  
Place them on their back anyway. Work on other settling tools — a [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby), [wake-window](/wake-windows-by-age) timing, a [pacifier](/pacifier-and-sleep) if you use one — rather than a tummy-down start.

**Does a sleep positioner help?**  
No. The AAP and KidsHealth advise against wedges, positioners, and similar products. They have been linked to deaths and do not prevent SIDS.

**When can they sleep on their tummy every night on purpose?**  
After the first birthday, Sleep Foundation and NICHD-aligned advice say you no longer have to restrict them to their back. Many one-year-olds still start on their back out of habit, which is fine.

**I found my 2 week old sleeping on their side. Is that all right?**
Start every sleep on the back. If you find them on their side and they cannot yet roll both ways, return them to their back. The week-2 snapshot is on [2 week old baby sleep](/2-week-old-baby-sleep). Side-sleep is not a "newborn exception."

**Why does my baby sleep with their bum in the air?**
It is a common rolled position, not a separate diagnosis. Always **start** on the back, on a firm, flat, clear surface. If they can roll both ways and they get themselves into a rump-up position, the same rolling rule already on this page applies: you usually leave them if the space is clear and there are no loose blankets or pillows. If they cannot roll both ways yet, or the space is not clear, follow the stomach-sleeping advice above and talk to your pediatrician if you are unsure.

**Can a 4 month old sleep on their stomach if they roll over?**
Start every sleep on the back. If they roll to the tummy and can roll both ways, the rule already on [baby sleeping on stomach](/baby-sleeping-on-stomach) applies: leave them if the crib is clear, and stop swaddling. That is not a reason to *place* them on the tummy.
