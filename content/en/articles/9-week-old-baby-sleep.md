# 9 Week Old Baby Sleep: The First Week After the Clock May Start

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

This is a **week 9 snapshot**. It is not [8 week old baby sleep](/8-week-old-baby-sleep) (the short-nap, body-clock week), not [10 week old baby sleep](/10-week-old-baby-sleep), and not [2 month old sleep schedule](/2-month-old-sleep-schedule). We will not copy that month page's sample day.

## Short answer

You are still in the 0–3 month period. KidsHealth cites NSF's **14–17 hours** over a full day. HealthyChildren.org's "about 16–17 in pieces," the **11–19** band, and some NHS pages at about **8–18** have not been replaced by a week-9 official figure. This snapshot will not invent one.

The landmark that has *just become relevant* is the one Sleep Foundation places around **8–9 weeks**: a melatonin/cortisol rhythm starting to look more like a day–night clock. "Starting" is the word. It does not mean a 7 p.m. bedtime now holds every night, and it does not mean daytime naps suddenly last 90 minutes.

A social smile is expected **by 2 months** (HealthyChildren.org / CDC milestone language). Week 9 is past 8 weeks, so that check may already have been part of a two-month visit. A smile is not a settling method.

### The clock may have started — the hour total has not changed

- **Hours:** KidsHealth still cites NSF's 14–17 hours for the 0–3 month period. No official week-9 total sits on top of that.
- **What is new this week:** Sleep Foundation places a melatonin/cortisol rhythm around 8–9 weeks. This snapshot sits at the end of that window. "Started" is not the same as "finished."
- **A long night:** KidsHealth's 1–3 month use of "through the night" is often 5–6 hours, not 12.
- **Smile check:** a social smile is expected by 2 months (HealthyChildren.org / CDC). That is a development check, not a settling method.

## What is usually going on at nine weeks

Days may look slightly more "day-like" than week 6: more alert mornings, slightly more predictable first stretches. They may not. Feeds in the day are often still every 2–3 hours. If a longer night stretch has appeared, confirm whether you still need a planned feed.

Evening fuss may be easing for some babies at this age and still present for others. The longer 6-week fuss essay is not rebuilt here.

Short bassinet naps remain typical. The 8-week short-nap page already covers "only 30 minutes in the bassinet, longer in arms." This week asks a different question: now that the clock *may* have started, why are nights still uneven?

## A long stretch that may not stay

Forums often celebrate a first 7- or 8-hour stretch at this age — and then worry when the next night is ordinary fragments, or when someone asks whether a 9- or 10-hour stretch is "too long." Those worries shaped the examples below. They are not rates or official limits.

- **A first long stretch, then a messy night.** The clock starting does not lock in a 7-hour skill. Treat a long stretch as something that *can* happen at 1–3 months, and ask your pediatrician if you should still wake them.
- **"They slept 8–10 hours — should I have woken them?"** Use your weight-gain plan, not a forum average. KidsHealth's sourced meaning of "through the night" at this age is about 5–6 hours. A longer stretch can be all right when gain is good and your clinician has said so. If they are hard to wake or feeding has dropped, that is a clinical question — see [can a newborn sleep too much](/can-a-newborn-sleep-too-much) and [should you wake a sleeping baby](/should-you-wake-a-sleeping-baby).
- **Days still look like scraps.** A better night does not fix 30-minute naps on its own. See [8 week old short naps](/8-week-old-short-naps).

Safe sleep is unchanged.

## The first long stretch, then 4 a.m. (example only)

Not the 2-month timetable and not week 8's mixed-nap day. Suppose lights go down around 9:15 p.m. after a last feed, a first stretch lasts until about 3:40 a.m., a feed and a brief wide-awake spell follow, and then a second doze until a 6:50 a.m. morning. The next night the first stretch may end at 12:30 a.m. The sketch exists to show an uneven "clock has started" night, not to promise a 3:40 waking.

## After a very long stretch, and at follow-up

If a two-month follow-up is still on the calendar, ask whether a longer night stretch is all right and whether the social-smile milestone is on track. Do not wait for that visit if they are limp or very hard to rouse after a long sleep, if feeds have fallen off, or if breathing looks wrong. Use emergency services if they cannot be woken or cannot breathe comfortably.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a 9 week old sleep 8 hours at night?**
Some do, some nights. It is not an official target. Confirm wake-to-feed with your pediatrician. "Through the night" at 1–3 months often means 5–6 hours (KidsHealth).

**Is there a 9 week sleep regression?**
No official one. Uneven nights after a first long stretch are common in community discussions. Do not start formal training this week.

**Is this the same as the 2-month schedule page?**
No. That page is the month view, with its own sample day. This is the week-9 snapshot after the 8–9 week clock landmark.
