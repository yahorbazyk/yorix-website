# Baby Sleep Cues: How to Read Sleepy Signs

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Sleep cues are the small changes that mean "I could sleep soon." This page is a **short how-to for those signs** — early versus later, and why they are not the same in every baby. It is not a second [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age) chart (that page already has a short cue list and a paper calculator). It is not a rewrite of [overtired baby](/overtired-baby), which owns the plan for when the window has already been missed.

## Short answer

Cleveland Clinic and Sleep Foundation describe a familiar set of **early** signs: yawning, droopy eyelids or blinking more often, rubbing the eyes, staring off or looking "zoned out," frowning or grimacing, and a drop in activity or interest. Cleveland Clinic also lists furrowed brows, ear-pulling, finger-sucking, turning away from a feed, a face, or other stimulation, clinginess, and a low "grizzling" whine that is not yet a full cry.

**Later** signs can look like the opposite of sleepiness: louder, more frantic crying, arching, clenched fists, or a baby who suddenly seems "wired" (Cleveland Clinic; our overtired page uses the same contrast). Put the baby down when you see the early signs, not after the later ones, if you can (Cleveland Clinic).

No official body publishes a required checklist that every baby must show. Cleveland Clinic is clear that one child may use only a few of these signs, and a next child may use different ones. Missed cues are common. That is why [wake windows](/wake-windows-by-age) are a backup neighborhood, not a replacement for watching the baby.

### Early versus later signs

| Stage | What sources describe | What it is useful for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earlier (still settle-able) | Yawning, droopy or blinking eyes, eye-rubbing, staring off, frowning, quieter activity, turning away, a low whine | Start the wind-down and head for the crib or bassinet |
| Later (harder to settle) | Louder, frantic crying; arching; seeming "wired" or unexpectedly energetic | You may still get a nap, but it often takes longer. See [overtired baby](/overtired-baby) |
| Not a tired cue by itself | Hunger, a dirty diaper, pain, fever, or a new cough | Feed, change, or treat as a health question first |

Cleveland Clinic's tiredness article also mentions sweating and a rise in cortisol and adrenaline when a baby is *too* tired. We report that as **that page's clinical explanation**. Our [overtired](/overtired-baby) article already marked a precise, timed "cortisol spike" as **unverified** in the sources we used there, so we will not turn it into a stopwatch rule here either.

## How to use cues without watching the clock alone

1. Notice *your* baby's usual first sign over a few days — often a yawn, a stare, or a turn away — rather than waiting for a list of 15 to appear.
2. Begin a short, boring wind-down when that sign shows up, even if the clock is a little early.
3. If the same sign reliably appears at a similar stretch after waking, that stretch is useful context. The ranges and the paper method live on [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age).
4. If you usually only notice the later, "wired" version, move the wind-down earlier the next day rather than adding a new entertainment step at the crib.

Newborns cycle in and out of sleep so quickly that cues can be subtle and feeds come first. See [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule) and [should you wake a sleeping baby](/should-you-wake-a-sleeping-baby). Older babies may show clearer eye-rubbing and yawning; toddlers can look hyper when they are actually spent (Cleveland Clinic's exhaustion article for children). The *plan* for a late bedtime is on the overtired page.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if you cannot tell tiredness from pain or illness, if the baby is unusually hard to wake or rarely shows any sleepy signs, or if breathing, feeding, or weight gain worries you.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the first baby sleep cues?**  
Often yawning, droopy or blinking eyes, eye-rubbing, staring off, and turning away (Cleveland Clinic, Sleep Foundation). Your baby may use only some of these.

**Is crying a sleepy sign?**  
A low whine can be. A loud, frantic cry is more often a late, overtired sign, or a need (Cleveland Clinic). Check hunger and comfort first.

**Should I follow cues or wake windows?**  
Use both, with the baby ahead of the average. Cues time the moment; windows keep you in the right part of the day. Details of the ranges stay on the wake-window page.

**Are sleep cues the same as hunger cues?**  
No. Rooting, hands to the mouth with searching, and a cry that settles with a feed are feeding questions — see [baby feeding schedule](/baby-feeding-schedule). Some signs (finger-sucking, fussing) can appear in both; the last feed and the length of the wake stretch help you tell them apart.
