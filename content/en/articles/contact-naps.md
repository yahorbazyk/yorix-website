# Contact Naps: Safety Rules, Benefits, and How to Transition to the Crib

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A contact nap is any nap your baby takes on or against you: on your chest, in your arms, or snuggled in a carrier. For many babies, especially in the early weeks, it's the only nap that seems to work. Contact naps are not a bad habit, and they can be lovely. But they come with one firm safety rule, and if they become the only way your baby sleeps, they can wear you down. This guide covers both.

## Short answer

**Contact naps** are fine as long as **you stay awake and alert** the whole time. U.S. safe sleep guidance from NICHD's Safe to Sleep campaign, based on AAP recommendations, says couches and armchairs are **never safe places for babies to sleep** and are **extremely dangerous** if an adult falls asleep while holding a baby. If you might doze off, put your baby on their back in their own crib or bassinet. If you do fall asleep, move your baby to their own sleep space **as soon as you wake**. Carriers and slings are not meant for routine naps; if your baby falls asleep in one, move them to their sleep space as soon as possible. In the early months, holding your baby a lot won't spoil them (KidsHealth).

## Why babies love contact naps

Being held is calming. Newcastle Hospitals NHS lists cuddling, skin-to-skin contact, and being carried among the activities that help babies relax enough to sleep. The NHS describes skin-to-skin time as comforting for both parent and baby in the early weeks. KidsHealth notes that newborns who are held or carried more during the day tend to have less colic and fussiness.

For many families, contact naps are also simply practical: a way to get a newborn to nap at all, or to rest during a growth spurt or illness.

## The safety rules for contact naps

These rules come from NICHD's Safe to Sleep campaign and HealthyChildren.org (AAP):

1. **Stay awake.** A contact nap is safe only while you are alert. If you feel drowsy, put your baby on their back in their crib, bassinet, or play yard.
2. **Never on a couch or armchair if you might fall asleep.** NICHD calls these surfaces extremely dangerous when an adult falls asleep while feeding, comforting, or snuggling a baby.
3. **If you fall asleep anyway,** move your baby to their own safe sleep space as soon as you wake (NICHD; HealthyChildren.org).
4. **Carriers and slings are not nap spaces.** NICHD says not to use carrying devices for regular sleep. If your baby falls asleep in one, move them to their sleep space as soon as you can. The NHS advises using slings safely.
5. **Clear the area** of pillows and loose bedding when you feed or cuddle while tired (HealthyChildren.org).

### Tips to stay awake during a contact nap

HealthyChildren.org suggests ideas for tired parents during feeds that work for contact naps too:

- have a snack and water within reach;
- listen to or watch something that keeps your attention;
- set a phone alarm to go off every few minutes;
- ask another adult to stay with you.

## When contact naps work well

Contact naps can be a good choice when:

- your baby is a newborn and still sorting out days and nights;
- your baby is unwell or going through a rough patch and needs extra comfort;
- you are awake, rested enough, and happy to sit for a while;
- it's one nap of the day, and others happen in the crib.

## When contact naps become a problem

They may be worth gently changing if:

- **you can't stay awake** reliably while holding your baby;
- **every nap depends on you**, so you can't eat, rest, or care for others;
- **your baby wakes as soon as you move,** leaving very short naps;
- **night wakings need the same help.** HealthyChildren.org explains that babies who are held or rocked all the way to sleep may struggle to get back to sleep when they wake at night.

## How to transition from contact naps to the crib

There's no single proven method for this. The steps below combine sourced principles (drowsy-but-awake practice, a consistent routine) with practical suggestions from us.

### 1. Start with one nap

Pick the nap that tends to go best and try that one in the crib first. Keep the others as contact naps for now. *(Practical suggestion.)*

### 2. Use a short, repeatable pre-nap routine

A few steps every time, such as a diaper change, a dim room, a sleep sack, and a song, help your baby predict sleep. KidsHealth recommends consistent, soothing routines.

### 3. Practice "drowsy but awake"

HealthyChildren.org and KidsHealth suggest putting babies down while they're drowsy but still a little awake, so they learn to fall asleep in their own bed. Don't worry if it doesn't work every time.

### 4. Transfer gently if your baby falls asleep on you

Wait until they seem settled, then lay them on their back in the crib. Keep a hand on their chest for a moment if that helps. *(Practical suggestion.)*

### 5. Watch the timing

A baby who is overtired or not tired enough fights any nap, including in the crib. Our [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age) chart can help you pick the moment.

### 6. Be patient and consistent

Expect some resistance for a while. Keep offering the crib for the same nap each day.

## What can make contact naps riskier

- **Being exhausted.** It's easy to drift off holding a warm, sleeping baby.
- **Reclining on a sofa, armchair, or adult bed** with your baby.
- **Pillows, blankets, or cushions** around you and the baby.
- **Using a carrier as the main nap space.**

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Talk to your pediatrician if your baby only sleeps when held and seems unusually unsettled, cries inconsolably, feeds poorly, or has reflux symptoms or breathing noises during sleep. And if you're so tired that you are falling asleep holding your baby, ask for help from a partner, family, or your healthcare provider.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are contact naps safe?**  
Yes, as long as you stay fully awake. If you might fall asleep, put your baby on their back in their own crib or bassinet (NICHD Safe to Sleep).

**Will contact naps spoil my baby?**  
Not in the first months. KidsHealth says spoiling isn't a concern for young babies.

**My baby only naps on me. Is that normal?**  
Very common, especially for newborns. If it becomes hard to sustain, try one crib nap a day.

**How do I stop contact naps?**  
Gradually: start with one crib nap, keep a short routine, practice drowsy-but-awake, and watch awake times.

**Can my baby nap in a carrier?**  
Carrying is soothing, but NICHD says carriers and slings shouldn't be used for regular sleep. Move your baby to a safe sleep space when you can.
