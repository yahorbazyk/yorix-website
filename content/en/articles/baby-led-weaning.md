# Baby-Led Weaning (BLW): First Foods, Safety, and How to Start

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. Talk to your pediatrician before starting baby-led weaning, especially if your baby was born early, has trouble sitting, chewing, or swallowing, has severe eczema, or has a known allergy.*

Baby-led weaning means skipping (or mostly skipping) the spoon and letting your baby feed themselves soft pieces of real food from the first meal. Fans love the shared family meals and the mess-is-learning attitude; worried parents mostly ask one question: "Won't my baby choke?" This page explains how BLW works, when your baby is ready, which first foods to offer and **how to shape them**, how to tell normal gagging from choking, and how to make sure iron and allergens aren't missed. For general timing and purees, see [when to start solids](/when-to-start-solids).

## Short answer

**Baby-led weaning** is a way of starting solids in which your baby **picks up and eats soft finger foods themselves**, usually from **about 6 months**, once they can sit upright with little support, reach for food and bring it to the mouth, and no longer push everything out with the tongue. Offer **soft foods that squash between your fingers**, cut into **finger-length strips** (about the size of a small baby carrot), never round coins or hard chunks. Include **iron-rich foods** (meat, fish, eggs, beans, iron-fortified cereal) and **common allergens** early. A 2016 randomized trial of a modified BLW approach found **no more choking** than with spoon-feeding when parents were taught which foods to avoid. Always seat your baby upright in a high chair and stay with them for every bite.

## What is baby-led weaning?

The word "weaning" here means *introducing foods*, not stopping breastfeeding. In BLW:

- your baby sits with the family and is offered **small portions of the same meal**, adapted to be safe (AAP, Cleveland Clinic);
- **your baby decides** what to pick up, how much to eat, and when to stop;
- spoon-feeding isn't banned: you can preload a spoon with oatmeal or yogurt and hand it to your baby (AAP, Cleveland Clinic).

**Possible benefits** cited by the AAP and Cleveland Clinic include independence, earlier practice with textures and flavors, better self-regulation of appetite, fine-motor practice, and shared family meals. The AAP stresses that there are **very few studies** on the health benefits, so treat them as plausible rather than proven.

**Possible downsides** the AAP lists: some babies take in too few calories, self-chosen foods can be low in **iron** and other micronutrients, and choking is a real risk if food isn't prepared safely.

## When to start BLW: readiness signs

Most babies are ready around **6 months**, not earlier (Cleveland Clinic). Look for all of these:

- sits upright in a high chair with little or no support and steady head control;
- reaches for objects and brings them to the mouth;
- the tongue-thrust reflex is fading (food isn't automatically pushed out);
- shows interest in what you're eating.

The AAP advises waiting longer if your baby was born prematurely, has trouble sitting on their own, or struggles to chew and swallow even soft food. Cleveland Clinic suggests using **corrected age** for preterm babies and talking to your pediatrician or a speech-language pathologist if you're unsure.

## BLW first foods (6 months)

Pick foods that are **soft, easy to grip, not sticky, and served at room temperature** (Cleveland Clinic). A simple test: if you can squash it between your thumb and finger, it's soft enough *(practical tip)*.

| Food group | Good first options | How to serve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vegetables | Sweet potato, carrot, squash, broccoli | Steamed or roasted until very soft; long wedges or florets with a "handle" |
| Fruit | Ripe banana, avocado, mango, pear, kiwi | Banana in half-peel "handle" or thick strips; avocado in wedges; ripe pear or mango in long slices |
| Protein | Shredded or ground meat and fish, tofu, eggs | Shredded meat, meatballs or patties that crumble easily, omelet strips, well-cooked egg |
| Dairy | Plain whole-milk yogurt, cheese | Yogurt on a preloaded spoon; soft cheese in thin strips or grated |
| Grains | Iron-fortified infant cereal, oatmeal, well-cooked pasta, toast strips | Oatmeal on a preloaded spoon; pasta shapes that are large and soft |
| Legumes | Lentils, beans | Mashed or smashed, never whole beans (CDC) |

Sources for this table: AAP, Cleveland Clinic, CDC. Small round fruits such as grapes, berries, and cherry tomatoes must be **cut into small pieces** (CDC, Cleveland Clinic).

### How to cut BLW foods

- **Finger shapes, not coins.** Long strips your baby can hold in a fist with food sticking out of the top (AAP).
- **Round foods** (grapes, cherry tomatoes, blueberries): cut into small pieces (CDC). Round slices of hot dog or sausage are a classic choking hazard; avoid hot dogs and meat sticks altogether in the first year (AAP).
- **Hard foods**: cook until soft enough to mash with a fork (CDC).
- **Nut butters**: spread thinly on toast strips or stir into oatmeal; never a spoonful or a chunk (AAP, CDC).
- **As your baby develops a pincer grasp** (around 9–12 months; it is on CDC's 1-year checklist), you can offer smaller, bite-size soft pieces. The AAP recommends pieces no larger than ½ inch for young children.

## Foods to avoid in baby-led weaning

From CDC, AAP, and Cleveland Clinic lists:

- whole nuts and seeds, chunks or spoonfuls of nut butter;
- popcorn, chips, pretzels, crackers or bread with whole seeds or nut pieces;
- whole grapes, uncut berries or cherry tomatoes, whole corn kernels, raisins and other dried fruit;
- raw hard vegetables and fruit (raw carrot, celery, apple chunks);
- hot dogs, sausages, meat sticks, tough or large chunks of meat, bones in meat or fish;
- large chunks of cheese, especially string cheese;
- marshmallows, hard or gummy candy, chewing gum;
- honey before 1 year (CDC), salty foods, and added sugar;
- fish high in mercury (Cleveland Clinic);
- cow's milk or juice as drinks before 12 months (Cleveland Clinic).

## Gagging vs choking

Knowing the difference is the single most useful thing you can learn before starting.

| | Gagging | Choking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A protective reflex that pushes food forward | The airway is blocked |
| Sounds | Loud: coughing, retching, sputtering | Little or no sound; can't cry or cough effectively |
| Face | Red, watery eyes, tongue thrust forward | May turn bluish |
| What to do | Stay calm, keep your baby upright, let them work it out | Act immediately: infant first aid and call emergency services |

The choking signs come from the AAP: can't breathe, gasping or wheezing, can't talk, cry, or make noise, bluish color, panicked movements, going limp. In the BLISS trial, gagging was common in the early weeks and **fell faster** in BLW babies than in spoon-fed babies by 8 months. Huckleberry (app example) describes gagging as "loud and protective" and choking as quiet.

**Before you start:** the AAP recommends taking an infant **CPR and first-aid class** (American Red Cross or American Heart Association), so you know what to do if your baby chokes.

## Is baby-led weaning safe?

The best evidence is the **BLISS randomized trial** in New Zealand (Fangupo et al., *Pediatrics*, 2016): 206 healthy babies were assigned either to usual feeding or to a modified BLW approach in which parents got extra support and advice designed to reduce choking risk.

- About **35% of all babies choked at least once** between 6 and 8 months, and there was **no significant difference** between the BLW and spoon-fed groups.
- **Worrying finding:** at 7 and 12 months, **52% and 94%** of babies in both groups were offered at least one food that posed a choking risk during a 3-day record.

The takeaway: BLW done *with* safety guidance doesn't appear riskier than spoon-feeding, but **parents in both groups often offer unsafe foods**. The AAP also notes that some studies suggest BLW doesn't raise choking risk, while stressing that choking is a leading cause of accidental death in infants. Safety comes from the food shape and supervision, not from the method's name.

## Iron, calories, and allergens in BLW

- **Iron:** around 6 months, babies (especially breastfed ones) need iron from food (CDC). Offer an iron-rich food at most meals: soft meat, fish, egg, beans or lentils, or iron-fortified cereal on a preloaded spoon (AAP, CDC).
- **Calories:** babies who choose mostly fruit may not get enough energy (AAP). Keep offering breast milk or formula between or alongside meals.
- **Allergens:** the AAP says most babies should have small portions of peanut, dairy, and egg around 6 months, and you may need to "flex" BLW rules to make sure your baby actually eats them, for example thinned peanut butter on a preloaded spoon. See [introducing allergens](/introducing-allergens).

## BLW vs purees: do you have to choose?

No. The AAP says you can adapt the baby-led idea to your family; purees are nutritious too, more portable for travel, and a few days or weeks of spoon-feeding won't stop your baby from learning to feed themselves. Many families do a **mixed approach**: finger foods at family meals plus preloaded spoons of yogurt, oatmeal, or mashed lentils.

| | Baby-led weaning | Spoon-feeding purees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who controls the amount | Baby | Shared; follow baby's cues |
| Textures early on | Varied from day one | Smooth first, then lumpier |
| Mess | A lot | Less |
| Watch-outs | Iron, calories, safe shapes | Moving on to lumps and finger foods in time |

## When to talk to your pediatrician

- **Before starting**, especially if your baby was premature, has trouble sitting or swallowing, or has severe eczema or a food allergy (AAP).
- If your baby gags on every food after several weeks, coughs or chokes often, or seems to have trouble swallowing.
- If your baby isn't gaining weight well or eats very little after a few months of solids.
- **Call emergency services** if your baby shows choking signs that don't clear, or swelling of the face, trouble breathing, or repeated vomiting after a new food.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is baby-led weaning?**  
A way of starting solids in which babies feed themselves soft, finger-shaped pieces of family food instead of being spoon-fed purees.

**When can I start BLW?**  
At about 6 months, when your baby sits upright with little support, reaches for food, and no longer pushes everything out with the tongue.

**What are the best BLW first foods?**  
Soft, finger-shaped foods such as steamed sweet potato, avocado, banana, omelet strips, and soft shredded meat, plus iron-rich options.

**Is baby-led weaning safe?**  
In a randomized trial, BLW with safety guidance didn't lead to more choking than spoon-feeding. Safe food shapes and constant supervision matter most.

**Is gagging normal in BLW?**  
Yes. Gagging is a loud, protective reflex and usually decreases within a couple of months. Silent struggling, a bluish color, or no sound means choking.

**Can I combine BLW and purees?**  
Yes. The AAP supports adapting the approach; many families use both.
