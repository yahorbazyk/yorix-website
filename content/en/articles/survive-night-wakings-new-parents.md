# Baby Waking at Night: How to Get Through It (and Look After Yourself)

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

At 3 a.m., advice about "drowsy but awake" can feel like it was written for someone else. This guide is for the parent who is up again: what is normal at each age, how to tell hunger from a need for comfort, how to stay safe when you are exhausted, and how to protect your own sleep and mental health. It combines our earlier pages on night wakings and on parent stress.

## Short answer

Night waking is normal for babies. HealthyChildren.org (AAP) says newborns may wake **as often as every one or two hours**, and two NHS services describe most babies **not settling all night, every night, until about their first birthday**. In a Lullaby Trust survey quoted by the NHS, **59% of parents of babies under 12 months** said their baby slept less than 4 hours at a time. What helps most is a quick, calm, dim response; a sleep space that stays safe even when you are half asleep; shared night duties; and getting help early if you feel low or unable to cope.

## What's normal: night waking by age

| Age | What many babies do at night | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3 months | Wake every 1–3 hours, mainly to feed | Pregnancy, Birth and Baby; HealthyChildren.org |
| Around 3 months | Some manage a stretch of about 5 hours | Newcastle Hospitals NHS; Derbyshire NHS |
| 3–6 months | Some sleep 5–8 hours or longer, but not all | NHS |
| 4–5 months | Waking often increases again | Newcastle Hospitals NHS; Derbyshire NHS |
| By 6 months | Most sleep 9 hours or more at night, with brief awakenings | KidsHealth |
| 8–9 months | More waking again for some babies | Newcastle Hospitals NHS; Derbyshire NHS |
| About 12 months | Many are settled most nights, though plenty still wake | Newcastle Hospitals NHS |

If your baby wakes more than this table suggests, it doesn't automatically mean something is wrong. Ranges are wide. It is worth checking the daytime schedule, though: our [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age) chart can help.

## Hunger or comfort?

HealthyChildren.org says babies suck for comfort as well as hunger, and that it can be hard at first to tell the difference. Sometimes a baby just needs a cuddle or a diaper change.

| More likely hunger | More likely comfort or something else |
| --- | --- |
| Rooting, lip-licking, hands to mouth, opening mouth (HealthyChildren.org) | Settles quickly with a pat, a voice, or being held |
| A long gap since the last feed | Woke soon after a full feed |
| Feeds actively and then relaxes | Sucks briefly, then dozes |
| Newborn weeks, or a growth spurt | Older baby with the same waking time every night |

HealthyChildren.org also says crying is a **late** hunger sign. In the early months, feeding on demand is recommended for most healthy, full-term babies. Whether an older baby still needs a night feed is a question for your pediatrician, as it depends on growth and feeding.

## Getting through a night waking

### Respond early and keep it boring

Newcastle Hospitals NHS suggests going to your baby as soon as you can so they don't become very upset, because a distressed baby takes longer to settle. Keep lights low and the room quiet. HealthyChildren.org adds that night feeds should not be playtime.

### Try a calm sequence of soothing

HealthyChildren.org suggests building up slowly when your baby cries: look at them first, then use your voice softly, and only then move on to a pacifier, a light touch, or picking them up, pausing between steps.

### Put your baby back down before they're deeply asleep

HealthyChildren.org says babies who are held or rocked all the way to sleep may struggle to get back to sleep when they wake. Laying them down a little awake gives them practice.

### Decide on the plan before night falls

Agree in daylight what you will do at each waking: who goes, whether to feed, how long to try settling. Fewer decisions at 3 a.m. means less stress.

## Safe sleep when you're exhausted

Tired parents are more likely to fall asleep holding their baby. HealthyChildren.org warns that sleeping with a baby on a bed, sofa, or armchair raises the risk of sudden unexpected infant death, and recommends:

- **Avoid feeding on a couch or armchair.**
- **Clear the area of pillows and bedding** if you think you might doze off.
- **Use tools to stay awake:** a snack and water, a podcast, an alarm every few minutes, or another adult nearby while you feed.
- **If you do fall asleep while feeding,** move your baby onto their back in their own crib or bassinet as soon as you wake.
- **Keep the crib bare and place your baby on their back** for every sleep.

## Protecting your own sleep

The NHS and HealthyChildren.org offer practical ideas:

- **Rest when your baby sleeps**, even if the chores wait. Set an alarm if you worry about oversleeping (NHS).
- **Go to bed soon after the last evening feed** (HealthyChildren.org).
- **Split the night.** If you formula feed, a partner can share feeds. If you breastfeed, they can take nappy changes and early mornings, and once feeding is established, sometimes give expressed milk (NHS).
- **Ask friends or relatives** to watch the baby while you nap, or to stay for a few days if you are on your own (NHS).
- **Get outside daily.** The NHS notes that regular exercise, even a short walk with the pram, can help you feel less tired.

## Parent stress and mental health

Feeling exhausted and overwhelmed is common. HealthyChildren.org says caring for a new baby is one of the hardest things you'll do. But exhaustion can also hide something that needs treatment.

The NHS lists symptoms of postnatal depression including:

- finding it hard to enjoy anything;
- feeling hopeless, guilty, or unable to cope;
- constant worry or anxiety;
- **trouble sleeping even when you have the chance to rest**;
- difficulty bonding with your baby;
- thoughts of harming yourself or your baby.

The "baby blues" usually pass within two weeks of birth. If low mood lasts longer, gets worse, or you're struggling, the NHS says to speak to your GP, midwife, or health visitor, even if you have only some of the signs. Symptoms can start up to a year after birth, and partners can be affected too. Treatment helps.

### Where to get help

- **United States:** National Maternal Mental Health Hotline, **1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262)**: call or text, free, confidential, 24/7 (HRSA). It is not a crisis line. **In a crisis, call or text 988; in an emergency, call 911.**
- **United Kingdom:** your GP, midwife, or health visitor; Samaritans on **116 123** (NHS). In England, you can refer yourself to NHS talking therapies.
- **Anywhere:** if you feel you might harm yourself or your baby, put your baby somewhere safe, such as their crib, and call emergency services.

## What can make night wakings worse

- **A day with too little sleep.** KidsHealth notes that overtired babies often sleep worse at night.
- **A late last nap** that leaves your baby not tired enough at bedtime.
- **Bright lights, screens, and play** during night feeds.
- **A different response every night,** so your baby can't predict what happens.
- **Illness, teething, or a growth spurt.** These usually pass. See a doctor if your baby seems unwell.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Call your pediatrician if your baby has a fever (urgently if under 12 weeks), seems unusually sleepy or hard to wake, is feeding poorly or having fewer wet diapers, breathes with effort or pauses in breathing, snores loudly, or cries inconsolably in a way that is new. Mention night waking at checkups too, especially if it is wearing you down.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal for my baby to wake every 2 hours?**  
In the newborn months, yes: HealthyChildren.org says newborns may wake every 1–2 hours. In older babies, check daytime sleep and settling habits, and ask your pediatrician if you are worried.

**When do babies stop waking at night?**  
Many babies still wake in their first year. Two NHS services say most are about 1 year old before they settle all night, every night.

**How do I know if my baby is hungry at night or wants comfort?**  
Look for early hunger cues such as rooting and hands to mouth, and consider the time since the last feed. Babies also suck for comfort (HealthyChildren.org).

**How can I cope with sleep deprivation as a new parent?**  
Rest when your baby sleeps, share the night with a partner or helper, go to bed early, and ask for help (NHS; HealthyChildren.org).

**When is tiredness a sign of postnatal depression?**  
If you can't sleep even when your baby sleeps, or you feel hopeless or unable to cope, speak to a doctor (NHS).
