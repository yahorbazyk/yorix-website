# Colic in Babies: What It Is, How Long It Lasts, and What Actually Helps

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If your baby has a fever, is vomiting forcefully, isn't feeding, or the crying sounds different from usual, contact your pediatrician or emergency services.*

Hours of crying that nothing seems to fix, often at the same time every evening, are among the hardest parts of the newborn months. Colic is common, it ends, and it isn't caused by anything you did. Below: what doctors mean by colic, how it differs from normal evening fussiness, signs that point to something else, what parents can try (and which popular remedies lack evidence), and how to look after yourself so that a long evening never becomes a dangerous one.

## Short answer

**Colic** means a **healthy, well-fed baby** cries intensely and can't be soothed, usually for **3 or more hours a day, 3 or more days a week, for 3 or more weeks** (the "rule of 3s"). About **1 in 5 babies** have it (AAP). It typically begins in the first few weeks, is **worst around 6 weeks**, and usually eases by **3–4 months**, though it can last until about 6 months. The cause is unknown, and there is no cure, but motion, closeness, white noise, a pacifier, and feeding adjustments help some babies. Gas drops and gripe water lack good evidence. **Always rule out illness first**, and **never shake a baby**; if you're at your limit, lay your baby on their back in the crib and step away for a few minutes.

## What is colic?

"Colic" is a description, not a disease. It means a baby who is otherwise healthy, feeding and growing well, has repeated spells of hard crying with no obvious reason and is very difficult to calm (KidsHealth, Mayo Clinic). Between spells, the baby behaves normally.

During a spell, parents often notice (Cleveland Clinic, Mayo Clinic, KidsHealth, NHS):

- loud, high-pitched crying or screaming that sounds like pain;
- a red or flushed face, sometimes paleness around the mouth;
- clenched fists, stiff arms, legs pulled up to the belly, or an arched back;
- a tight or rumbling tummy and lots of gas.

The gas is real, but KidsHealth points out that it usually comes *from* swallowing air while crying rather than causing the colic.

## Normal evening fussiness, "witching hour", or colic?

All babies cry more in the first 3 months than at any other time (Mayo Clinic, KidsHealth). The AAP describes a **regular fussy period**, often between 6 p.m. and midnight, that **peaks at about 3 hours a day around 6 weeks** and falls to 1–2 hours a day by 3–4 months. If your baby settles within a few hours and is fairly calm the rest of the day, that's normal fussiness.

Some parents call this the **witching hour**; Huckleberry (app example) describes it as an evening fussy period starting around 2–3 weeks, worst near 6 weeks, and usually gone by 12 weeks. Colic is the more intense end of the same spectrum: crying that keeps building and is very hard to soothe (AAP).

The **Period of PURPLE Crying** program, cited by KidsHealth and Cleveland Clinic, explains this phase with an acronym: crying that **P**eaks, comes **U**nexpectedly, **R**esists soothing, looks **P**ain-like, is **L**ong-lasting, and is worse in the **E**vening. Cleveland Clinic says it usually starts at about 2 weeks and fades by 3–5 months.

## What causes colic?

No one knows for sure. Ideas researchers have considered (Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic, AAP) include:

- an immature digestive system or gut bacteria;
- sensitivity to something in formula or in the breastfeeding parent's diet (the AAP says this explains under 5% of colicky crying);
- feeding too much, too little, or not burping enough;
- a nervous system that finds it hard to calm down after stimulation;
- possibly an early form of migraine;
- family stress.

Mayo Clinic notes that the baby's sex, prematurity, breast- versus formula-feeding, and birth order have **not** been shown to raise the risk, while smoking during or after pregnancy may.

## Is it colic or something else? Red flags

Colic is diagnosed only after a doctor has checked that nothing else is wrong. Call your pediatrician promptly if your baby (KidsHealth, Cleveland Clinic, AAP):

- has a **fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher**: in a baby 3 months or younger, call right away;
- is **vomiting forcefully**, especially green or bloody vomit;
- has diarrhea or **blood in the stool**;
- is feeding poorly, sucking weakly, or has fewer wet diapers;
- is less alert or active than usual;
- isn't gaining weight, or is losing weight;
- has a cry that sounds **weak, high-pitched, or different** from usual: the NHS advises emergency care for this;
- cries mainly during or straight after feeds, arches, and spits up a lot, which can point to reflux (NHS lists reflux among other causes of crying);
- still has colic-like crying after 4 months (NHS).

You don't have to wait 3 weeks to see a doctor; Mayo Clinic recommends an appointment for colic symptoms at any point.

## What helps a colicky baby

There is no single fix. Try one change at a time for a few days. Ideas from the AAP, NHS, KidsHealth, Cleveland Clinic, and Mayo Clinic:

**Check the basics first**
- Is your baby hungry, wet, too hot or cold, or overtired?
- Burp more often during feeds; keep your baby upright while feeding to reduce swallowed air (NHS, KidsHealth).
- Don't overfeed. The AAP suggests waiting about 2–2½ hours from the start of one feed to the start of the next, if your pediatrician agrees.

**Motion and closeness**
- Walk with your baby in a carrier, rock, sway, or push the stroller (AAP, NHS).
- Hold your baby tummy-down across your knees and rub the back (AAP). Napper (app example) describes a similar "colic hold" along your forearm. If your baby falls asleep, move them onto their back in the crib.
- Skin-to-skin contact (Cleveland Clinic).
- A warm bath (NHS, KidsHealth).

**Sound and less stimulation**
- Steady background sound: a fan, white-noise machine, or the vacuum in the next room (AAP). Never put a baby on top of a washer or dryer. See our [white noise](/white-noise-for-baby-sleep) guide for safe volume and distance.
- Some babies settle with *less* input: a dim room and quiet (KidsHealth).
- Swaddle in a thin blanket (AAP), but stop as soon as your baby shows signs of trying to roll (KidsHealth).
- A pacifier (AAP).

**Feeding changes, only after talking to your pediatrician**
- *Breastfeeding:* AAP, Cleveland Clinic, and KidsHealth mention removing one food at a time (for example dairy or caffeine) for about 2 weeks. The NHS takes a different view and says you don't need to change your diet. Ask your doctor before cutting foods, so your own nutrition doesn't suffer.
- *Formula:* your pediatrician may suggest a short trial of an extensively hydrolyzed formula (AAP; Mayo Clinic mentions a one-week trial).

## Remedies with weak or no evidence

| Remedy | What sources say |
| --- | --- |
| Simethicone gas drops | AAP: studies suggest simethicone doesn't help colic; pediatricians increasingly advise against them. Cleveland Clinic still lists colic drops as something to try. |
| Gripe water | Not regulated by the FDA as a medicine, and no scientific evidence it is safe or effective (Cleveland Clinic). |
| Probiotics | Some studies of *Lactobacillus reuteri* showed less crying, but Mayo Clinic says most experts don't think the results support using it yet; NHS does not recommend probiotic supplements. |
| Herbal supplements | Not recommended; no evidence (NHS). |
| Spinal manipulation, cranial osteopathy | Little evidence and may hurt your baby (NHS). |

Always ask your pediatrician before giving any drops, supplements, or remedies to a young baby.

## Looking after yourself

Colic can wear down even the calmest parent, and Cleveland Clinic notes it has been linked with postpartum depression and with shaken baby syndrome. Plan for the hard evenings:

- **Take turns.** Hand over to a partner, relative, or friend, and leave the house for an hour if you can (AAP).
- **The safe-break rule.** If no one else is around and you feel overwhelmed, put your baby on their back in a crib with nothing else in it, close the door, and check back in about 10 minutes. Use that time to breathe, drink water, or wash your face (KidsHealth).
- **Never shake a baby.** Shaking can cause blindness, brain damage, or death (AAP).
- **Get help.** In the U.S., the Childhelp hotline 1-800-422-4453 (1-800-4-A-CHILD) is available any time if you're afraid you or someone else might hurt the baby (KidsHealth). In the UK, the NHS lists the Cry-sis helpline 0800 448 0737.
- **Tell your own doctor** if you feel low, anxious, or can't cope; postpartum depression is treatable. Our [night wakings survival guide](/survive-night-wakings-new-parents) has more on parent wellbeing.

## Colic and sleep

Colic spells often land right at bedtime, which makes the early weeks feel like a sleep problem. Keep the basics of safe sleep, use a simple bedtime routine, and expect things to get easier around 3–4 months. See our [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule) and [2 month old sleep schedule](/2-month-old-sleep-schedule) for realistic sleep expectations at this age.

## Gas at night

A gassy evening does not automatically mean the crying is caused by gas, and it is not a second diagnosis beside colic. This page already notes that babies often swallow air *while* they cry, so gas can be a result rather than the cause, and that simethicone and gripe water have weak or no good evidence for colic.

If your baby seems uncomfortable with a tight tummy in the evening:

- Offer a burp during and after feeds, and try a slightly more upright hold while they are awake and feeding (not an inclined *sleep* position — they still sleep [flat on their back](/safe-sleep-for-babies)).
- A diaper change and a brief, calm bicycle-legs or tummy-rub session while they are **awake** is reasonable. Do not put them to sleep on their tummy to "help gas pass."
- Noisy straining, especially with soft stools, can be ordinary infant dyschezia, not a trapped-gas emergency — see [newborn grunting in sleep](/newborn-grunting-in-sleep).
- A late, frantic feed can swallow more air. That is a routine and pacing issue, not a reason to prop the mattress.

Call the pediatrician if the belly looks swollen and tender, if there is repeated vomiting, blood in the stool, poor weight gain, or crying that does not fit the usual evening pattern. Those are not "just gas."

## Frequently asked questions

**What is colic in a baby?**  
Long spells of intense, hard-to-soothe crying in a healthy baby, usually 3+ hours a day, 3+ days a week, for 3+ weeks.

**What time of day is colic worst?**  
Usually late afternoon and evening, which is why parents search for "evening colic".

**How long does colic last?**  
It usually peaks around 6 weeks and ends by 3–4 months, occasionally lasting until about 6 months.

**Is colic caused by gas?**  
Not usually. Babies swallow air while crying, so gas is often a result rather than the cause.

**Do colic drops or gripe water work?**  
The evidence is weak. The AAP says simethicone doesn't appear to help colic, and Cleveland Clinic says gripe water isn't proven safe or effective.

**Can colic harm my baby?**  
Colic itself doesn't have long-term effects (Cleveland Clinic). The real danger is a caregiver at breaking point, so take breaks and never shake a baby.
