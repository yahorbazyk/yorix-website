# 4 Month Old Sleep Schedule: Naps, Bedtime, and How Much Sleep Is Enough

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Four months is often the first age at which a day starts to look like a plan: a morning nap, a midday nap, something in the afternoon, and a bedtime you can roughly predict. This guide gives you two example days (one with three naps, one with four), the daily sleep totals that experts recommend, and what to do when the plan falls apart by 10 a.m.

## Short answer

A **4 month old sleep schedule** usually has **3 or 4 naps**, a bedtime in the early evening, and one or more night feeds. On **how much a 4 month old should sleep**, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends **12–16 hours in 24 hours, including naps**, for babies aged 4 to 12 months, and says the AAP endorses this. KidsHealth puts typical daytime sleep at this stage at **about 3–4 hours**. Short naps of 30–45 minutes are common and not a sign that something is wrong.

## How much should a 4 month old sleep?

The AASM range is **12 to 16 hours per 24 hours**, counting naps and night sleep together. Derbyshire Community Health Services (NHS) uses the same range from around 4 months. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby, Australia's government parenting service, gives a slightly narrower **12 to 15 hours** for 3–6 months, and Tresillian says most babies of this age sleep about 14 or 15 hours.

A range this wide exists because babies genuinely differ. Derbyshire's advice is a good test: if your baby is growing and is alert and happy when awake, they are very likely getting enough sleep.

Baby apps publish their own targets, and they don't agree with each other. As **examples only**: Huckleberry suggests aiming for about 14.5 hours with 3–4 naps, while Napper describes 12–15 hours with 2–3 naps. Both sit inside the AASM range, which is the number to go by.

### 3 naps or 4: how to choose

Look at nap *length*, not the calendar. If most naps end at 30–45 minutes, your baby builds up sleepiness again sooner and usually needs a fourth nap to reach bedtime without a meltdown. If one or two naps reliably run over an hour, three naps tend to fit. Many babies switch back and forth for a few weeks, and a mix of 3-nap and 4-nap days in the same week is normal.

## 4 month old wake windows

How long can a 4 month old stay awake? Cleveland Clinic gives **1.25–2.5 hours** for 3–4 months, and Sleep Foundation gives **1–3 hours** for 1–4 months. Start at the shorter end, especially for the first window of the day and after a short nap. Watch for yawning, eye rubbing, staring off, or fussing, and begin your wind-down then.

The apps disagree on which window is longest. Huckleberry describes morning windows as usually the shortest, while Napper says the first window is often the longest. That disagreement is a good reminder to follow your baby rather than a rule. Our [wake windows by age chart](/wake-windows-by-age) compares the sources in one table.

**Wake windows for a 4 month old, side by side:**

| Source | Wake window at about 4 months |

On a four-nap day the windows sit near the short end; once your baby moves toward three naps they stretch toward 2 hours or a little more *(practical suggestion)*. If a 4 month old wake window of 2 hours ends in tears every time, shorten it by 15 minutes and watch for a week.

## Short naps at 4 months: why they happen

Tresillian, an Australian early-parenting service, explains that at 4–5 months a baby's sleep cycle lasts around **30–50 minutes**, and that it's very common to wake after **30–40 minutes**. In other words, a single cycle and then awake is developmentally ordinary.

What can help a nap last longer:

- Offer the nap before your baby is overtired.
- Keep the wind-down short and identical: same room, same song, same sleeping bag.
- If your baby stirs at the half-hour mark, pause briefly before going in. Mayo Clinic suggests that from about 4 months, you can check on a baby who cries after being put down, offer soothing words, and give them a little time to settle.
- Accept that some naps will stay short. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby notes naps at 3–6 months last *up to* 2 hours, so anything shorter is still within the normal spread.

## Bedtime for a 4 month old

No official body sets a bedtime for this age. In practice, bedtime follows the day: the time of the last nap, plus a wake window, plus a short routine. After a day of short naps, moving bedtime earlier is usually easier than stretching a tired baby to the "normal" time. As an **app example**, Huckleberry describes 6:00–8:00 p.m. as a common range.

KidsHealth encourages a soothing, repeatable routine at this age and putting your baby down drowsy but still awake, so they practice the last step of falling asleep in their own bed. HealthyChildren.org (AAP) makes the same point.

## Night feeds and night waking at 4 months

Many 4-month-olds still feed once or more overnight, and that is expected. Newcastle Hospitals NHS notes that some babies sleep around 5 hours at a stretch by 3 months, but many begin waking more again at 4–5 months. Derbyshire NHS adds that babies do not routinely sleep through the night until around age 1, and that by 5 months some may sleep up to 8 hours.

If wakings suddenly multiply, our page on the [4 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/4-months) explains what changes in sleep around this age. Before you blame a regression, rule out hunger, illness, and discomfort.

## Swaddling, pacifiers, and rolling

- **Swaddling:** stop as soon as your baby shows signs of trying to roll (HealthyChildren.org / AAP). KidsHealth notes rolling typically starts around 3–4 months, so this is often the age to move to a wearable sleep sack.
- **Pacifier:** KidsHealth says it is fine to offer one at sleep time without forcing it, and you don't need to put it back in if it falls out once your baby is asleep.
- **Rolling over in sleep:** keep placing your baby on their back. If they can roll both ways on their own, HealthyChildren.org says you don't need to keep turning them back, as long as the crib is clear.

## What can make a 4 month schedule harder

- **Copying clock times from someone else's baby.** The examples above are anchored to a wake-up time, so if yours woke at 7:40, everything shifts.
- **A late, long last nap** that leaves no sleep pressure for bedtime.
- **Changing several things at once:** new room, new routine, and dropping a nap in the same week.
- **Bright, busy evenings.** Keep the last hour calm and dim.
- **Unsafe sleep fixes:** inclined sleepers, extra blankets, pillows, or weighted products. HealthyChildren.org says a surface that inclines more than 10 degrees is not safe for infant sleep.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Call if your baby is feeding less, has fewer wet diapers, has a fever, breathes noisily or with effort during sleep, is hard to wake, or if you are simply worried. It's also worth asking before you start any formal sleep training. Our [sleep training methods guide](/sleep-training-methods-explained) explains the options.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much should a 4 month old sleep?**  
12–16 hours in 24 hours, naps included (AASM, endorsed by the AAP).

**How many naps does a 4 month old take?**  
Usually 3 or 4. KidsHealth says at least two at this stage; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby gives 2–3 for 3–6 months. Short naps often mean an extra nap is needed.

**What is a good bedtime for a 4 month old?**  
Whatever time follows the last nap plus a wake window, often early evening. Move it earlier after a day of short naps.

**Is it normal for my 4 month old to nap only 30–45 minutes?**  
Yes. Tresillian notes waking after 30–40 minutes is very common at 4–5 months.

**Should a 4 month old still eat at night?**  
Often yes. Many babies wake for feeds well beyond 4 months, and NHS services say sleeping through the night routinely comes closer to age 1.

**When should I stop swaddling?**  
When your baby starts trying to roll (HealthyChildren.org / AAP).
