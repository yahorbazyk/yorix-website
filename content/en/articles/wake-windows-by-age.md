# Wake Windows by Age: A Chart From Newborn to 12 Months

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A **wake window** is simply the stretch of time your baby spends awake between one sleep and the next. Parents look up **wake windows by age** because timing a nap a little too early or too late can turn an easy settle into a long one. Below you will find the published ranges side by side, why they disagree, and a simple way to work out your own baby's number.

## Short answer

Wake windows grow from well under two hours in the first weeks to several hours by the end of the first year. Sleep Foundation gives **0.5 to 1.5 hours for 0–1 month** and **1 to 3 hours for 1–4 months**; Cleveland Clinic gives **0.5 to 1 hour from birth to 1 month** and **3 to 6 hours at 10–12 months**. These are rough guides from clinicians and health writers, not an official standard: we found no wake-window table from the AAP or the AASM. Your baby's tired cues should win whenever the clock and the baby disagree.

### Key numbers at a glance

Two medically reviewed or clinician-quoted sources publish a full table. They overlap, but not perfectly, so we show both rather than inventing a single "right" number.

**How to read this chart.** The upper ends are very wide on purpose. A 9-month-old who is happy after five hours awake is inside Sleep Foundation's range; another who melts down at three hours is still normal. Start near the lower end for your baby's age and move up only if your baby keeps resisting sleep.

## Why wake-window numbers differ between sources

You will see different figures on almost every site, and that is not because one of them is wrong. A few reasons:

- **No formal guideline exists.** The AASM recommends total daily sleep (12–16 hours for 4–12 months), not how long a baby should stay awake between sleeps. Wake-window charts come from clinical experience and are summaries, not research cut-offs.
- **Age bands are drawn differently.** Sleep Foundation groups 1 to 4 months together; Cleveland Clinic splits that into two bands.
- **Days are uneven.** Sleep Foundation notes that a baby's wake windows can change from one day to the next, and Cleveland Clinic says they can even vary from one nap to the next.

Baby-app makers publish their own charts too. These are useful as examples of how others apply the idea, but they are not clinical sources (see the next table).

## Wake windows at 3, 4, 5, and 6 months

This is the age when most parents start thinking in wake windows, and when the published numbers diverge the most. The first two columns are clinical sources; the last two are **app examples only**, included so you can see the spread.

| Age | Cleveland Clinic | Sleep Foundation | Huckleberry (app example) | Napper (app example) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 months | 1–2 h (1–3 mo) or 1.25–2.5 h (3–4 mo) | 1–3 h | 1–2 h | 75–120 min |
| 4 months | 1.25–2.5 h | 1–3 h | 1.5–2.5 h | 75–120 min |
| 5 months | 2–4 h | 1.5–4.5 h | 1.5–2.5 h | 2–3 h |
| 6 months | 2–4 h | 1.5–4.5 h | 2–3 h | 2–3 h |

What this tells you in practice: at 3–4 months, roughly one to two and a half hours is where all four overlap. By 6 months, two to three hours is the shared middle. If your baby sits outside that middle but is feeding well, growing, and mostly content, there is no reason to force the number.

## Newborn wake windows (0–3 months)

In the first weeks, a baby may manage only a feed, a diaper change, and a cuddle before needing sleep again. Sleep Foundation describes newborn windows of as little as 30–45 minutes and as much as 90 minutes. Mayo Clinic puts it a little differently: in the first month, a newborn awake for one to two hours most often needs to sleep again.

Two things are different at this age:

1. **Don't try to stretch them.** Sleep Foundation says there is no need to extend a newborn's wake windows to get longer sleep, because most infants lengthen them on their own. KidsHealth adds that keeping a baby up during the day in the hope of better nights tends to backfire, since overtired infants often sleep worse.
2. **Feeding sets the rhythm.** Hunger drives newborn sleep far more than the clock. If you want the broader picture of the first weeks, read our [newborn sleep schedule guide](/newborn-sleep-schedule).

## Does feeding count as awake time?

Yes, in most definitions. Sleep Foundation defines a wake window as starting when your baby wakes and ending the next time they fall asleep, so a feed in between is part of the window. If your baby dozes off at the breast or bottle, that drowsy stretch is where the window ends.

## Tired cues: when the window is closing

Numbers get you into the right neighborhood; cues tell you the exact moment. Sleep Foundation lists crying, yawning, eye rubbing, blinking more often, and staring off to the side. Cleveland Clinic says activity levels usually drop as a baby gets sleepy. Tresillian, an Australian parenting service, adds grimacing, frowning, clenched fists, jerky movements, and sudden overactivity.

Over a few days you will usually notice your baby's own signal. Cleveland Clinic gives this example: if a 4-month-old reliably shows sleepy signs about two hours into a window, lay them down a few minutes before that point.

## A simple wake window calculator you can do on paper

There is no formula that predicts every nap, but this method turns the chart into a starting point for *your* baby:

1. **Write down when your baby woke up.** Count from the moment they woke, not from when you got them out of the crib.
2. **Pick the lower end of the range for their age** from the chart above, for example about 1.25 hours at 4 months using Cleveland Clinic's band.
3. **Start a wind-down about 10 minutes before that time**, so your baby is in a calm, dim space as the window closes.
4. **Adjust after a short nap.** If the last nap was much shorter than usual, aim for the earlier side of the next window.
5. **Note what actually happened for three or four days**: when cues appeared, how long settling took, and how long the nap lasted. Patterns show up quickly.

*Worked example (illustration only):* a 4-month-old wakes at 6:45 a.m. Starting from 1.25 hours, the first nap target is about 8:00 a.m., with the wind-down starting around 7:50. If the baby shows sleepy cues at 7:40, follow the baby, not the math.

## What can make wake windows go wrong

- **Treating the top of the range as a goal.** Upper limits are where *some* babies cope, not where every baby should be.
- **Ignoring the previous nap.** A 25-minute nap rebuilds less rest than a long one, so the next window usually needs to be shorter.
- **Over-stimulation at the end of the window.** Bright screens, visitors, or rough play just before sleep make settling harder.
- **Unsafe shortcuts.** Letting a baby finish a nap in a swing, bouncer, or car seat is a common way to "fix" a mistimed window. HealthyChildren.org (AAP) advises moving a baby who falls asleep in a sitting device to a firm, flat surface as soon as you can.

## When wake windows stop mattering

Sleep Foundation notes that many parents stop counting wake windows once their child is down to one or two naps, and that most children nap two times a day or fewer between 12 and 18 months. At that point, a steady nap time and bedtime usually matter more. See [baby nap schedule by age](/baby-nap-schedule-by-age) for how naps drop over time.

Sleep regressions can also throw the numbers off for a while. During one, Sleep Foundation says windows may suddenly get shorter or longer.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Sleep Foundation suggests checking in if your baby regularly sleeps a lot more or less than the recommended total for their age, or if you notice snoring or thrashing during sleep. Call sooner if your baby is unusually hard to wake, is feeding poorly, has fewer wet diapers, or simply seems unwell.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a wake window?**  
The time your baby stays awake between two sleeps, from waking up until falling asleep again.

**How long should wake windows be at 4 months?**  
Cleveland Clinic gives 1.25–2.5 hours for 3–4 months; Sleep Foundation gives 1–3 hours for 1–4 months. More detail is in our [4 month old sleep schedule](/4-month-old-sleep-schedule).

**What are normal newborn wake windows?**  
Roughly 30 minutes to 1.5 hours in the first month, according to Sleep Foundation and Cleveland Clinic.

**My baby gets tired before the window ends. Should I keep them up?**  
No. Sleep Foundation's advice is to look at the baby's cues; the chart is only a starting point.

**Are wake windows backed by research?**  
Only loosely. Official recommendations cover total sleep per day, not awake time between naps, so wake-window charts are experience-based guides.

**Is there a wake window calculator?**  
The paper method above works for most families. Apps such as Yorix can do the counting from your logged sleep.
