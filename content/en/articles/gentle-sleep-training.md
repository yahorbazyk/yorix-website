# Gentle Sleep Training: Chair, Fading, and Pick Up / Put Down

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

The comparison of sleep-training methods already lives on [sleep training methods explained](/sleep-training-methods-explained). [Ferber](/ferber-method-baby-sleep) and [cry it out](/cry-it-out-method) have their own how-to pages. This article is the **practical walk-through of the slower, stay-close approaches** people usually mean by "gentle" or "no-cry": the chair method (camping out), pick up / put down, and bedtime fading. It is not a rewrite of the comparison table, and it is not a new age-sliced training page for 5, 6, or 9 months — that job stays on [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training).

## Short answer

"Gentle sleep training" is not one official protocol. It is a family of plans that keep the parent nearby or keep responses fairly prompt, and that change sleep habits over days or weeks rather than over a few nights. Sleep Foundation notes that these slower approaches can still involve crying, and that they often take longer than cry-it-out or Ferber — fading or camping out may run up to about two weeks in that description. Cleveland Clinic describes pick up / put down as more time-consuming and demanding than those two methods.

None of these plans is for a newborn. Sleep Foundation, in line with our readiness page, places formal training after about 4 to 6 months, with many clinicians more comfortable around 6 months. If your baby is younger, unwell, or still needs frequent night calories, wait and talk with the pediatrician. A consistent [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby) and putting the baby down drowsy, as HealthyChildren describes from about 4 months, is a reasonable foundation without picking a branded method.

### Which plan is which? (and a naming conflict)

Sources do not all use "fading" the same way. We keep the split our methods page already uses, and we flag the clash:

| Name we use here | What you actually do | What sources call it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chair method / camping out | After the routine, stay in the room and gradually sit farther from the crib over nights | Sleep Foundation treats "fading" and "camping out" / the chair method as the same family. KidsHealth New Zealand describes camping out for **primary-school** children, so we do not copy that night-by-night distance plan as a baby protocol |
| Pick up / put down | Pick up to calm, put down still awake, repeat | Cleveland Clinic; Sleep Foundation (often discussed for roughly 4–8 months) |
| Bedtime fading | Temporarily move bedtime closer to when the child actually falls asleep, then ease it earlier | A different idea from "fade your presence." Our methods page notes support in an AASM review and a 2016 trial. KidsHealth New Zealand also describes bedtime fading, including for older children |

There is no single AAP method sheet for any of these. The steps below are high-level, sourced where a clinical site describes them, and should be adapted with your pediatrician.

## Before you start

Use the readiness and "when not to start" advice on [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training). In short: the baby should be past the newborn period, you should have a feeding plan for the night, and you should not begin in the middle of an illness, a big household change, or a situation your clinician wants you to wait on. If the issue is mainly a strong feed-to-sleep habit, read [nursing to sleep](/nursing-to-sleep) and [sleep associations](/sleep-associations) first — you may only need a slow change to the start of the night, not a full formal method.

Agree the plan with every caregiver. Gentle methods fail most often when one adult sits in the chair and another rocks to sleep the next night.

## Chair method (camping out), in outline

Sleep Foundation's description: go through the bedtime routine, put the baby down drowsy, and stay nearby until they fall asleep. Over subsequent nights, move yourself a little farther — for example from beside the crib, then farther across the room, then toward the door — while offering only limited talking or touching. The parent remains in view for a stretch of this process.

Expect some protest. You are visible, which some families find easier than leaving the room, and which can also make it harder to stay quietly consistent. Sleep Foundation says this usually takes longer than leaving-the-room methods. KidsHealth New Zealand's camping-out page is written for school-age children who can be told "this is sleep time, not play time," and it mentions a range of about 7 to 21 nights; treat that as context for older children, not as a baby guarantee.

If the baby is asleep and later wakes, return to the place you had reached in the plan and stay until they settle again, rather than starting from zero with rocking or a feed that is no longer needed. Needed feeds still happen.

## Pick up / put down, in outline

Cleveland Clinic describes a loop: when the baby fuss or cries after you put them down, pick them up to soothe, then put them back down **before** they fall asleep in your arms, and repeat as needed. The aim is that the last moment of falling asleep happens in the crib, not on your chest. Sleep Foundation places this approach especially in a mid-infancy window (it cites about 4 to 8 months) when babies can begin to practice independent settling but still respond to being held.

The honest difficulty, which Cleveland Clinic also flags, is the number of repetitions. If crying starts the moment the back touches the mattress, you may be picking up and putting down many times in one evening. That is why some families find it "gentler" in theory and exhausting in practice. It is a valid choice; it is not automatically easier.

Do not use this as a wrestling match with a baby who is ill, very hungry, or too young. Put the baby down on their back, in a clear crib, every time.

## Bedtime fading, in outline

This is a clock change, not a presence change. If a child is taking a long time to fall asleep, a temporary later bedtime — closer to their actual sleep-onset time — can reduce long, frustrated evenings (KidsHealth New Zealand; our methods page). After they are falling asleep more easily, shift bedtime earlier in small steps (our methods page mentions about 15 minutes every few days as a typical pattern). Keep the morning wake time reasonably steady, or the whole day slides.

Bedtime fading can be combined with a chair plan, but you do not have to do both. It is often useful for toddlers as well as older babies.

## What "no-cry" does and does not promise

Marketing language sometimes implies that a gentle method means no tears. Sleep Foundation is clearer: crying is still common, especially with chair / fading, because the baby can see you and still wants the old pattern. The difference is how you respond, and how slowly you change the pattern — not a guarantee of silence. If a plan only works when you rock or feed the baby all the way to sleep every night, it is a [sleep association](/sleep-associations) you have kept, not a training method you have finished.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask first if your baby was premature (use [corrected age](/preemie-sleep)), has reflux, poor weight gain, or breathing concerns, or if you are unsure whether night feeds are still needed. Stop and call if the baby seems unwell, or if sleep is getting worse in a way that does not match a short adjustment period.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is gentle sleep training effective?**  
Sleep Foundation says several methods can work and that the better fit is the one the family can use consistently. Direct trial evidence is thinner for chair and pick up / put down than for some leaving-the-room methods — our methods page already records that. "Gentle" is a preference and a pace, not a separate proof of superiority.

**Will my baby still cry?**  
Often, yes, at least some. The selling point is a closer parent and a slower change, not zero crying.

**Can I combine this with Ferber later?**  
Some families start gentle and later switch. If you do, pick one plan at a time and give it a fair stretch, rather than changing rules every night. See [Ferber](/ferber-method-baby-sleep) if you need that how-to.

**Is this appropriate for naps?**  
Naps are often harder. A short, consistent try at the same approach can be reasonable once nights have a plan; a dedicated nap-training article is not what this page is. See [baby won't nap](/baby-wont-nap) and [short naps](/short-naps) for daytime sleep problems.
