# Dream Feed: What It Is, How to Do It Safely, and When to Stop

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A dream feed is one of the most popular baby sleep "hacks," and one of the least studied. The idea is simple: instead of waiting for your baby to wake you at 1 a.m., you offer a feed at around 10 or 11 p.m., while they are still mostly asleep, and then go to bed yourself. Some parents swear by it; for others it changes nothing. Below we explain what a dream feed is, how to do one safely, when to start and stop, and what the small amount of research really shows.

## Short answer

A **dream feed** is a late-evening breast or bottle feed given to a sleeping or very drowsy baby, usually just before the parents go to sleep, to line up the baby's longest stretch with theirs. The only randomized trial we found tested a **package**: a "focal feed" between 10 p.m. and midnight *plus* gradually stretching the gaps between night feeds and making nights clearly different from days. Breastfed babies in that program slept longer at night (Pinilla & Birch, 1993), and a Cochrane review called this "some evidence" based on that single small trial. Nobody has studied when to stop. If you try it, **hold your baby semi-upright for a bottle, never prop a bottle or feed lying flat, never leave a bottle in the crib, and lay your baby back down on their back.**

## What is a dream feed?

A dream feed (also called a focal feed) is a feed you start rather than your baby. Typically your baby goes to bed at around 7–8 p.m., and a couple of hours later, before you go to bed, you lift them, feed them while they're drowsy, and lay them back down. The goal is that the next waking comes later, closer to the time you'd want to be up anyway.

The term isn't medical. Napper (app example) describes it as a method developed by sleep coaches rather than a standard recommendation. In research, the same idea appears as a "focal feed" between 10 p.m. and midnight.

## Do dream feeds work? What the research shows

The evidence is thin, and it's worth knowing exactly what it says:

- **One small trial of a whole program.** Pinilla and Birch (1993) randomly assigned 26 first-time parents of exclusively breastfed babies to a program or to usual care. The program had three parts: a focal feed between 10 p.m. and midnight, gradually lengthening the gaps between middle-of-the-night feeds with other care (re-swaddling, diapering, walking), and maximizing the difference between day and night. By 3 weeks the babies had longer night sleep episodes, and by 8 weeks all 13 program babies "slept through" from midnight to 5 a.m., compared with 23% of the control group. Their total 24-hour milk intake didn't differ; they simply drank more in the early morning.
- **What that does and doesn't prove.** Because the focal feed was tested together with the other two parts, the trial can't show how much the late feed alone contributed. "Sleeping through" was defined as midnight to 5 a.m., not the whole night.
- **Cochrane review.** Renfrew and colleagues (2000) found only one trial that met their criteria (33 couples) and concluded there is "some evidence" that a structured program can help first-time parents teach breastfed babies to sleep through the night by 8 weeks.
- **An observational study.** In the Rise & SHINE cohort of 313 infants, bottle feeding at bedtime at 1 month was associated with a 62-minute longer longest night stretch at 6 months (Quante et al., 2022). This was not a trial, and a bedtime bottle isn't exactly a dream feed, so it can't prove cause and effect.

**Bottom line:** a late feed may help some families shift the longest stretch to fit their own sleep, especially as part of a broader day-night routine. It's reasonable to try for a week or two; if it doesn't help, nothing is lost by stopping.

## When to start a dream feed (and dream feeds for newborns)

There's no official starting age. In the trial above, the program began in the first weeks after birth. The app examples (not clinical guidance) vary:

- **Huckleberry:** most commonly used for babies between about 6 weeks and 10 months, with 3–4 months a popular time to start.
- **Napper:** most babies develop a day-night rhythm between 3 and 6 months, a good time to try; earlier is fine too.

**Dream feeds for newborns.** In the first weeks, frequent feeding is normal: KidsHealth says breastfed newborns feed about every 2–3 hours and bottle-fed newborns every 3–4 hours, and that a baby should be woken to feed every 3–4 hours until they show good weight gain. A dream feed doesn't replace that. If your baby was born early, had slow weight gain, or has a medical condition, ask your pediatrician before planning night feeds around your own sleep.

## How to dream feed, step by step

1. **Pick a time.** Usually 2–3 hours after bedtime and just before you go to bed, often between about 9 and 11 p.m. (Napper, app example). Keep it the same each night for a week *(practical suggestion)*.
2. **Keep the room dark and quiet.** Lift your baby slowly. Change the diaper only if it's dirty or very wet.
3. **Feed in a safe position.**
   - **Bottle:** hold your baby in your arms, semi-upright, with their head in the crook of your arm. HealthyChildren.org warns that feeding a baby lying on their back increases the risk of choking and can let milk run into the ears' eustachian tubes, possibly causing ear infections. Never prop the bottle.
   - **Breast:** feed sitting up in a chair or bed where you're unlikely to doze off *(practical suggestion)*. If there's any chance you'll fall asleep with your baby in your bed, the AAP says to remove pillows and blankets first and move your baby back to their own sleep space as soon as you wake. Never feed on a couch or soft armchair if you might fall asleep: the AAP says the risk of sleep-related death is up to 67 times higher there.
4. **Make sure your baby is actually feeding.** Napper (app example) advises making sure your baby is awake enough to suck and swallow actively before bottle feeding.
5. **Burp if your baby usually needs it.**
6. **Lay your baby down on their back** in their own crib or bassinet, with nothing else in it (AAP).

## Is dream feeding safe?

Dream feeding is not dangerous in itself if the feed and the sleep space are safe. The main risks come from **how** it's done:

- **Bottle feeding lying flat** (choking and ear infection risk) — HealthyChildren.org.
- **A bottle left in the crib** — HealthyChildren.org says never to put your baby in the crib with a bottle for comfort, because of tooth decay and ear infections.
- **Falling asleep with your baby** on a couch, armchair, or in an adult bed — AAP.

One physician resource for breastfeeding (Physician Guide to Breastfeeding) goes further: it says giving a large bottle of expressed milk (for example 5–6 ounces) to a **sleeping** baby isn't medically recommended, because drinking reflexively from a bottle while asleep is different from breastfeeding. It suggests that a partner gives a bottle when the baby wakes naturally instead. That's one expert view rather than an AAP policy, but it's a good reason to make sure your baby is drowsy and actively feeding rather than fully asleep. (The same page mentions bed-sharing; the AAP recommends against it, and we follow the AAP.)

## Example evening with a dream feed (example only)

**Example only.** Times will be different for your family.

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 6:45 p.m. | Bedtime feed, then bath, pajamas, book |
| 7:15 p.m. | Baby asleep |
| 10:15 p.m. | Dream feed in a dim room, then back down on their back |
| 10:45 p.m. | Parents go to sleep |
| 2:30–4:00 a.m. | Next waking (if any), handled as usual |
| 6:30–7:00 a.m. | Morning wake-up |

If your baby starts waking *earlier* after the dream feed than before, or is very hard to settle afterwards, the feed may be breaking up their sleep rather than extending it.

## When to stop or drop the dream feed

No study has looked at this, so there's no evidence-based age. Practical signs it may be time *(practical suggestions)*:

- Your baby takes very little at the dream feed.
- Your baby is hard to settle afterwards or wakes more often in the second half of the night.
- Your baby already sleeps a long stretch without it, or has started sleeping through.
- Your pediatrician says night feeds are no longer needed.

**How to drop it.** Huckleberry (app example) describes two options: stop waking your baby and let them wake when hungry, or gradually offer less milk or time at the breast each night. Napper (app example) says there's no particular reason to stop as long as it works for you. Happiest Baby (a third-party baby sleep brand) suggests stopping 2–4 weeks after your baby has been sleeping well from the dream feed through to the morning.

## When can babies drop night feeds altogether?

A dream feed is often the last night feed to go, so this question comes up alongside it:

- **Formula-fed babies:** HealthyChildren.org says most can make it through the night without a feed sometime between 2 and 4 months, or once they weigh more than about 12 pounds.
- **4–7 months:** KidsHealth says most babies no longer need a middle-of-the-night feed, though some, often breastfed babies, still wake to feed.
- **By 6 months:** Nationwide Children's Hospital says most babies can physically sleep through the night without feeding, yet 25–50% continue to wake.

Before you drop night feeds, check with your pediatrician, especially if your baby was premature, had slow weight gain, or has a medical condition.

## Dream feeds and "drowsy but awake"

A dream feed doesn't conflict with teaching your baby to fall asleep on their own at bedtime. Cleveland Clinic suggests feeding at the start of a 20–30 minute bedtime routine so your baby doesn't rely on feeding to fall asleep, and KidsHealth suggests putting your baby down drowsy but still awake. The dream feed happens hours later, once your baby is already asleep. If your baby can only fall asleep while feeding at every waking, a dream feed won't change that on its own; see our [sleep training methods](/sleep-training-methods-explained) page.

## What can make dream feeding backfire

- Fully waking your baby with bright lights, talking, or play.
- Feeding a baby who is too deeply asleep to suck and swallow properly.
- Timing it too late, so it cuts into your baby's natural longest stretch (Napper, app example).
- Adding a dream feed to replace night feeds your baby still needs.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Talk to your doctor before planning night feeds around your own sleep if your baby was born early, isn't gaining weight well, has reflux, or has fewer wet diapers. HealthyChildren.org suggests contacting your pediatrician if a breastfed baby feeds fewer than 8–12 times a day, a bottle-fed baby fewer than 5–8 times, or there are fewer than 4 wet diapers a day.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a dream feed?**  
A late-evening feed given to a sleeping or drowsy baby, usually just before parents go to bed, to try to push the next waking later.

**How do you dream feed safely?**  
Keep the room dim, hold your baby semi-upright for a bottle, never prop the bottle or feed lying flat, and lay your baby back down on their back in their own sleep space.

**Do dream feeds work?**  
Sometimes. One small trial of a combined program (including a 10 p.m.–midnight feed) found longer night sleep in breastfed babies; nobody has tested the dream feed on its own.

**When should I start a dream feed?**  
There's no official age. Many families try it from about 6 weeks to 4 months.

**When should I stop the dream feed?**  
There's no research on this. Drop it when your baby takes little at the feed, sleeps well without it, or the feed seems to cause extra wakings.

**Can I dream feed a newborn?**  
Newborns need frequent feeds anyway. A dream feed can fit into that, but it shouldn't replace feeds your newborn needs.
