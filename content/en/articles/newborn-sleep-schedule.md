# Newborn Sleep Schedule: What's Normal, Week by Week

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

If you searched for a **newborn sleep schedule** at 3 a.m., here is the honest headline: in the first weeks there isn't one, and that is how it is supposed to be. What you *can* have is a loose rhythm (feed, a short awake time, sleep, repeat) and a sense of what's normal so you know when not to worry and when to call.

## Short answer

Newborns sleep in short pieces around the clock because hunger, not daylight, runs their day. Total daily sleep varies hugely: Sleep Foundation and Pregnancy, Birth and Baby give **11–19 hours**, while NHS pages describe anything from about 8 to 18 hours as normal. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's sleep-duration recommendations start at 4 months, so there is no official target for newborns. Sleep Foundation says sleep may start to become more predictable around 2 months, and a clock-based routine usually comes later.

### Key numbers at a glance

| Question | What the sources say | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total sleep per day | 11–19 hours; NHS: about 8 up to 18 hours | Sleep Foundation; Pregnancy, Birth and Baby; NHS |
| Longest stretch | Often 1–2 hours at a time; rarely more than 3 hours | HealthyChildren.org (AAP); Newcastle Hospitals NHS |
| Feeds | Breastfed: often every 2 hours, 10–12 times a day; formula: every 2–3 hours, at least 8 a day | HealthyChildren.org (AAP) |
| Awake time between sleeps | About 30 minutes to 1.5 hours | Sleep Foundation; Cleveland Clinic |
| Wet diapers after day 4–5 | At least 5–6 a day | HealthyChildren.org (AAP) |

## Why newborns can't follow a sleep schedule yet

Three things make a fixed timetable impossible in the early weeks:

- **No body clock yet.** Newcastle Hospitals NHS explains that babies are born without a body clock and can't tell night from day; it develops over time. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says a newborn's sleep responds to hunger and fullness rather than light and dark.
- **Tiny stomachs.** HealthyChildren.org (AAP) compares a newborn's stomach to the size of an egg, which is why small, frequent feeds are the norm.
- **Short sleep cycles.** A newborn's sleep cycle is about **45 to 60 minutes**, according to Pregnancy, Birth and Baby, so waking often is built in.

That's why parenting services advise following your baby's cues for now rather than the clock.

## How much do newborns sleep? Why every source gives a different number

| Source | Newborn total sleep per 24 hours |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep Foundation (newborn schedule page) | 11–19 hours |
| Pregnancy, Birth and Baby (birth to 3 months) | 11–19 hours |
| KidsHealth (citing the National Sleep Foundation) | 14–17 hours; some newborns up to 18–19 |
| HealthyChildren.org (AAP) | About 16–17 hours, often only 1–2 hours at a time |
| Mayo Clinic (first month) | About 16 hours |
| Derbyshire NHS | Commonly 16–18 hours until around 4 months |
| NHS Best Start in Life | Around 8 hours for some newborns, up to around 18 for others; both normal |
| Newcastle Hospitals NHS | 8 to 20 hours in the first few months |

The spread is not a mistake. Some figures are averages, some are "recommended" ranges, and some are the extremes seen in healthy babies. The practical message is the same everywhere: a wide range is normal, and **how your baby feeds, grows, and behaves when awake tells you more than the total**.

## Newborn sleep week by week (what to expect)

Every baby moves at their own pace. Use these stages as rough landmarks, not deadlines.

**Weeks 1–2: feeding drives everything.** Expect frequent waking. The Sydney Children's Hospitals Network says a baby in the first weeks may wake as often as every 40 minutes and need a feed every 2 to 3 hours overnight. KidsHealth advises waking a newborn who sleeps for long stretches every 3–4 hours to feed until they show good weight gain, which usually happens within the first couple of weeks. Follow your own pediatrician's advice on this.

**Weeks 3–6: a little more awake.** Short alert periods appear, often after feeds. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says the circadian rhythm starts to develop at around **4 to 6 weeks**, so your baby begins to respond more to light and dark. Day-night confusion (long daytime sleeps, wakeful nights) is common now.

**Weeks 6–9: patterns start to show.** Sleep Foundation explains that at about **8 to 9 weeks**, the release of melatonin and cortisol starts following a daily rhythm. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby notes that some babies begin sleeping for longer periods at around 8 weeks.

**Weeks 9–12: longer night stretches for some.** Newcastle Hospitals NHS says that by 3 months some babies skip a night feed and sleep around 5 hours at a time. The Sydney Children's Hospitals Network says many babies around 3 months stay awake longer in the day and sleep longer at night. Others take longer, and that is normal too.

A '2 week sleep regression' search belongs on [2 week old baby sleep](/2-week-old-baby-sleep).

## The "second night" (a popular name, not a diagnosis)

Some hospital leaflets and parent articles call the stretch around **24 hours after birth** "second night syndrome." It is **not a formal medical diagnosis** and it is not a reason to invent a new schedule.

A Norfolk and Norwich University Hospitals NHS leaflet written for breastfeeding families on baby's second day (adapted from Lactation Education Resources, 2019) describes a familiar picture: a baby who may want to feed very often, may be harder to settle, and may cluster at the breast while parents are exhausted. The useful response is the ordinary newborn one, not a special protocol: **skin-to-skin**, frequent feeds, and asking for help so you can rest between them. Do not try to "stretch" feeds to teach a day-old baby a schedule — this page already says newborns are not ready for one.

This spell often lasts **a night, or a few nights**, and then blends into the frequent day-and-night feeding of weeks 1–2. Frequent feeding is common. It is not, by itself, a guarantee that intake is adequate. If your baby will not wake to feed, is too sleepy to feed, has fewer wet or dirty nappies than your midwife or pediatrician expects, is hard to rouse, or shows the warning signs already listed under "Red flags: when sleepiness is not normal" later on this page, seek clinical care rather than waiting for the "second night" to pass.

Do not confuse this early-days bump with [reverse cycling](/reverse-cycling) (nights getting heavier because days are underfed later on) or with [witching hour](/witching-hour).

## A flexible newborn day (example only)

Instead of clock times, think in loops. One loop is roughly: **feed → short awake time → sleep**. A newborn might repeat it 8–12 times in 24 hours.

| Part of the loop | What it might look like | Tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feed | Breast or bottle when you see early hunger cues | HealthyChildren.org: crying is a late hunger sign |
| Awake | A diaper change, a cuddle, a few minutes of face time or tummy time | Often just 30–60 minutes, feed included |
| Wind down | Dim the room, hold close, swaddle if your baby isn't trying to roll | Keep it short; newborns tire fast |
| Sleep | On the back, in a bassinet or crib | Anywhere from a short catnap to a few hours |

*Example of a single morning (illustration only):* 7:10 feed, 7:40 diaper and a chat, 8:00 asleep in the bassinet, 9:40 awake and hungry, and the loop starts again. Tomorrow will look different, and that's fine.

## “Eat, play, sleep” — a flexible pattern, not a law

Some parent sites, including Raising Children Network, describe a **feed, play, then sleep** rhythm as one way a day can go once a baby is a little older: a feed, a short stretch of awake time, then a nap, rather than feeding every baby all the way to sleep, every time. It is a **cue-led example**, not an official AAP protocol and not a timetable for a day-old baby.

Newborns on this page are **not** ready for a fixed three-step law. They need frequent feeds, including at night, and they often fall asleep at the breast or bottle — that is ordinary, not a failed routine. If a popular graphic tells you to “never feed to sleep” in week one, ignore the graphic and stay with the flexible day already sketched above. For a later bedtime pattern, see [bedtime routine for baby](/bedtime-routine-for-baby). For frequent evening feeds, see [cluster feeding](/cluster-feeding).

## Helping your newborn tell day from night

You can't speed up the body clock, but you can give it clear signals. NHS advice:

- **Daytime:** open curtains, play and talk, and don't worry about everyday household noise while your baby naps.
- **Night-time:** keep lights low, talk little, change diapers only when needed, and put your baby down as soon as they've been fed and changed.

KidsHealth adds that it can take a few weeks for a baby's brain to learn the difference, and that there are no tricks to hurry it.

Newcastle Hospitals NHS suggests that daylight and outdoor time in the day help, and that starting the day at a similar time each morning can support the body clock over a couple of weeks.

## What's normal in newborn sleep (and looks scary but usually isn't)

- **Noisy, twitchy sleep.** Babies spend more of their sleep in active (REM-like) sleep. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says they may breathe shallowly, twitch, flutter their eyes, and make noises.
- **Falling asleep at the breast or bottle.** NHS calls this common in the early weeks; you can keep feeding until your baby is done.
- **Only sleeping when held.** Tresillian describes this as normal behavior in the early weeks. You can hold your baby while you are awake and alert, then move them to their own flat, clear sleep space.
- **Waking every 1–3 hours.** Pregnancy, Birth and Baby says a baby this age may wake after one or two sleep cycles, so every 1 to 3 hours.

## Safe sleep in the newborn weeks

The AAP's guidance, explained on HealthyChildren.org:

- **Back to sleep** for every nap and night.
- **A firm, flat surface** with only a fitted sheet. No pillows, bumpers, quilts, or soft toys.
- **Room-share without bed-sharing**, ideally for at least the first 6 months. HealthyChildren.org says this can lower SIDS risk by as much as 50%. NHS also advises keeping your baby in your room for at least the first 6 months, day and night.
- **Not too warm:** dress your baby in about one layer more than you'd wear.
- **Swaddling** is optional; stop once your baby tries to roll, and never use weighted swaddles or blankets.
- **Pacifiers:** HealthyChildren.org suggests waiting until breastfeeding is established, around 3 to 4 weeks, before offering one.

If your baby falls asleep in a car seat, swing, or carrier, move them to a flat sleep surface as soon as you can.

## Red flags: when sleepiness is not normal

Newborns are sleepy, but there's a difference between sleepy and hard to rouse. HealthyChildren.org (AAP) advises seeking medical help right away if a newborn seems increasingly sleepy, unresponsive, or hard to wake.

**Call emergency services (911 in the US)** if your baby can't be woken, is very weak or floppy, grunts or moans with each breath, or has bluish or gray lips, tongue, or face (HealthyChildren.org symptom checker).

**Call your doctor now** if your newborn has to be woken for every feed or can't finish feeds, is sleeping much more than usual, has a fever while under 12 weeks old (don't give fever medicine before being seen), or looks or acts abnormal in any way (HealthyChildren.org symptom checker). Fewer wet diapers than expected (fewer than 5–6 a day after the first 4–5 days, per HealthyChildren.org) is another reason to call.

## Looking after yourself in the newborn weeks

NHS advice for parents is practical: sleep when your baby sleeps, share night duties with a partner if you can, and accept help from friends or family. If you are formula feeding, a partner can take some feeds; if you are breastfeeding, they can take over early-morning changes. Newcastle Hospitals NHS suggests going to bed early to make the most of your baby's longest stretch at the start of the night. If you feel unable to cope, speak to your doctor, midwife, or health visitor.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I put my newborn on a sleep schedule?**  
Not in the first weeks. Sleep Foundation says newborns don't follow a predictable schedule for roughly the first two months. A loose feed–awake–sleep loop works better.

**How many hours should a newborn sleep?**  
There's no official target. Published figures range from about 11 to 19 hours, and NHS pages call totals from around 8 to 18 hours normal.

**Should I wake my newborn to feed?**  
In the early weeks, often yes. KidsHealth suggests feeding every 3–4 hours until good weight gain is established. Ask your pediatrician what applies to your baby.

**Why does my newborn sleep all day and stay awake at night?**  
Their body clock isn't working yet. Bright, normal days and quiet, dim nights help it develop over the first weeks.

**When will my baby sleep longer at night?**  
Some babies manage around 5-hour stretches by about 3 months (Newcastle Hospitals NHS). Many take longer.

**My newborn only sleeps on me. Is that normal?**  
Very common in the early weeks (Tresillian). Hold your baby while you are awake, and move them to their own safe sleep space before you sleep.
