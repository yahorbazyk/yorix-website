# Self-Soothing: A Skill That Grows, Not a Method

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Self-soothing is a **developmental skill**: a growing ability to settle, or to settle again after a normal waking, with less immediate help. This page stays on that meaning — what the word refers to, when it is reasonable to expect the beginnings of it, and what it is *not*. It is not a second methods explainer. How-tos for chair, fading, Ferber, and cry-it-out live on [sleep training methods explained](/sleep-training-methods-explained), [gentle sleep training](/gentle-sleep-training), [Ferber](/ferber-method-baby-sleep), and [cry it out](/cry-it-out-method). Readiness and age ranges for formal training stay on [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training), which already has a short self-soothing section; this article is the fuller developmental account, not a copy of that section.

## Short answer

Cleveland Clinic describes a self-soothing baby as one who can calm down on their own enough to fall asleep without being held the whole way, and who can often fall back asleep after a waking. The same page is blunt about timing: **do not try to teach this before 3 months**. Newborns need you to help them regulate; emotional control takes years, not a weekend. Cleveland Clinic also calls the process **co-soothing**: the parent still sets the conditions, and the baby gradually takes a larger role. It is "not a switch you can flip."

HealthyChildren's advice, aimed at babies **about 4 months and older**, is to put them down **drowsy, not already asleep**, so they get practice falling asleep in their own sleep space, and not to rush in at the first sound. It is normal, that page says, for a 6-month-old to wake and go back to sleep after a few minutes. You still go in for a feed, a diaper, or illness.

Self-soothing is **not** the same as [sleeping through the night](/when-do-babies-sleep-through-the-night). A baby can learn to settle and still wake. A baby can have a long stretch without having practiced settling. Comfort objects stay out of the crib for the first year (AAP).

## How the skill actually shows up

In the first months, being soothed *by* a caregiver is the work. Hands-to-mouth and a [pacifier](/pacifier-and-sleep), once breastfeeding is established if you are nursing, are early regulation tools, not proof that a newborn should be left to figure out the night.

From the middle of the first year, two things often arrive together: longer stretches are more possible for some babies, and night wakings are still normal. HealthyChildren's picture of a "good sleeper" at this age is a baby who wakes and gets back to sleep, not one who never wakes. Leaving a brief pause before you go in — when the baby is old enough, well, and not due a feed — is how many families first *see* whether resettling is starting to happen. That pause is not, by itself, a formal method.

Cleveland Clinic's supporting conditions are ordinary, not a protocol: needs met first (diaper, temperature, hunger, not overtired), a fairly regular sleep time, and putting the baby down drowsy rather than exhausted. A hand on the chest or a few words without picking up immediately is one way some parents stay present while the baby practices. If you later choose a named method, that is a separate decision.

## A timing conflict to keep in view

Sources do not all start the clock on the same week:

- Cleveland Clinic: do not try to *teach* self-soothing before **3 months**; many babies can begin to practice around then, with the parent still involved.
- HealthyChildren's drowsy-but-awake and "do not rush in" advice is written for **4 months and older**, with a 6-month example of a baby who wakes and resettles.
- Formal behavioral training is often discussed from about **4 to 6 months**, and many clinicians are more comfortable nearer 6 months — that debate lives on the readiness page.

We will not average these into a single "self-soothing birthday." Use the baby's age, health, and feeding, and ask the pediatrician if you are unsure. For babies born early, use [corrected age](/preemie-sleep).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask before you experiment with longer pauses if your baby is under 4 months, was premature, is not gaining well, or has reflux or breathing concerns. Go in sooner, not later, if the cry sounds unlike their usual settle, or if you think they are unwell.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can babies self-soothe?**  
Not as newborns. Cleveland Clinic advises waiting until at least 3 months to even begin teaching. Practice often becomes more realistic in the 4- to 6-month stretch. There is no official week when every baby "can."

**Is self-soothing the same as cry it out?**  
No. Self-soothing is the skill. Cry-it-out is one method some families use later. Many babies begin to resettle with far less formal structure than that.

**Does a pacifier count?**  
It can be an early tool (Cleveland Clinic; AAP also lists a pacifier at sleep time for risk reduction). It can also become a habit you have to replace at every waking. See [pacifier and sleep](/pacifier-and-sleep).

**Should I leave them to cry so they learn?**  
Not as a newborn, and not as a substitute for checking needs. A short pause in an older, well baby is different from a method you have not actually chosen. If you do want a plan, use the readiness and methods pages rather than inventing one at 3 a.m.

**What does “drowsy but awake” mean?**  
It is a phrase, not a separate method and not an official AAP protocol. It means putting a baby down when they are clearly ready for sleep but still awake, so that the crib or cot is part of falling asleep — the same idea already discussed on this page and on [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training). It is not a rule for a newborn, and it is not a reason to leave a distressed young baby to cry. Caring for Kids describes putting a baby down drowsy but awake as one healthy-sleep habit in the first year, with more emphasis later; Cleveland Clinic advises waiting until at least 3 months before even beginning to teach self-soothing. We already refuse to average those clocks. If you want a named settling approach, see [responsive settling](/responsive-settling) or the [methods](/sleep-training-methods-explained) page rather than turning this phrase into a third plan.

**What if “drowsy but awake” does not work?**
It is a popular phrase, not an official AAP protocol, and it does not work for every baby or every night. If they cry as soon as they hit the mattress, you are in [sleep associations](/sleep-associations) and [baby won't sleep in crib](/baby-wont-sleep-in-crib) territory. If you are considering a formal plan, see [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training) and [is sleep training harmful](/is-sleep-training-harmful).
