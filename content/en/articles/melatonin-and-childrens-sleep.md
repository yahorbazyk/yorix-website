# Melatonin and Children's Sleep: Not a Do-It-Yourself Sleeping Pill

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

This is a **safety and decision** page: what melatonin is, what HealthyChildren.org and KidsHealth actually say, and why a pediatrician should be in the conversation before a bottle of gummies comes home. It is not a shopper's guide, not a brand review, and not a second [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby). If the real problem is a fight at lights-out, start with [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles) or the [why won't my baby sleep](/why-wont-my-baby-sleep) hub.

## Short answer

Melatonin is a hormone the brain releases in the dark; it helps the body get ready for sleep. The same name is sold over the counter as a **dietary supplement**. In the United States, dietary supplements are **not approved by the FDA as sleep medicines**, and KidsHealth notes that the amount in a bottle can differ from the label, or include ingredients that are not listed.

HealthyChildren.org, writing for the American Academy of Pediatrics, is clear on the order of operations: **melatonin is not a sleeping pill**. It should be used only **after a discussion with your pediatrician**, and only after you have already tried a regular bedtime routine that does not depend on a supplement. KidsHealth is even more reserved: **doctors usually do not recommend melatonin for kids and teens**, and improving sleep habits may be all that is needed. We report both tones. We do not average them into a Yorix "start age" or a default dose.

If a pediatrician does recommend a short trial, HealthyChildren.org describes a cautious approach (lowest dose first; many children respond to 0.5 mg or 1 mg, taken 30 to 90 minutes before bed; most who benefit, including many children with ADHD, do not need more than 3 to 6 mg). That is **their** wording, not a protocol you should start on your own. KidsHealth says the doctor should help you choose the form and the dose. Inhaled or room-spray products have not been studied in children and are **not recommended** (KidsHealth).

### What the sources actually say

| Should I start it myself? | No. Talk to the pediatrician first; habits come first | No. Doctors usually do not recommend it; talk to the doctor first |
| What might it be used for? | Short-term help while you build a routine; sometimes to reset an older child's or teen's clock after a break; sometimes, with monitoring, in children with autism or ADHD | A short trial if the doctor suggests it, for example to reset a teen's schedule, or for some conditions that affect sleep |
| Official U.S. dose guideline | There is **no** specific official dosing guideline for children; the figures above are from that page, to discuss with a clinician | The doctor figures out form and dose |
| Safety cautions | Poisoning reports in children rose from 2012 to 2021 (more than 260,000 reports; most had no symptoms, some needed hospital care, two children died). Some products have contained much more melatonin than the label, or serotonin or CBD | A few poisoning reports needed hospital care; products may be mislabeled; store out of reach |

HealthyChildren.org also says we **need more research** on longer use in children, including questions about growth and puberty. Common short-term side effects they list include morning sleepiness, drowsiness, and possibly more urination at night. KidsHealth lists headaches, dizziness, anxiety, and morning sleepiness. Melatonin can interact with other medicines. Neither page treats diphenhydramine (the medicine in many "PM" or allergy products) as a home sleep aid; do not add it on your own.

## Habits first — and why screens matter

A consistent wind-down, a regular bedtime, and a dark, boring room do more lasting work than a supplement (both sources). The AAP, as stated on HealthyChildren.org, recommends **avoiding screens for at least one hour before bed**. Blue light can suppress the body's own melatonin. That habit belongs on the [routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby) page; we mention it here because it is part of why a pill is the wrong first move.

If your child is autistic, or you are considering any supplement for sleep, read [autism and sleep](/autism-and-sleep) as well — and still do not start melatonin without the pediatrician. This page will not write a treatment plan.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Talk before you buy a supplement. Talk sooner if your child is already taking one, if they are unusually sleepy by day, if they have other medicines, or if they may have swallowed some. In the United States, Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222; call 911 if a child has collapsed, is having a seizure, has trouble breathing, or cannot be woken (HealthyChildren.org).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is melatonin safe for toddlers?**  
There is no simple yes. KidsHealth: doctors usually do not recommend it. HealthyChildren.org: only after a pediatrician conversation, and not as a substitute for a routine. Long-term use in young children is not well studied.

**Can I follow a dose I saw on a blog?**  
No. There is no official pediatric dosing guideline. If a clinician recommends melatonin, use the product and the timing they name, and store it out of reach.

**Will it fix early waking or night wakings?**  
Unlikely, by itself. Early rising and night waking have their own pages: [early morning waking](/early-morning-waking), [survive night wakings](/survive-night-wakings-new-parents).
