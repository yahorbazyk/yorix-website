# Bed-Sharing and Co-Sleeping: What the Evidence Says

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

"Co-sleeping" is a slippery word. Some people use it to mean **room-sharing** — the baby sleeps in your room, on a separate crib or bassinet. Others use it to mean **bed-sharing** — the baby sleeps on the same surface as an adult, or even on a sofa. Those two arrangements are not equally safe. This page is about that distinction, and about a real disagreement between U.S. and UK public-health advice. The full ABC checklist lives on our [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) hub; we will not repeat it here.

## Short answer

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) **recommends room-sharing and cannot recommend bed-sharing under any circumstances**, including with twins. Room-sharing for at least the first 6 months can lower SIDS risk by as much as 50%, according to HealthyChildren.org. Sofas and armchairs are in a worse category still: the AAP cites a risk of sleep-related death up to 67 times higher. The NHS and Lullaby Trust agree that a baby's own clear cot or Moses basket, in your room, is the safest place. They also publish a "when not to bed-share" list (alcohol, smoking, drugs that cause drowsiness, prematurity, or birth weight under 2.5 kg / 5.5 lb) and, for families who do share a bed, harm-reduction steps. That is not the same as the AAP's "we cannot recommend it." We show both rather than picking a quieter middle.

## First, get the words straight

Sleep Foundation's co-sleeping article, medically reviewed in 2025, usefully splits the language:

- **Room-sharing:** the baby has their own approved crib, bassinet, or play yard in the same room.
- **Bed-sharing / surface-sharing:** the baby and a caregiver sleep on the same bed, couch, chair, or floor.
- **Rooming-in:** the hospital version of room-sharing in the first day or two after birth.

Lullaby Trust uses "co-sleeping" as an umbrella for bed, sofa, or chair sharing, and treats room-sharing as a separate, recommended practice. When a friend says they "co-sleep," it is worth asking which of these they mean.

## “Breastsleeping” and the “Safe Sleep Seven”

These are **popular parent-education names**, not official AAP, NHS, or Lullaby Trust protocols. **Breastsleeping** is a term some breastfeeding advocates use for sleeping in close contact while nursing. The **“Safe Sleep Seven”** is an unofficial checklist circulated in some parent communities (a list of conditions under which those authors consider bed-sharing lower risk).

We name them here because people search for them. We will not reprint the unofficial list as if it were a medical standard, and we will not average it with the AAP’s advice already in the next section (the AAP recommends room-sharing **without** bed-sharing). UK advice that overlaps — and the places it does not — stays in the UK section already on this page. If a book or a forum says the “seven” make bed-sharing official policy, that is not accurate. Use this page’s sourced comparison, not a third checklist.

## Why the AAP draws a hard line

The 2022 AAP policy, in parent language on HealthyChildren.org, is unambiguous: based on the evidence, the AAP recommends that parents **avoid bed-sharing**, and it is "unable to recommend bed-sharing under any circumstances." The reasons are suffocation, entrapment, overlay, and a higher rate of sudden unexpected infant death. Pillows, duvets, a soft mattress, and an adult who does not reliably wake all add risk that a bare crib does not.

The policy is not a moral judgment about closeness or breastfeeding. The AAP wants the baby nearby — in your room — because that makes feeds and checks easier and is associated with lower SIDS risk. If you bring the baby into the bed to feed or comfort them, the advice is to put them back in their own space before you go to sleep. If there is any chance you might doze off, first remove pillows and loose bedding, then move the baby back as soon as you wake. That is damage control, not a recommended sleep plan.

The AAP also flags situations in which bed-sharing is especially hazardous (figures from HealthyChildren.org):

- alcohol, marijuana, medicines, or drugs that make it harder to wake (more than 10 times higher)
- a parent who smokes or vapes, or who smoked in pregnancy
- a baby younger than 4 months (5 to 10 times higher), or a baby who was preterm or low birth weight (2 to 5 times higher)
- anyone other than a parent in the bed, including other children
- a sofa, armchair, waterbed, or other soft surface

## Where UK advice overlaps — and where it does not

NHS and Lullaby Trust start in the same place: the **safest** spot is the baby's own firm, flat, clear cot or Moses basket, in your room, for at least 6 months. They are just as firm as the AAP about **sofas and armchairs**: never fall asleep there with a baby. They also tell you **not** to share a bed if the baby was premature or under 2.5 kg (5.5 lb) at birth, or if anyone in the bed smokes, has drunk alcohol, or has taken medicine or drugs that cause drowsiness.

The difference is tone after that list. The AAP stops at "we cannot recommend bed-sharing." Lullaby Trust, acknowledging that many families share a bed on purpose or by accident, then lists ways to make an adult bed less hazardous if it happens: keep pillows and adult duvets away from the baby, no slatted or decorative headboards that can trap a head, no other children or pets in the bed, no gap between mattress and wall, never leave a baby alone on an adult bed, place the baby on their back, and consider a well-fitting sleep bag as the baby's own layer. They do not recommend pods or nests on the adult mattress.

We are not going to turn those harm-reduction points into a Yorix "how to bed-share safely" guide, because the AAP does not consider any routine bed-sharing arrangement recommendable. If your family does share a bed, read Lullaby Trust's page in full, talk honestly with your pediatrician, and at the very least stay off sofas and out of the high-risk situations above.

Sleep Foundation notes that some countries where bed-sharing is common also have low SIDS rates, but it is not clear whether bed-sharing itself is protective there or whether other factors (more breastfeeding, less smoking, better access to care, firmer, clearer sleep surfaces) explain the difference. That uncertainty is another reason not to treat another country's rate as a green light.

## Room-sharing is the recommended middle

A crib or bassinet next to the adult bed gives you the access that many families want from bed-sharing, without the shared-surface risk. The AAP, NHS, and Lullaby Trust all ask for this for **at least the first 6 months**. Older AAP wording said "ideally the first year"; the 2022 policy uses the six-month minimum. When you are ready to discuss a later move, see [when to move baby to their own room](/when-to-move-baby-to-own-room).

Accidental bed-sharing still happens, especially during night feeds. Planning the baby's own surface within arm's reach makes it easier to put them back. If you think you might fall asleep while feeding, do not do that feed on a sofa.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Tell your baby's doctor what actually happens at night, even if you worry about being judged. Sleep Foundation makes the same point: pediatricians know that many families bed-share at least sometimes, and they can talk about your baby's age, birth history, and smoking or medication in the home. Call urgently if a baby has been in an unsafe sleep situation and you are worried about their breathing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is co-sleeping the same as bed-sharing?**  
Not always. Ask which arrangement someone means. Room-sharing is recommended; bed-sharing is not, on AAP advice.

**Is bed-sharing safer if I am breastfeeding?**  
Breast milk is associated with a lower SIDS risk, but the AAP still does not recommend bed-sharing as the way to get that benefit. Bring the baby into the bed to feed if you want, then return them to their own space.

**What if I already bed-share?**  
Know the high-risk situations (alcohol, smoke, drowsiness, prematurity, low birth weight, sofas) and have an honest conversation with your pediatrician. The safest change is a crib or bassinet in your room.

**Can twins share our bed?**  
The AAP says no, including with twins. Lullaby Trust advises against bed-sharing with more than one baby at a time because of a greater risk of accidents. See [twins and sleep](/twins-and-sleep).

**Are in-bed sleepers or "sidecar" cribs a loophole?**  
The AAP has said it cannot recommend for or against some bedside or in-bed products until more research is done. If you use a product, it still needs to meet current safety standards and must not become a soft, inclined, or cluttered nest. When in doubt, a standalone, approved bassinet or crib is the clearer choice.

**Can a baby sleep on my chest if I am awake?**
Holding a baby on your chest while you are fully awake and alert is not the same as sleeping with them on your chest. The risk is dozing off, especially on a sofa or armchair: HealthyChildren.org (AAP) cites a risk of sleep-related death up to 67 times higher when a baby sleeps with someone on a couch or soft chair — described in the safe-sleep guidance. If there is any chance you might fall asleep, move them to their own firm, flat, clear surface on their back first. A contact nap in your arms still has to end on a safe surface if you need to put them down; see [contact naps](/contact-naps).
