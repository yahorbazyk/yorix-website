# Why Does My Baby Fight Sleep? A Triage, Not a New Regression

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Parents search "fighting sleep" when a baby looks exhausted and still arches, cries, or plays at the edge of sleep. This page is that named phrase. It is not a second [overtired baby](/overtired-baby) essay, not a second [why won't my baby sleep](/why-wont-my-baby-sleep) hub, and not [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles). Those pages stay the homes for the full plans. This one helps you tell *which* of those pictures you are in.

## Short answer

"Fighting sleep" is not an official diagnosis and not an official regression age, even though Google Suggest shows the phrase at 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, and 18 months. It usually means one of four ordinary patterns:

1. **Wired and tired** — they have gone past the point of easy settling. The full picture and what to try first are on [overtired baby](/overtired-baby).
2. **Not tired enough** — the last stretch of awake time was short, or a late nap ate the bedtime window. Age schedules and [wake windows by age](/wake-windows-by-age) are the place for ranges (which, like sleep-hour bands, do not all agree).
3. **They can settle, but not in the place or way you hoped** — that is often [baby won't sleep in crib](/baby-wont-sleep-in-crib) or [contact naps](/contact-naps).
4. **A verbal toddler stalling** — [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles).

There is no official "fighting sleep hour total." Use the 24-hour bands on [how much sleep does a baby need](/how-much-sleep-does-a-baby-need) (NSF and AASM ranges conflict; we do not average them).

## Why the phrase shows up at so many ages

The wording is the same; the cause is not. At 4 months it often travels with the cycle change described on [4 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/4-months). At 8–10 months, standing and separation can look like a fight — see [sleep regression at 8 months](/sleep-regression/8-months) and [separation anxiety and sleep](/separation-anxiety-and-sleep). At 18 months, language and the one-nap day change the evening. None of those is a separate official "fighting sleep regression."

A slightly earlier bedtime after a short-nap day is a common experiment on the overtired page. A slightly later bedtime after an undertired evening is the opposite experiment. Do not run both on the same night.

## When fighting sleep is not the first problem

If they are hard to rouse at other times, feeding poorly, breathing oddly, or seem in pain, skip the schedule tweaks and call. Reflux, illness, and ear pain can look like "they will not go down." Those are clinical questions, not a fight-sleep method.

## When to get clinical advice

Call if the evenings are new *and* they seem unwell, if breathing or colour worries you, or if you cannot get any useful sleep into a 24-hour period and they look exhausted and unwell. Urgent care is for a baby who cannot be woken, who is struggling to breathe, or whose colour changes suddenly.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal for a 6 or 9 month old to fight sleep?**  
The phrase is common at those ages in search. It is not a diagnosis. Use the triage above.

**Is there a fighting-sleep regression?**  
No official one. Use the named regression or month page that matches the age, plus the overtired or battles page.

**Should I start sleep training because they fight sleep?**  
Only if you and your pediatrician are already in the usual readiness window (Sleep Foundation generally discusses formal training around 4–6 months). See [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training) and [is sleep training harmful](/is-sleep-training-harmful). This page is not a method.

**Could they just be hungry?**  
Yes. A fight at bedtime after a short last feed is sometimes hunger, especially in younger babies. It can also be overtiredness. If weight gain is a concern, ask before you stretch feeds.
