# Baby Snoring and Sleeping With the Mouth Open

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A baby who snores, or who sleeps with the mouth open, worries people — and sometimes the worry is right. This page is a **conservative** guide to ordinary congestion, mouth breathing, snoring, and the signs that need a clinician, including possible obstructive sleep apnea. It is not a diagnosis, and it is not a rewrite of [newborn grunting in sleep](/newborn-grunting-in-sleep). Grunting in young babies is often a gut or breathing-pattern sound of a different kind; that article owns it. It is also not a second [sick baby sleep](/sick-baby-sleep) page, though a cold is one common reason for short-term mouth breathing and a stuffy-sounding night.

## Short answer

A blocked nose can make a baby snore softly or breathe through the mouth for a few days. HealthyChildren's stuffy-nose sleep advice still applies: **sleep remains on the back, on a firm, flat surface**. Saline and gentle suction can help before a feed or sleep. Wedges, pillows, and sitting devices are not a congestion treatment.

Snoring that is **frequent, loud, or paired with pauses, gasps, or a chin-out, mouth-open posture** is a reason to talk with the pediatrician, not to wait for the next cold to pass (Cleveland Clinic, HealthyChildren, KidsHealth). HealthyChildren also describes **periodic breathing of infancy**: short pauses followed by rapid "catch-up" breaths, then regular breathing again. That pattern is generally considered normal and is usually outgrown by about 6 months — it is not the same as obstructive apnea, but you should still ask if you are unsure which one you are seeing.

Call emergency services if breathing stops for **more than 20 seconds**, or if the baby becomes pale, bluish, or gray, or if muscle tone changes in a way that scares you (HealthyChildren).

### Sounds and postures, in brief

| What you notice | Often means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soft, occasional noisy breathing during a cold | Congestion | Saline, brief suction if you use it, flat on the back. See [sick baby sleep](/sick-baby-sleep) |
| Mouth open only while the nose is blocked | The baby is bypassing a stuffy nose | Same as above; mention it if it continues after the cold |
| Frequent or loud snoring, most nights | Needs a clinical ear — including possible sleep-disordered breathing | Pediatrician. Cleveland Clinic also flags sleeping with the mouth open and the chin or neck extended |
| Pauses, snorts, gasps, choking, very restless sleep | Possible obstructive sleep apnea or another problem | Pediatrician promptly; 911 if the pause is long or color changes |
| Brief pauses with catch-up breaths in a young infant, then regular breathing | May be periodic breathing of infancy (HealthyChildren; usually gone by ~6 months) | Ask if you cannot tell this from a longer, frightening pause |
| Newborn grunting, especially with straining or a period of quiet sleep | Often a different issue | [Newborn grunting in sleep](/newborn-grunting-in-sleep) |

## When mouth breathing is just a cold — and when it is not

Babies prefer to breathe through the nose. A cold, allergies, or dried mucus can force the mouth open. That can look dramatic and still be temporary. If mouth breathing or snoring **outlasts the illness**, or it is the usual way your baby sleeps even when they are well, get it checked. Persistent mouth breathing can have more than one cause (remaining congestion, large tonsils or adenoids in older infants and toddlers, a narrow airway, or something a clinician needs to see). We will not diagnose those from a website.

Do not prop the mattress or use a pillow to "open the airway." The AAP's reflux and cold-related advice is the same: elevation is not a safe sleep strategy. Details are on [reflux and baby sleep](/reflux-and-baby-sleep) and the sick-sleep page.

## Snoring and sleep apnea — stay conservative

Obstructive sleep apnea is when something blocks the upper airway during sleep. KidsHealth lists snoring with pauses, snorts, or gasps; heavy breathing; very restless sleep; and, in older children, daytime sleepiness or behavior and learning problems. HealthyChildren lists frequent snoring, night breathing problems, daytime sleepiness, and attention or behavior issues. Cleveland Clinic adds coughing or gasping, restless sleep, and, in some children, night sweats or bedwetting. Not every snoring baby has apnea. Regular, noisy snoring is still worth mentioning at a visit.

Diagnosis, if it is needed, is a clinician's job and may include a sleep study (polysomnogram) (HealthyChildren, KidsHealth, Cleveland Clinic). Treatment in older children sometimes involves the tonsils and adenoids; that is not a home decision and is less often the first conversation in a very young infant, whose anatomy and causes can differ. We stop at "get it evaluated."

If you are heading to an appointment, Cleveland Clinic suggests keeping a simple log for a week: how many nights the snoring happens, how often during the night, and whether there are pauses or a mouth-open, chin-out posture. That is something a tracker can support without turning into a diagnosis.

## If the pediatrician mentions a sleep study, or tonsils

A **sleep study** (polysomnogram) is an overnight test, usually in a hospital or sleep center, that records how a child actually sleeps (KidsHealth, reviewed November 2021). Sensors can track breathing, oxygen, carbon dioxide, heart rate, brain waves, eye movements, snoring, and body movement. One parent can usually stay. Results often take a few weeks and should be explained at a follow-up visit — not decoded from a printout on this page.

Doctors may recommend a study when they are worried about problems such as **obstructive sleep apnea**, among other sleep disorders KidsHealth lists. Loud regular snoring, pauses, gasping, or very restless sleep on this page are reasons to **talk to the pediatrician**, who decides whether a referral or a study is next. We will not declare that your child “needs” one from a blog checklist.

Families also hear that **tonsils or adenoids** might be involved in noisy, blocked breathing. That can be true for some children, and it is a specialist conversation after an exam (and, if ordered, after a study). This page will not recommend surgery, a medicine, or a CPAP plan. Bring the nights you have logged, and let the clinician lead.

## When to talk to your pediatrician — and when to call 911

Talk to the pediatrician if snoring is frequent or loud, mouth breathing is the usual pattern, you hear pauses or gasps, the baby is a very restless sleeper, or daytime behavior or alertness worries you.

Call **911** (or your local emergency number) if breathing stops for more than 20 seconds, the skin looks pale, blue, or gray, or the baby is working hard to breathe or cannot be roused (HealthyChildren; Cleveland Clinic also flags pale, blue, or gray color and trouble breathing).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal for a baby to sleep with their mouth open?**  
It can be, for a few days, when the nose is blocked. If it is every night, or it comes with snoring or pauses, ask the pediatrician.

**Do all babies who snore have sleep apnea?**  
No. Frequent or loud snoring still deserves a mention at a visit, especially with other signs (HealthyChildren, KidsHealth, Cleveland Clinic).

**Is this the same as newborn grunting?**  
Usually not. Grunting, especially in the first weeks, often has a different rhythm and a different typical cause. Use [newborn grunting in sleep](/newborn-grunting-in-sleep) for that pattern.

**Can I use a wedge or a car seat so they breathe better?**  
No. Sleep stays flat, on the back, in a clear crib or bassinet. See [sick baby sleep](/sick-baby-sleep) and [baby sleeping in a car seat](/baby-sleeping-in-car-seat).

**My 2 week old sleeps with their mouth open. Is that normal?**
A briefly open mouth in active sleep can happen. If the baby is congested, having trouble feeding, or you hear regular snoring or pauses, use the advice already on this page and [sick baby sleep](/sick-baby-sleep). Newborns with persistent mouth breathing or poor feeding should be discussed with the pediatrician, not diagnosed from a photo.

**When should I see a sleep specialist or ask for a sleep study?**
Start with your pediatrician. This page already lists breathing, snoring, and unusual-event reasons to call. A specialist or a study is their referral, not a parent DIY step . If the issue is nightmares, sleepwalking, or sleep talking rather than breathing, see [night terrors](/night-terrors), which already compares those events.
