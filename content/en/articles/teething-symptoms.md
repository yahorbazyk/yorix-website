# Teething Symptoms: What's Really Teething, What Isn't, and How to Help

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If your baby has a fever, seems unwell, is hard to wake, or isn't drinking, contact your pediatrician or seek urgent care.*

Teething gets blamed for almost everything in the first two years: runny noses, diaper rash, fevers, bad nights. Some of that blame is deserved, much of it isn't. This guide sorts teething signs that research actually supports from the myths, explains when the first tooth usually appears and in what order the rest follow, and lists remedies that are safe, plus the ones the FDA warns against.

## Short answer

Most babies get their first tooth **between about 4 and 7 months** (AAP), though anywhere from 3 to 12+ months can be normal. Real teething signs are **mild**: drooling, chewing and gnawing, gum rubbing, sore or swollen gums, some fussiness, a rash on the face from drool, and at most a **slight** rise in temperature. In the largest prospective study, symptoms clustered in an **8-day window** around each tooth (4 days before to 3 days after). Teething **does not cause a true fever** (100.4°F / 38°C or higher), diarrhea, vomiting, or a diaper rash, so those need another explanation. For pain, use a clean finger, a firm chilled (not frozen) teether, and, if needed, acetaminophen or (from 6 months) ibuprofen after checking the dose. **Avoid benzocaine or lidocaine gels, homeopathic tablets, and amber necklaces.**

## When do babies start teething?

Sources give slightly different windows, which shows how wide the normal range is:

- **AAP (HealthyChildren.org):** typically by about 4–7 months; later is often hereditary and not a problem.
- **NHS:** most around 6 months; some before 4 months, some after 12 months, and a few babies are born with a tooth.
- **Mayo Clinic:** many around 6 months, others a few months later.
- **MedlinePlus (U.S. National Library of Medicine):** generally between 6 and 8 months.

If there is no tooth at all by the first birthday, it's reasonable to mention it at the 12-month checkup; the AAP still says late teeth usually aren't a sign of illness.

### Baby teeth order (typical ages)

| Teeth | NHS (approximate) | Cleveland Clinic chart |

| Lower central incisors (bottom front) | Around 6 months | 6–10 months |
| Upper central incisors (top front) | Around 6 months | 8–12 months |
| Lateral incisors | Around 8 months | Upper 9–13, lower 10–16 months |
| First molars | Around 12 months | 13–19 months |
| Canines | Around 18 months | 16–23 months |
| Second molars | Around 24 months | 23–33 months |

Bottom teeth usually come before the matching top teeth, and teeth tend to arrive in pairs. Cleveland Clinic's rough rule: about four teeth for every six months of life.

## Teething symptoms: what the evidence says

The best data come from a 2000 *Pediatrics* study in which parents of 125 healthy babies recorded temperatures and 18 symptoms every day from 4 months to 1 year, covering 475 tooth eruptions. Symptoms were more common only in the **4 days before, the day of, and 3 days after** a tooth came through.

| Linked with teething (Macknin 2000) | **Not** linked with teething |
| --- | --- |
| More biting and sucking | Congestion, cough |
| Drooling | Sleep disturbance (as recorded in the study) |
| Gum rubbing | Looser or more frequent stools |
| Irritability | Less interest in milk (liquids) |
| Being more wakeful | Rashes other than on the face |
| Ear rubbing | Vomiting |
| Rash on the face | Fever over 102°F (38.9°C) |
| Less interest in solid food | |
| Mild rise in temperature | |

Two important details: **no single symptom showed up in more than 35% of teething babies**, and no combination of symptoms could predict that a tooth was about to appear. Many babies cut teeth with almost no signs at all (NHS, Mayo Clinic).

## Teething fever: how high is too high?

This is the most common question, and the most important one to get right.

- **What counts as a fever:** 100.4°F (38°C) or higher, ideally measured rectally in young babies (AAP).
- **What teething can do:** at most a *slight* rise below fever level. Cleveland Clinic and MedlinePlus say teething doesn't cause fever; the NHS describes a temperature that is slightly raised but under 38°C.
- **A wrinkle in the sources:** the AAP's teething page says the rise should not go above 101°F (38.3°C). Other AAP and clinical sources use 100.4°F (38°C) as the fever line. We'd treat **anything at 100.4°F / 38°C or above as fever, not teething.**

Call your pediatrician about a fever, and don't put it down to teething, especially if:

- your baby is **3 months old or younger** with a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher: call right away, even if they seem well (AAP);
- your baby is 3–6 months with 101°F (38.3°C) or higher, or older than 6 months with 103°F (39.4°C) or higher (AAP);
- the fever lasts more than 3 days, your baby is hard to wake, or isn't drinking enough (Cleveland Clinic);
- the temperature is above 104°F (40°C): seek emergency care (Cleveland Clinic).

## Teething rash: face yes, diaper area no

- **Drool rash on the face** (chin, cheeks, neck folds) is a genuine teething effect in Macknin's study. The NHS also mentions a flushed cheek and a facial rash. Gently wiping drool often helps prevent it (NHS, KidsHealth, MedlinePlus).
- **Diaper rash** isn't caused by teething, according to Cleveland Clinic; irritation from diapers or wipes, heat, foods, or detergents are more likely.
- **Rash elsewhere on the body, or a rash with fever**, isn't a teething sign. Call your pediatrician.

## Diarrhea, vomiting, and runny nose

None of these were linked with teething in the prospective study, and the NHS says there is no evidence teething causes diarrhea. Babies get many viral illnesses at the same age they get teeth, so the timing often overlaps. Napper (app example) points out that ear infections and teething can look alike (both can bring ear-pulling and fussiness); ear infections more often follow a cold and come with fever.

## Teething and sleep

Results differ. The NHS lists poor sleep as a possible sign, and Macknin's study linked teething with more wakefulness but not with recorded sleep disturbance. Cleveland Clinic's pediatrician says teething discomfort is mild and shouldn't seriously affect sleep. Our take: one or two rough nights around a tooth are plausible; weeks of broken sleep are unlikely to be "just teeth". For sleep problems that last, see [sleep regression ages](/sleep-regression) and our age-by-age sleep schedules. Comfort your baby, then put them back to sleep on their back in their own crib. Don't leave a bottle of milk in the crib, because the AAP warns it can cause tooth decay.

## How to soothe teething pain safely

**Recommended (AAP, FDA, NHS, Mayo Clinic, KidsHealth):**

1. **Rub the gums** with a clean finger or wet gauze; Mayo Clinic suggests about 2 minutes at a time.
2. **A firm rubber or silicone teether.** Chill it in the fridge, not the freezer: frozen teethers get too hard and can hurt the gums (AAP, NHS, FDA). Avoid liquid-filled rings that can leak (KidsHealth, MedlinePlus).
3. **A cold, wet washcloth.** KidsHealth suggests about 30 minutes in the freezer, taken out before it's rock hard and washed after each use.
4. **Cool foods for babies already on solids**, such as yogurt or applesauce (MedlinePlus), or soft fruit in a mesh feeder for children over 1 (Mayo Clinic). Always supervise because of choking risk.
5. **Pain medicine if your baby is really uncomfortable:** acetaminophen, or ibuprofen only from 6 months unless your doctor says otherwise (AAP). Dose by weight, use the syringe that comes with the medicine, and ask your pediatrician first. Mayo Clinic suggests stopping after 1–2 days.
6. **Comfort and distraction:** cuddles, play, a change of scene (NHS).

**Avoid (FDA, AAP, Mayo Clinic):**

- **Benzocaine** gels and liquids (sold under several brand names): they can cause methemoglobinemia, a rare but potentially fatal drop in the blood's ability to carry oxygen (FDA).
- **Lidocaine** (viscous) solution: can cause seizures, heart problems, and brain injury in babies (FDA).
- **Homeopathic teething tablets:** no proven benefit, and some products have contained harmful amounts of belladonna (AAP, Mayo Clinic).
- **Amber teething necklaces, bracelets, and anklets:** no benefit and a risk of choking and strangulation (FDA, AAP).
- Aspirin on the gums, alcohol rubbed on the gums, or cutting the gums (MedlinePlus).

*Note: the NHS (UK) allows pharmacy teething gels made for children as a second-line option and gives ibuprofen from 3 months. U.S. guidance from the FDA and AAP is stricter. We follow the U.S. guidance.*

## Caring for the first teeth

- Wipe the gums with a soft, clean cloth twice a day before teeth come in (CDC, Mayo Clinic).
- Once a tooth appears, brush twice a day with a soft, small brush. The AAP and Mayo Clinic say to use a smear of fluoride toothpaste the size of a grain of rice; CDC's page says to start with plain water and to ask your dentist or doctor about fluoride toothpaste before age 2. Ask your pediatrician which approach they recommend.
- Ask about fluoride varnish once the first tooth appears (CDC, Mayo Clinic).
- First dental visit within 6 months of the first tooth and no later than 12 months (AAP).

## When to call your pediatrician

- Any fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby 3 months or younger, or the higher thresholds above for older babies.
- Diarrhea, vomiting, a body rash, or a rash with fever.
- Your baby is very irritable, cries inconsolably, or can't be comforted the way they usually can (AAP, Mayo Clinic).
- Refusing to eat or drink, fewer wet diapers, or signs of dehydration.
- Unusually sleepy or hard to wake.
- Pale, gray, or bluish skin or lips, or trouble breathing (for example after a numbing gel was used): call emergency services. The FDA warns that benzocaine can sharply reduce the blood's ability to carry oxygen.
- No teeth at all by the first birthday (worth mentioning at the 12-month visit), or teeth coming in much more slowly than expected (KidsHealth suggests talking to your doctor).

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the first signs of teething?**  
Drooling, chewing on hands and toys, rubbing the gums, and mild fussiness, usually in the few days before a tooth appears.

**Can teething cause a fever?**  
Not a true fever. A slight rise in temperature is possible, but 100.4°F (38°C) or higher points to illness. Call your pediatrician.

**What does a teething rash look like?**  
Red, slightly bumpy skin on the chin, cheeks, or neck where drool sits. Wiping drool often helps. A rash elsewhere, or with a fever, isn't teething.

**Does teething cause diarrhea?**  
There's no good evidence that it does. Talk to your pediatrician if your baby has diarrhea.

**How long does teething pain last?**  
In the main study, symptoms were concentrated in about 8 days around each tooth.

**Are teething gels safe?**  
The FDA says benzocaine and lidocaine products should not be used for teething.
