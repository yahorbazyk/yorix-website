# Daylight Saving Time and Baby Sleep: Spring and Fall

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

The clock changes twice a year in much of the United States and Europe, and babies do not care what the government decided. This is one evergreen page for **both spring-forward and fall-back**. HealthyChildren.org's daylight saving article (updated 26 October 2026, by Anna Esparham, MD, FAAP) is the main parent-facing AAP source we use here. We are not giving a country-by-country calendar; check when your local clocks change.

## Short answer

Start nudging naps and bedtime **a few minutes each day, beginning about a week before** the change, then use morning daylight and a steady [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby) to help the body clock catch up. HealthyChildren.org says that in **spring**, getting children to bed or up in the morning can be a challenge, though the spring change may be a little easier on young children who already wake early. **Fall** can lead younger children to crash early and then wake too early. Most kids, in that article's words, adjust **within about a week**. Newborns already have messy clocks — see [day-night confusion](/newborn-day-night-confusion) — so a 1-hour shift is often less dramatic for a 4-week-old than for a 10-month-old with a set bedtime. We are not recommending melatonin for babies.

Sleep Foundation's bedtime-routine article, already used on our routine page, mentions shifting bedtime in 15-minute steps over a few days. That is a more specific increment than HealthyChildren.org's "a few minutes." We treat 15 minutes as one workable example, not as an AAP mandate.

## Spring: clocks jump forward (you lose an hour)

On the new clock, a baby who used to wake at 6:30 may now wake at 7:30, and the bedtime that felt "on time" will feel early for their body. The painful part is often the first morning, when you need to be somewhere at a new, earlier time.

HealthyChildren.org's approach:

- In the week before, move naps and bedtime a little later each day, so Sunday is less of a cliff.
- On the days after, open curtains and get **morning daylight**. Light is the strongest clock cue you have.
- Keep the routine in the same order even if the clock time is slightly off for a few days.
- Protect bedtime from screens for at least an hour.

If you did not prepare, you can still shift a few minutes later each day after the change. Do not keep the baby up a full extra hour on Saturday night and hope for the best; that is a good way to get an [overtired](/overtired-baby) bedtime.

## Fall: clocks fall back (you gain an hour)

The same 6:30 wake can become 5:30 on the new clock, and a toddler may be exhausted at a late-afternoon hour that used to be mid-afternoon. HealthyChildren.org flags early crashes and early mornings as the typical fall pattern.

In the week before, nudge naps and bedtime a little **earlier** each day. After the change, still use morning light, but do not start the day at 5 a.m. with a full "morning" (bright play, breakfast theater) if you are trying to slide wake-up later. Keep the room dark until a reasonable wake window, then go into daylight. For a baby who already wakes early, see [early morning waking](/early-morning-waking) — the clock change can aggravate a pattern that was already there.

## Newborns, older babies, and what "a week" looks like

Under about 8 weeks, many babies do not have a clock-stable bedtime yet. A light-and-dark contrast still helps, but you do not need a minute-by-minute DST project. From the age when a [schedule](/baby-nap-schedule-by-age) actually exists, the week-before nudge is more useful.

One-year-olds and toddlers often feel the change more because their bedtime is more set. Hold the routine and the new clock, rather than negotiating a moving target every night.

If you also need to [wake a baby](/should-you-wake-a-sleeping-baby) for a feed, keep the medical plan and shift the "extra" schedule pieces, not the required calories.

## What we are not recommending

HealthyChildren.org mentions melatonin at low pediatric doses for some children, only after talking to a pediatrician, and as part of a larger sleep plan. **That is not advice for infants.** We are not listing doses, and we are not suggesting a supplement as a daylight-saving shortcut for a baby. If a clinician raises melatonin for an older child, that conversation belongs with them.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if sleep was already a serious problem before the clock change and is still off two weeks later, or if you are considering any sleep aid. Call sooner if the baby seems unwell, not merely "on the wrong clock."

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I handle daylight saving with a baby?**  
Shift naps and bedtime by a few minutes a day for about a week, use morning light, and keep the routine. Expect about a week to adjust.

**Is spring or fall harder?**  
HealthyChildren.org: in spring, bedtime and morning can both be a struggle, but the change may be a little easier on young early risers and harder on teens. In fall, younger children may crash early and wake earlier than usual.

**Should I keep my baby on "old time"?**  
You can delay the shift by a day or two if a Sunday event is optional, but living on a private time zone usually becomes messy once childcare or work is involved.

**Can I use the clock change to fix a late bedtime?**  
Sometimes, in the spring, because the new clock already moves social time earlier relative to the body. Make only one project at a time if you can.

**Does this apply outside the United States?**  
The method is the same whenever clocks move by an hour. The March and November dates above are the U.S. ones; the UK and EU change on different dates.
