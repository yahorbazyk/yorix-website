# Night Terrors vs Nightmares: How to Tell Them Apart

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A screaming child at 10 p.m. can look like a nightmare, a tantrum, or something medical. **Night terrors** and **nightmares** are different events, at different times of night, and they need different responses. This page is not a second [night-wakings](/survive-night-wakings-new-parents) guide, and it is not the same as a baby [crying in their sleep](/baby-crying-in-sleep) during ordinary active sleep.

## Short answer

**Nightmares** are scary dreams. The child usually wakes, can often remember the dream, and wants comfort. HealthyChildren.org says they can start as early as 6 months and tend to peak between 3 and 12 years, often in the **second half of the night**. **Night terrors** happen in deep, non-dream sleep, usually **early in the night**. The child may scream, thrash, look terrified, and not recognize you — and typically **will not remember** it in the morning. HealthyChildren.org says they occur most often in toddlers and preschoolers; the NHS says they are most common between ages 3 and 8. Do **not** try to wake a child from a night terror (NHS: they may get more upset). Stay calm, keep them safe, and wait it out. If episodes happen at the same time each night, the NHS suggests waking them 15 minutes beforehand, every night for two weeks.

### Key numbers at a glance

| Feature | Night terrors | Nightmares |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When in the night | Early, often in the first few hours (deep sleep) | Later, second half of the night (dream sleep) |
| Is the child awake? | Appears distressed but is not truly awake; may not recognize you | Wakes and can usually be comforted |
| Memory the next day | Typically none | Often can recall the dream |
| How long? | NHS: up to 15 minutes. HealthyChildren.org: can last as long as 45 minutes, though most are much shorter. We are not picking one "correct" outer limit. | Until the child settles after waking |
| Common ages | NHS: most common at 3–8 years. HealthyChildren.org: most often toddlers and preschoolers | HealthyChildren.org: may start at 6 months; peak 3–12 years |

## What a night terror looks like

HealthyChildren.org lists: uncontrollable crying, sweating, shaking or fast breathing, a terrified, confused, or glassy-eyed look, thrashing, screaming, kicking or staring, not realizing you are there, and pushing you away if you try to hold them. After it ends, most children fall straight back to sleep because they were never really awake.

Night terrors can run in families and often travel with [sleepwalking](/sleepwalking-and-sleep-talking). Being very tired, unwell, or off schedule can make them more likely (NHS). They are frightening to watch and usually harmless to the child.

## What to do in the moment

**For a night terror** (NHS and HealthyChildren.org, compiled):

- Stay calm. Your job is safety, not a conversation.
- Do not try to wake them. Do not shake them, turn on bright lights, or insist they "snap out of it."
- Make sure they cannot fall, hit furniture, or leave the room unsafely. HealthyChildren.org: if they try to get out of bed, gently restrain them. NHS: do not try to stop them moving unless there is a risk of injury.
- Tell babysitters what a night terror looks like and what not to do.
- If the same clock time keeps triggering it, try the NHS scheduled-waking plan (15 minutes before, nightly for two weeks), and mention it to your pediatrician.

**For a nightmare** (HealthyChildren.org):

- Go in promptly. Reassure them that they are safe and that dreams are not real.
- Let them tell you about it if they want to. A dim light is fine if it helps.
- When they are ready, help them back to sleep. Look for an obvious scare in the room, such as a shadow, and fix it.

A consistent, calming [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby), enough total sleep (see [how much sleep a baby needs](/how-much-sleep-does-a-baby-need) and the [2-year](/2-year-old-sleep-schedule) or preschool ranges), and less late-evening screen time all reduce the "overtired or overstimulated" fuel that both problems share.

## Sleepwalking and sleep talking

Like night terrors, these are deep-sleep events: a blank stare, little response, and usually no memory in the morning. Gently guide the child back to bed and make the room safe (stairs, doors, trip hazards). There is a wording difference between sources on whether to wake them — that conflict, age figures, and when to see a GP are on [sleepwalking and sleep talking](/sleepwalking-and-sleep-talking).

## Nightmares — what to do when they wake

Nightmares are scary dreams. As the table above already says, the child usually **wakes**, often later in the night, can often **remember** the dream, and wants comfort. HealthyChildren.org: they may start as early as 6 months and tend to peak between 3 and 12 years. Caring for Kids: talk calmly, cuddle, and reassure; do **not** “search for monsters.” Tell a preschooler, simply, that it was a dream. A brief, boring return to their own bed is the goal, not a new every-night sleepover in yours unless you are choosing that for other reasons (see [fear of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark) and [toddler won't stay in bed](/toddler-wont-stay-in-bed)).

If the same nightmare keeps coming back after a frightening event, or the child cannot settle night after night, talk to the pediatrician. Recurring, very distressing dreams are a reason to ask, not a reason to invent a new diagnosis on this page.

**Sleep talking** (murmurs, odd sentences, even a short shout) often happens in the same family of partial arousals as walking or terrors, or in lighter dream sleep. It is usually harmless and not remembered. You do not need to wake the child or have a conversation. Mention it at a visit if it is new, very frequent, or comes with snoring, unusual daytime sleepiness, or other changes. Confusional arousals (looking awake but not making sense, often early in the night) stay on this page with terrors and walking; they are not a new URL.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

The NHS suggests seeing a GP if nightmares continue after age 6, are frequent, or follow a frightening event; if night terrors **start after age 5**; or if they **continue after age 12**. HealthyChildren.org is less age-specific and says to talk with the doctor if night terrors keep happening. Use the lower bar if you are worried: frequent, violent, or injurious episodes, breathing pauses or loud snoring between events, or a child who seems unusually sleepy by day all deserve a medical look. This page cannot tell a night terror from a seizure; that is a clinician's job.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between night terrors and nightmares?**  
Nightmares: the child wakes from a scary dream and remembers it, usually later at night. Night terrors: early, deep sleep, looks terrified, does not really wake, and usually has no memory.

**Should I wake my child during a night terror?**  
No. Keep them safe and wait.

**Will they remember it?**  
Usually not, which is why it is often worse for you than for them.

**Can babies have night terrors?**  
HealthyChildren.org places night terrors mostly in toddlers and preschoolers. Very young babies who cry out briefly in [active sleep](/baby-sleep-cycles) are more often doing something ordinary — see [crying in sleep](/baby-crying-in-sleep). If you are unsure, ask your pediatrician rather than labeling it.

**Do they mean my child is anxious or traumatized?**  
Not by themselves. Tiredness, illness, and family history are common contributors (NHS). Recurring nightmares after a scary event are a reason to call the doctor.

**When should I see a sleep specialist or ask for a sleep study?**
Start with your pediatrician. This page already lists breathing, snoring, and unusual-event reasons to call. A specialist or a study is their referral, not a parent DIY step . If the issue is nightmares, sleepwalking, or sleep talking rather than breathing, see [night terrors](/night-terrors), which already compares those events.
