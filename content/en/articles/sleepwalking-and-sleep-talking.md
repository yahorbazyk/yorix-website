# Sleepwalking and Sleep Talking: Keep Them Safe, Then Guide Them Back

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A child who sits up, walks, or talks without really being awake is usually in a **deep-sleep** event, not a [nightmare](/night-terrors). This page is walking and talking. The comparison of **night terrors versus nightmares** — and what to do in those moments — stays on [night terrors](/night-terrors). [Fear of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark) is a different, usually later-evening problem.

## Short answer

HealthyChildren.org (21 September 2023) groups sleepwalking and sleep talking with night terrors as events that happen in **deep sleep**. The child may have a **blank stare**, may not respond, and may be **very hard to wake**. Most sleepwalkers **return to bed on their own** and **do not remember** getting up. Sleepwalking tends to **run in families**. The same organization's older "A Lullaby for Good Health" page (20 June 2013) says sleepwalking is experienced by **as many as 40% of children, usually between ages 3 and 7**. The 2023 how-to page does not repeat that percentage. Other pediatric pages use different age bands. We will not average them.

Sleep talking is usually milder: talking, laughing, or crying out without awareness or next-day memory (HealthyChildren.org, 2013). It is still not a conversation you need to join at 11 p.m.

NHS (page last reviewed 11 April 2025): sleepwalking usually happens in the **first part of the night**, often lasts **a few minutes**, and most children **grow out of it by the time they are teenagers**. It does not usually need treatment. It can be linked with not getting enough sleep, being unwell with a temperature, stress, some medicines, and other sleep conditions such as sleep apnoea. See a GP if it is **disrupting sleep**, you are **worried about accidents**, or there are **other symptoms such as daytime tiredness or breathing that stops and starts** in sleep — that last sign belongs on [baby snoring and mouth breathing](/baby-snoring-and-mouth-breathing) as well.

### What to do in the moment — and a conflict on waking

HealthyChildren.org (2023): **do not try to wake** children when they are sleepwalking or sleep talking. **Gently lead them back to bed**; they will probably settle on their own.

NHS: try to **gently guide them back to bed**. **If you need to wake them**, wake them **gently**. A sudden wake can leave them confused, scared, angry, or upset.

We will not pretend those two instructions are identical. In practice both start with a calm guide-back and a safe room. If you must wake the child — for example because they are heading for a stair or a door — the NHS wording is to do it gently, not with a shout.

### Safety at home

From HealthyChildren.org and the NHS, in our words:

- Clear trip hazards on the floor.
- **Block stairs**; do not put a sleepwalking child in the **top of a bunk bed** (NHS).
- **Lock outside doors** (and, NHS, keep windows closed). The NHS also mentions door alarms if there is a risk of leaving the house, and locking away sharp objects.
- Do not let the event become a family performance. In the morning, a short, calm check-in is enough; many children will have no memory of it.

Enough sleep and a regular bedtime help, because being very tired is a common trigger. The NHS list also says “avoid naps during the day” as general sleep hygiene. That bullet is written for a mixed-age audience. It is **not** a reason to drop an age-appropriate nap in a toddler who still needs one — too little sleep can make these events more likely. See [how much sleep a baby needs](/how-much-sleep-does-a-baby-need) and [when do toddlers stop napping](/when-do-toddlers-stop-napping).

NHS mentions medicines that a sleep clinic might use if other things have not worked. We will not name or dose them. That is a specialist decision.

## When this is not “just sleepwalking”

Call or use urgent care if the child is injured, cannot be woken at all, is struggling to breathe, or looks the wrong colour. Talk to your pediatrician or GP if events are frequent, dangerous, or come with loud snoring, pauses in breathing, or a sudden change in daytime behaviour. Sleepwalking is not, by itself, a sign that something is “psychologically wrong,” and it is not a reason to start a formal sleep-training method in the middle of the night.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I wake a sleepwalking child?**  
HealthyChildren.org says not to try. NHS says guide them back, and if you need to wake them, do it gently. Safety comes first; a shout is not the plan.

**Is sleep talking the same thing?**  
It is the same family of deep-sleep events. You do not need to answer the words. Guide them back if they are getting up.

**Will they grow out of it?**  
NHS: most children do, by the teenage years, and treatment is often unnecessary.

**Can a nightmare cause sleepwalking?**  
Nightmares usually come later in the night and the child wakes and remembers them. Walking and talking are typically earlier, deeper-sleep events. The comparison is on [night terrors](/night-terrors).
