# 18 Month Sleep Regression: Bedtime Battles, One Nap, and a Busy Brain

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

By 18 months, many toddlers have been sleeping fairly well for a while, which makes a sudden run of bedtime protests, early wake-ups, and 2 a.m. calls for a parent all the more surprising. The "18 month sleep regression" is one of the most talked-about rough patches online, but it has less research behind it than its reputation suggests. Here is an honest look at what's known, and practical ways through it.

## Short answer

Sleep Foundation describes the **18 month sleep regression** as a phase that **can** happen, not something every toddler goes through: many children have no regression at this age, and some sleep better. When sleep does slip, it is usually linked to **bedtime resistance as independence grows, separation anxiety, more mobility, teething, and the move to one nap**. Sleep Foundation says symptoms **rarely last more than a few weeks**. Toddlers aged 1–2 need **11–14 hours** of sleep in 24 hours (AASM). Direct research on an 18-month regression is limited; the best evidence links sleep disruption to new skills, including **language milestones**.

## How strong is the evidence?

Honestly: weak for a fixed "18 month" event, better for the general idea that development and sleep interact.

- **Sleep Foundation** says toddler sleep regressions are best understood as phases that can occur in any child's development, not something destined to happen at a specific point. Toddlers may have trouble before or after 18 months, or not at all.
- **Waugh and Berger (2026)** found sleep disruptions around language milestones such as using new words and labeling objects, as well as around walking. Huckleberry (app example) also points to a burst in language development around this age, which may explain some rough nights.
- We found **no study** that measured toddler sleep and found a predictable dip at exactly 18 months.

## Signs of an 18 month sleep regression

Sleep Foundation lists:

- fussiness and resistance at bedtime;
- waking in the night, with more difficulty calming down;
- longer or more frequent daytime naps.

Huckleberry (app example) adds nap refusal, early waking, and difficulty falling asleep without help as things parents commonly report.

## What can be behind it

| Factor | What you might see | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independence | Stalling, saying "no" to bed, testing the rules | Sleep Foundation |
| Separation anxiety | Crying out after a parent leaves; trouble resettling | Sleep Foundation; KidsHealth |
| Mobility | Restlessness linked to new physical abilities | Sleep Foundation |
| Teething | Discomfort that wakes the child | Sleep Foundation |
| One-nap transition | Overtiredness while adjusting to longer awake periods | KidsHealth; Boys Town |
| Dreams and nightmares | Waking upset; harder to settle | KidsHealth |
| Screens | Harder time falling asleep | KidsHealth |

## The move to one nap

KidsHealth says most toddlers combine their two naps into a single afternoon nap at around 18 months, or sometimes sooner, and that a toddler who fights the morning nap is probably ready. Boys Town places the switch between 15 and 18 months. During the changeover, many toddlers are overtired by evening. Mayo Clinic suggests moving the afternoon nap and bedtime earlier by about half an hour once the morning nap is gone.

Don't mistake nap refusal for being done with naps altogether. A meta-analysis by Staton and colleagues found that fewer than 2.5% of children stop napping before age 2.

**App example (not clinical guidance):** Huckleberry suggests about 13–14 hours in total at 18 months, with at least 11 hours at night and 2–3 hours of day sleep.

## How to get through it

### Keep the routine short and soothing

Sleep Foundation recommends a consistent pre-bed routine of soothing activities in soft light, making sure your toddler is comfortable, and a reassuring goodnight. KidsHealth suggests keeping the routine from growing longer over time. Decide in advance how many drinks of water or "one more book" requests you will allow.

### Set limits kindly and stick to them

KidsHealth encourages setting bedtime rules and keeping to them. A predictable answer is easier for toddlers than one that changes each night.

### Respond to night calls without starting a party

Sleep Foundation suggests:

- pausing briefly before responding, to give your toddler a chance to settle;
- comforting with lights low and voices quiet, without taking them out of bed;
- if they call out repeatedly, soothing from a little farther away each time;
- not bringing them into your bed, so they learn to feel secure in their own.

KidsHealth adds that night visits should be brief and boring.

### Offer a comfort object

KidsHealth suggests a small comfort item, such as a lovey or small blanket, for reassurance. Sleep Foundation notes that a dim night light can help toddlers who are afraid of the dark.

### Mind the evening

KidsHealth recommends keeping toddlers away from screens in the hour before bed and choosing calm bedtime books. Sleep Foundation suggests getting energy out during the day, ideally outdoors in natural light.

## What can make it worse

- **Long negotiations at bedtime,** or rules that change from night to night.
- **An overtired toddler** during the one-nap switch, without an earlier bedtime.
- **Screens in the hour before bed.**
- **Bringing your child into your bed** at every waking (Sleep Foundation advises against it).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Talk to your child's doctor if sleep problems seem severe or last more than a few weeks, if your child has frequent nightmares (KidsHealth), loud snoring or pauses in breathing, or if poor sleep comes with fever, pain, or other signs of illness. Also seek help if the nights are leaving you exhausted or low.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the 18 month sleep regression real?**  
Many toddlers have a rough patch around this age, but many don't. Sleep Foundation calls it a phase that can happen, not something every child goes through.

**How long does the 18 month sleep regression last?**  
Rarely more than a few weeks (Sleep Foundation).

**Should my 18 month old be on one nap?**  
Many are. KidsHealth says most toddlers move to one afternoon nap around 18 months or sooner.

**My 18 month old refuses to nap at all. Are they done?**  
Very unlikely. Fewer than 2.5% of children stop napping before age 2 (Staton et al., 2020).

**Can I sleep train an 18 month old?**  
Behavioral approaches can be adapted for toddlers. See our [sleep training methods guide](/sleep-training-methods-explained), and check with your pediatrician if sleep problems are severe.
