# 4 Year Old Sleep Schedule: The Preschool Day, With or Without a Nap

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

This page is the **preschool timetable** at 4, and it also answers the usual 5-year questions. We will not publish a separate `/5-year-old-sleep-schedule`. The official sleep range is the same across ages 3 to 5, so the useful difference is what the afternoon looks like, and how early the morning has to be for preschool or school.

It is not [3 year old sleep schedule](/3-year-old-sleep-schedule) (the year when many families are still deciding about the nap). It is not [when do toddlers stop napping](/when-do-toddlers-stop-napping) (the process of dropping it). It is not [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles) or [toddler won't stay in bed](/toddler-wont-stay-in-bed). Use those when the fight, not the clock, is the problem.

## Short answer

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine consensus, which the AAP has endorsed and which Caring for Kids (Canadian Paediatric Society) reprints, is **10 to 13 hours in 24 hours at ages 3–5, including any nap**. KidsHealth's preschool page uses the same 10–13 hour band. There is no official AAP bedtime on the clock.

Whether a nap still happens is the part that splits families at 4 and 5. Mayo Clinic: most children still nap until about 3, and **most have stopped by 5**. Sleep Foundation, citing Weissbluth's 1995 study, gives a slower curve: almost all still nap at 3, **about 60% at 4**, **fewer than 30% at 5**, and nearly all have stopped by 7. Those two pictures agree that 5 is a common finish line; they disagree about how typical a remaining nap is at 4. We show both. We do not average them.

KidsHealth: many preschoolers already get all of their sleep at night. For those who still nap, set a regular time and a short routine; **most of those naps are about an hour**. If they will not sleep, use **quiet time**. Caring for Kids makes the same offer: after lunch, a chance to read and relax, even on days without sleep. When the nap goes, move bedtime earlier so the night can carry the total — that shift is explained on the stop-napping page, not rebuilt here.

NHS advice for young children is in the same family: a calm, predictable routine, a set bedtime, limited evening screens, and a boring response if they get up. On screens, sources do not use one number: the AAP (via HealthyChildren.org) says **at least one hour** off before bed; the NHS suggests **30 to 60 minutes**. We report both.

## What a preschool weekday can look like

These are sketches, not official timetables. Anchor the morning to the real preschool or school door, then count backward so the 10–13 hour total still fits.

**Still napping.** A fairly early morning; play and a midday meal; a regular early-afternoon sleep, often around an hour; then play, supper, and a [routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby) that is short enough that lights-out still leaves room for a long night. A late nap is what usually pushes bedtime past sense.

**No nap, or only some days.** The same kind of morning, then a protected quiet stretch after lunch (books, rest, no new show to "help them crash"). The late afternoon can get ragged. Bring the evening forward so the night, not a secret second nap at 5 p.m., holds the day's sleep. Mixed nap and no-nap days are normal in this year; the morning does better if it stays in a narrow window.

We will not add a wake-window chart for preschoolers. A steady get-up time, an honest decision about the afternoon, and an earlier night on no-nap days do more than a timer.

Caffeine has no place here (Caring for Kids: no drinks with caffeine; also watch chocolate close to bed). A comfort object and a dim [night light](/night-lights-and-baby-sleep) are reasonable if they help a child who is [afraid of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark). KidsHealth: do not start a new pattern of lying down until they fall asleep, or of falling asleep in another room, if you can help it.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if the 24-hour total is regularly short of 10 hours and days are a struggle, if loud snoring or pauses worry you (see [snoring and mouth breathing](/baby-snoring-and-mouth-breathing)), or if fears or night events need sorting ([night terrors](/night-terrors) versus nightmares).

## Frequently asked questions

**How much sleep does a 5-year-old need?**  
The same official 10–13 hour band. Many 5-year-olds no longer nap; the night has to get longer. Stay on this page for the timetable, and use [when do toddlers stop napping](/when-do-toddlers-stop-napping) for the drop itself.

**What time should a 4-year-old go to bed?**  
There is no official clock. Count back from the morning you actually have, and include any nap, so the day still lands in 10–13 hours.

**Should I keep the nap for school?**  
Only if the child still sleeps, and it is not wrecking bedtime. Quiet time can replace a nap that has already gone. Forcing a nap that will not start belongs on the stop-napping and [won't nap](/baby-wont-nap) pages, not here.
