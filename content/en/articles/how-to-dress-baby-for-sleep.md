# How to Dress a Baby for Sleep: Layers, Heat, and Room Temperature

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Parents worry about two opposite mistakes: a baby who is too cold, and a baby who is bundled into a higher risk of overheating. **How to dress a baby for sleep** is mostly about that balance. The rule you will see in AAP parent advice is simple — about **one more layer** than an adult would need in the same room — and then there is a messy disagreement about what temperature the room itself should be.

## Short answer

Dress your baby in light layers that match the room, usually a onesie or similar base layer plus, if needed, a wearable blanket (a [sleep sack](/sleep-sacks)). Keep **loose blankets, quilts, and hats** out of the sleep space in the first year. Feel the **chest or back**, not the hands, to judge warmth. Signs of overheating include sweating, damp hair, flushed cheeks, and a hot chest. On room temperature, sources do not agree on a single number: NHS and Lullaby Trust recommend **16–20°C** (61–68°F); some U.S. pages mention **68–72°F** (20–22°C); the AAP's 2022 safe-sleep policy itself does not set a range. Use a comfortable room, watch the baby, and adjust layers rather than chasing one "perfect" thermostat setting.

## Start with the baby, not the thermostat

Babies are not as good as older children at regulating temperature. They also sweat less, which is one reason overheating is treated as a SIDS risk, not just a comfort issue. The AAP's practical test is clothing: if you are comfortable in a long-sleeved shirt, your baby often needs that plus one light layer, not a fleece suit, a swaddle, and a blanket.

A typical starting point:

- **Mild room:** a short- or long-sleeved bodysuit, plus a [sleep sack](/sleep-sacks) if you want a wearable layer.
- **Cooler room:** add a light footed sleeper under the sack, or a slightly heavier sack, not a quilt.
- **Warm room or hot weather:** a single bodysuit, or a very light sack, may be enough. If you are in a T-shirt and still warm, your baby does not need an extra layer on top of that simply because "babies should be bundled."

Sleep Foundation notes that a onesie is often a good base layer, that several thin layers are easier to adjust than one thick garment, and that sleep gowns with an open bottom can make night diaper changes easier. Buttons and ties should be secure; nothing — including a pacifier — should be clipped to clothing with a cord.

## Why the room-temperature numbers disagree

This is worth spelling out, because charts on parenting sites often pick one range and present it as a universal rule.

- **NHS** tells parents not to let a baby get too hot or too cold and to keep the room at **16–20°C**.
- **Lullaby Trust** uses the same **16–20°C** range and suggests a room thermometer.
- A **HealthyChildren.org** article on how much sleep children need says the AAP and other medical organizations recommend avoiding overheating and head covering "and temperatures between 68°F to 72°F for infants and children."
- **Sleep Foundation** (updated 11 June 2026) says some studies have found **68–72°F** comfortable, then immediately adds that **the AAP refrains from providing a specific temperature range** in its policy and instead focuses on dressing for the room. The same article notes that what feels right can depend on local climate, and that strong air-conditioning or heavy winter heating can be unhelpful.

Those ranges overlap only at the warm end of the UK advice and the cool end of the U.S. figures (around 20°C / 68°F). A room at 72°F (22°C) is above the UK range; a room at 16°C (61°F) is below the U.S. figures people often quote. We are not going to declare a winner. If you live with NHS or Lullaby Trust advice, 16–20°C is the range to aim for. If you follow U.S. pediatric material, a mid-to-high 60s to low 70s Fahrenheit room, plus the one-layer rule, is the usual starting point. In both cases, **the baby's chest** is a better guide than the number on the wall.

Place the crib away from radiators, air conditioners, and sunny windows. Sleep Foundation cautions that heating has been studied as a SIDS risk factor, and that a fan, if you use one in warm weather, should not blow directly on the baby. An older California study (Coleman-Phox, Odouli, and Li, 2008) found that fan use at higher room temperatures was associated with a lower SIDS risk; that is one study, not a reason to point a fan at a newborn.

## How to tell if your baby is too hot or too cold

Feel the **chest, back, or tummy**. Hands and feet are often cooler and can fool you into adding a layer the baby does not need.

Sleep Foundation lists common signs of overheating: sweating, damp hair, red or rashy skin, reddened cheeks, quicker breathing, and a chest that is noticeably hot. The AAP similarly flags sweating, a hot chest, and flushed skin. If those appear, remove a layer and cool the room. Do not put a hat on a sleeping baby indoors; hats can contribute to overheating and can slip over the face.

If a baby is too cold, the skin on the chest may feel cool, and they may seem unsettled. Add a light layer or a wearable blanket. Sleep Foundation also lists pale or grey skin, breathing difficulty, limpness, vomiting, poor feeding, and unusual irritability as possible signs of hypothermia — those are medical signs, not a cue to pile on blankets. Seek urgent care if they appear.

If your baby has a fever, ask your pediatrician before you add layers. Over-bundling a feverish baby can make things worse.

## Blankets, sleep sacks, swaddles, and what stays out of the crib

For the first year, AAP safe-sleep advice is to keep loose blankets, pillows, quilts, and stuffed toys out of the crib. Warmth should come from clothes or a wearable blanket that cannot ride up over the face. That is the job of a [sleep sack](/sleep-sacks). A [swaddle](/how-to-swaddle-a-baby) is a newborn option only, and it has to stop when rolling starts.

Do not use weighted blankets, weighted sleepers, or weighted swaddles. The AAP and KidsHealth both rule those out.

**TOG ratings** (a measure of how warm a garment is) appear on many sleep-sack labels and in unofficial charts that pair a TOG number with a room temperature. We did not find a TOG table in the AAP, NHS, or Lullaby Trust pages we opened for this article, so we are not reproducing a manufacturer chart here. If a label uses TOG, treat it as a starting hint and still check your baby's chest.

## A word on "keep them warm so they sleep longer"

Comfort matters, but "warmer is sleepier" is not a safe rule. Overheating is one of the SIDS risks the AAP, NHS, and Lullaby Trust all highlight. A slightly cool, steadily dressed baby is the aim, not a flushed, sweaty one who looks "cozy."

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Get in touch if your baby's chest stays very warm or very cool after you change the room and the clothing, if they have a fever, or if they were born early — Sleep Foundation notes that preterm babies can take longer to regulate temperature and may need slightly warmer conditions with close watching for overheating. Call urgently for the hypothermia-type signs listed above, or if breathing looks hard.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should a newborn wear to sleep?**  
Usually a light base layer plus, if the room is not warm, a swaddle or a sleep sack. No loose blanket, no hat indoors, no weighted product.

**Is 72°F too warm?**  
It is inside the 68–72°F band some U.S. sources quote, and it is above the NHS/Lullaby Trust 16–20°C range. If the room is 72°F, dress down (often a single layer) and watch for sweating.

**Can I use a blanket if I watch the baby?**  
For the first year, AAP advice is to skip loose blankets in the sleep space, even when you are nearby. A wearable blanket is the usual alternative.

**How do I dress my baby for sleep in summer?**  
Lighter layers, a cooler room if you can, and extra attention to sweating. A fan in the room may help comfort, but do not aim it at the baby, and do not use it as a reason to ignore the rest of [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies).

**Are fleece pajamas enough on their own?**  
In a cool room, often yes, sometimes with a light sack. Fleece plus a heavy sack plus a hat is usually too much.
