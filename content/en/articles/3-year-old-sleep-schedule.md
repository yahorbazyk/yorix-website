# 3 Year Old Sleep Schedule: With a Nap, or Without

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Three is a schedule age, not a new "regression" we have chosen to publish. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AAP-endorsed) recommends **10 to 13 hours of sleep in 24 hours** for children ages 3 to 5, including any nap. KidsHealth and Cleveland Clinic use the same preschool range. This page maps **a working day at age 3**: the daily total, two sample timetables (nap still in, nap already gone), and how a fixed preschool start changes the evening. It is not a second [2-year-old sleep schedule](/2-year-old-sleep-schedule), and it is not a copy of [when toddlers stop napping](/when-do-toddlers-stop-napping). That article owns the drop itself — signs, a step-by-step change, and what to do to bedtime when the nap goes.

## Short answer

At 3, some children still take a regular afternoon nap; many are giving it up, or have already given it up (KidsHealth: many preschoolers get all of their sleep at night). For those who still nap, KidsHealth says most sleep about an hour, and that a regular time plus a short naptime routine helps. Cleveland Clinic notes that preschoolers who wake too early or have trouble falling asleep at night may not need the afternoon nap. If the nap stays, it still counts toward the 10–13 hour total. If it goes, bedtime usually needs to move earlier so the night can carry the whole amount — details on the nap-drop page.

There is no official AAP clock time for lights out. Set bedtime so that, with or without the nap, the 24-hour total is realistic before a reasonably consistent morning, especially if preschool or childcare has a fixed start.

## A typical day at 3 (examples only)

Clock hours below are sketches. Move them to match your kitchen and the preschool door.

**If they still nap**

| Clock time (sketch) | A possible shape of the day |
| --- | --- |
| 7:00 a.m. (give or take) | Up and breakfast |
| Morning | Play, time outside if you have it, then lunch |
| Early afternoon | The nap, often around an hour — KidsHealth's usual length, not a requirement |
| Afternoon | Snack and play. If the nap ends late, the evening will almost always slip |
| Early evening | Dinner, the [routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby), and lights out chosen so 10–13 hours in a day is still reachable |

**If they no longer nap**

| Clock time (sketch) | A possible shape of the day |
| --- | --- |
| 7:00 a.m. (or earlier if preschool is early) | Up and breakfast |
| Midday | Lunch, then **quiet time** even without sleep (KidsHealth: a chance to slow down) |
| Afternoon | Snack, play; watch for a late-day crash |
| Earlier evening than the nap-day plan | Dinner and bedtime moved up so the night can hold most or all of the 10–13 hours |

We will not add a separate wake-window table for this birthday. A steady morning, a clear decision about the afternoon sleep, and a slightly earlier lights-out on no-nap days do more work now than a timer.

## Bedtime and preschool mornings

The same short wind-down is still worth doing every night. If evenings have become a negotiation, that is [toddler bedtime battles](/toddler-bedtime-battles), not a schedule failure. If the child is scared of the room, use [fear of the dark](/fear-of-the-dark). Night terrors, which are different from nightmares, are on [night terrors](/night-terrors).

Preschool does not invent a new official sleep number, but it does invent a morning deadline. Guard the night. A late lights-out rarely produces an easy preschool morning. If the class still offers a rest period, treat that as a maybe-nap, not as a reason to keep a late home bedtime on days they do not sleep there.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Bring it up if your child is often below 10 hours in a day and looks worn out or hard to manage, if snoring, pauses, or mouth breathing worry you ([baby snoring and mouth breathing](/baby-snoring-and-mouth-breathing)), or if nightmares or night terrors keep returning.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much sleep does a 3-year-old need?**  
The AASM range for ages 3–5 is 10–13 hours in a day, counting a nap if they still take one. KidsHealth and Cleveland Clinic use that same band.

**Should a 3-year-old still nap?**  
Some do; many do not. Watch how the night looks after a nap or a skipped nap, rather than obeying the birthday. Use the nap-drop page if you are making the change.

**What time should a 3-year-old go to bed?**  
Choose a lights-out that, together with any afternoon sleep, still lands inside a sensible daily total before the morning you actually need. The AAP does not publish a clock time.

**Is there a 3-year sleep regression?**  
Preschool, a bigger imagination, and a fading nap can all make nights uneven. We are not publishing a separate "3-year regression" guide; use this timetable plus the battles and fears pages, and call a clinician if the change is sudden or severe.
