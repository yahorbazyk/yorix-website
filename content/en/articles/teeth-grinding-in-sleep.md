# Teeth Grinding in Sleep: Toddler Bruxism, Not a Second Teething Page

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

The grating sound from a crib or toddler bed is often **bruxism**: clenching or grinding the teeth, usually during sleep (including naps). HealthyChildren.org (last updated 17 April 2026) says it is common in toddlers and preschoolers and can even start before a child's first birthday. This page is about that habit. It is not a second [teething and sleep](/teething-symptoms) article, even though teething pain is one possible trigger.

## Short answer

Many children grind or clench and never know they are doing it. Family members hear it first. Most outgrow it without lasting damage (KidsHealth, reviewed January 2021). The two parent pages we opened do not use the same "when it stops" line, and we will not average them: HealthyChildren.org says **in most cases it disappears by age 6**; KidsHealth says **most kids outgrow bruxism when they lose their baby teeth**. Until then, routine dental visits are the way to catch wear early.

Research has not pinned down one cause (HealthyChildren.org). Contributors that dentists and pediatricians often discuss include stress (school, family, friendships, even an argument at home), teeth that do not line up, irritation in the mouth, pain from teething or an earache, allergies that affect breathing in sleep, some sleep disorders, some health conditions, and some stimulant or antipsychotic medicines, including some ADHD medicines. HealthyChildren.org also notes research linking **secondhand smoke** (tobacco or cannabis) with bruxism.

KidsHealth lists similar everyday triggers (alignment, pain, stress) and, less often, medical reasons such as hyperactivity, cerebral palsy, or some medicines. By itself, bruxism is **not** a sign of a serious mental-health problem (HealthyChildren.org), even when stress is part of the picture.

### Signs, and when it is more than a sound

| What you may notice | What sources say to do |
| --- | --- |
| Grinding noises in sleep | Common; often more bothersome to the household than to the child (KidsHealth) |
| Morning face, ear, or jaw soreness; pain with chewing; sensitive teeth | Mention it to the dentist and, if it is regular, the pediatrician |
| Chipped, cracked, or worn teeth | A dental visit; permanent teeth need protection if an older child is grinding (HealthyChildren.org) |
| Loud grinding plus a child who does not seem to get enough rest | HealthyChildren.org: a sleep study may be the next conversation — the clinician decides, not this page |

HealthyChildren.org also flags jaw soreness in older children, because grinding that continues into the teen or adult years can be linked with temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and muscle problems.

## What may help — without inventing a treatment

A calm bedtime wind-down is the first practical step both pages name: a warm bath or shower, quiet music, a book, or (on HealthyChildren.org) simple breathing; teens may want to design their own version. Screens off **at least one hour** before bed helps at every age (HealthyChildren.org). If stress is the likely fuel, KidsHealth: ask what is upsetting them and help with that, rather than only treating the sound.

Do not invent dental treatment at home. If teeth are sore or damaged, the dentist may recommend a **custom night guard** made from a mold of the child's teeth (both pages). HealthyChildren.org: over-the-counter guards exist, but a custom guard is usually more comfortable and more effective, and this is typically a conversation for **older** children when a dentist or orthodontist sees worrisome damage. We will not name a brand or a start-at-home age.

Medication changes, if a medicine is a suspected trigger, are a **pediatrician** decision (HealthyChildren.org). Sleep-related breathing problems belong on [baby snoring and mouth breathing](/baby-snoring-and-mouth-breathing), not here.

## When to talk to the dentist or pediatrician

See the dentist if you hear regular grinding, if there is morning pain, or if teeth look worn or chipped. See the pediatrician as well if sleep seems poor, breathing in sleep worries you, a medicine might be involved, or you have questions about stress or another condition. In rare cases, KidsHealth says, everyday stress relief is not enough; if the child cannot sleep or is acting unlike themselves, work with the dentist or doctor on a plan.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is this just teething?**  
Teething pain can be a trigger, but bruxism is its own habit and can last after a tooth has come through. Use [teething and sleep](/teething-symptoms) for the eruption itself.

**Will they damage their adult teeth?**  
Most young children outgrow grinding before that is a long-term problem. HealthyChildren.org: take chipped, cracked, or worn teeth in an older child seriously, so permanent teeth can be protected.

**Can I buy a guard at the pharmacy tonight?**  
Ask the dentist first. Both pages point to a custom guard if one is needed, not to a casual over-the-counter fix.
