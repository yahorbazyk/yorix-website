# Introducing Allergens to Your Baby: When, How, and What to Watch For

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or an allergist. If your baby has severe eczema, has reacted to a food, or you have concerns about allergy, talk to your pediatrician before introducing allergens. If your baby has trouble breathing, swelling of the face or tongue, or becomes pale or floppy after eating, call emergency services.*

For years parents were told to hold off on peanut and egg "just in case". The advice has flipped: large studies showed that **waiting doesn't prevent food allergy**, and for peanut, early regular eating can **lower** the risk. This page explains the current U.S. guidance, which babies need a doctor's input first, how to serve peanut, egg, and other allergens safely, and how to recognize a reaction.

## Short answer

Introduce common allergens **around 6 months**, once your baby has started solids and handled a few first foods. For most babies, there's **no need to delay** egg, peanut, dairy (yogurt, cheese), wheat, soy, fish, shellfish, sesame, or tree nuts (AAP, CDC, KidsHealth). Offer **one new food at a time**, start with a small taste, and once it's tolerated, **keep it in the diet regularly**. Babies with **severe eczema and/or an egg allergy** are at high risk of peanut allergy: talk to your pediatrician early (at the 2- or 4-month visit), because the NIAID guideline recommends introducing peanut to them **as early as 4–6 months**, often after testing. **Never give whole nuts or thick chunks of nut butter**: the AAP lists them as choking hazards and advises keeping high-risk foods away until about age 4.

## Why early introduction?

The turning point was the **LEAP study** (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 2015). Researchers randomly assigned 640 babies aged 4 to 11 months with severe eczema, egg allergy, or both to **eat or avoid peanut** until age 5. Among babies whose skin test was negative at the start, **13.7%** of the avoidance group had peanut allergy at 5 years compared with **1.9%** of the peanut-eating group. NIAID's parent summary describes this as an **81% reduction** in risk. A follow-up of the same children showed the protection lasted (AAP).

The AAP adds that studies have also found protective effects from early **egg** and from infant-appropriate forms of **dairy**. The NHS states that delaying peanut and hen's egg beyond 6–12 months may increase the risk of allergy to them.

## Which foods count as "allergens"?

The foods most likely to cause allergic reactions in babies include (AAP, KidsHealth, NHS):

- **peanut** and **tree nuts** (cashew, almond, walnut, and others);
- **egg**;
- **cow's milk** products (yogurt, cheese);
- **wheat**;
- **soy**;
- **fish** and **shellfish**;
- **sesame**.

The NHS list for the UK is longer: it also names celery, mustard, beans, lentils and peas, and sulphur dioxide (found in some dried fruit and juices).

## Who should talk to a doctor first? The NIAID risk groups

The 2017 NIAID addendum guidelines, endorsed by the AAP, sort babies into three groups for peanut:

| Group | Who | Advice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — High risk | **Severe eczema** (persistent or recurring, often needing prescription creams) and/or **egg allergy** | Talk to your doctor early, ideally at the 2- or 4-month visit. Peanut is recommended as early as **4–6 months**. Your doctor may order a blood or skin-prick test; the first taste may happen at home, in the office, or at a specialist's, depending on results. |
| 2 — Moderate | **Mild to moderate eczema** | Introduce peanut **around 6 months**; no testing needed. Talk with your doctor about your baby's needs. |
| 3 — Low | **No eczema or food allergy** | Introduce peanut along with other foods, in line with your family's preferences. |

Sources: NIAID parent summary (January 2017); HealthyChildren.org (AAP).

**Also talk to your pediatrician first** if your baby has a diagnosed food allergy or eczema, or close family members have allergies, eczema, or asthma (KidsHealth, NHS).

## How to introduce allergens step by step

1. **Start solids first.** Offer a few low-risk first foods (for example infant cereal or pureed fruit) so you know your baby can manage solids (AAP).
2. **Choose a good time.** Offer a new allergen **at home**, earlier in the day, when your baby is well, so you can watch them for the next couple of hours *(practical suggestion based on NHS timing)*.
3. **One new allergen at a time.** The AAP's allergen page says to wait at least a day before the next new food; the AAP's solids page and CDC say every 3–5 days for new foods in general. Either way, don't introduce two new allergens on the same day.
4. **Start small.** A small taste first; if there's no reaction, increase the amount gradually (AAP).
5. **Keep it going.** Once tolerated, keep the food in your baby's diet regularly. The AAP gives examples of ongoing portions such as **2 teaspoons of peanut butter** (thinned) or **about ⅓ of a well-cooked egg**. The NHS also says tolerated allergens should become part of the usual diet.

## How to serve common allergens safely

| Allergen | Baby-safe ways to offer it | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peanut | Smooth peanut butter mixed into a puree, or foods made with peanut butter (AAP) | Whole peanuts or pieces, chunky peanut butter, thick globs of nut butter (AAP, NIAID) |
| Tree nuts | Smooth nut butters such as cashew (AAP); finely ground nuts (NHS) | Whole nuts |
| Egg | Well-cooked egg, such as scrambled or hard-boiled (AAP) | Raw or lightly cooked egg (NHS: unless it has the UK red lion stamp) |
| Dairy | Yogurt without added sugar, cheese (CDC) | Cow's milk as a drink before 12 months (CDC) |
| Wheat | Wheat-containing infant cereal or other soft wheat foods *(common examples)* | — |
| Sesame | Sesame tahini (AAP) | Whole seeds (NHS: serve them finely ground) |
| Fish | Soft, cooked fish with skin and all bones removed (CDC) | Bones (CDC) |
| Shellfish | Well-cooked and finely chopped | Raw or lightly cooked shellfish (NHS) |
| Soy | Soft tofu *(common example)* | — |

## Signs of an allergic reaction

Most reactions happen **within minutes**, but symptoms can take **up to 2 hours**, and for cow's milk allergy up to **3 days** (NHS).

**Mild to moderate signs** (NHS, AAP):

- hives or a red, itchy rash, often around the mouth or on the face;
- worsening eczema;
- red, watery eyes, sneezing, runny nose;
- vomiting, diarrhea, tummy pain.

**Baby-specific signs** listed by the AAP: eye rubbing, repeatedly sticking the tongue out, unusual crying, back arching, and reduced muscle tone.

**Signs of anaphylaxis — call 911 (or your local emergency number) immediately** (NHS, AAP):

- trouble breathing, wheezing, throat tightness, or coughing that won't settle;
- swelling of the throat or tongue;
- skin much paler than usual, floppiness (reduced muscle tone), fainting, or unresponsiveness.

The AAP says **epinephrine** is the main treatment for anaphylaxis and should be given right away (if prescribed), followed by a call to 911; antihistamines don't replace epinephrine.

**If you see a mild reaction:** stop the food and call your pediatrician (AAP). Don't remove whole food groups on your own; the NHS warns this can leave a child short of nutrients.

## Common worries

**"Should I avoid allergens while pregnant or breastfeeding?"** The NHS says there's no need to avoid foods like peanut unless you're allergic yourself.

**"Can I introduce peanut before solids are going well?"** Introduce a few other solid foods first (NIAID, AAP).

**"What if my family doesn't eat peanut?"** For low-risk babies, NIAID says you don't need to feel compelled to introduce it early; for high-risk babies, early introduction is recommended, so talk to your doctor.

**"What about allergen powders and mix-in products?"** We didn't find official guidance on specific commercial products in the sources we reviewed. Regular, family foods in safe forms meet the guidance; ask your pediatrician if you're considering a product.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

- **Before** introducing peanut if your baby has severe eczema or an egg allergy (ideally at the 2- or 4-month visit).
- If your baby has had **any** reaction to a food, or you suspect one.
- If eczema flares every time you introduce a certain food.
- If you're unsure how to fit allergens into a vegetarian, vegan, or restricted family diet.

## Frequently asked questions

**When should I introduce allergens to my baby?**  
Around 6 months for most babies, once they've started solids and tolerated a few first foods. High-risk babies may start peanut at 4–6 months under a doctor's guidance.

**When can babies have peanut butter?**  
From around 6 months for most babies, as smooth peanut butter mixed into a puree or in foods made with peanut butter, never as whole peanuts or chunky peanut butter.

**When can babies have eggs?**  
Well-cooked egg can be introduced from around 6 months along with other first foods.

**How long should I wait between allergens?**  
At least a day (AAP allergen guidance); many families wait 3–5 days between new foods (AAP, CDC).

**How do I know if my baby is allergic?**  
Watch for hives, swelling, vomiting, or coughing within minutes to 2 hours. Breathing trouble or swelling of the tongue or throat is an emergency.

**Do I need to keep giving the allergen after the first try?**  
Yes. Regular eating after introduction is part of the protective effect seen in studies.
