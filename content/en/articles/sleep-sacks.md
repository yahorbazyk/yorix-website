# Sleep Sacks: A Safer Alternative to a Loose Blanket

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A **sleep sack** — also called a wearable blanket — is a sleeveless (or short-sleeved) bag that zips or snaps over a baby's usual sleep clothes. It is one of the few extra layers the American Academy of Pediatrics is comfortable with in the crib, because it warms the body without leaving a loose blanket near the face. This page is about that product category, not about room temperature or how to wrap a newborn. Those are in [how to dress a baby for sleep](/how-to-dress-baby-for-sleep) and [how to swaddle a baby](/how-to-swaddle-a-baby).

## Short answer

Yes, a well-fitting sleep sack can be a safe way to keep a baby warm in the first year, when loose blankets should stay out of the crib. Choose a sack that is the right size, does not cover the head or restrict the hips, and is not weighted. A swaddle-style sack that holds the arms has to come off when your baby starts trying to roll. An arms-free sack can continue after that. We did not find a medical-source age at which every child must stop using one; many toddlers keep a wearable blanket until they can use a regular blanket safely, which is after the first birthday at the earliest.

## Why a sack is not the same thing as a blanket

The risk with a loose blanket is that it can end up over a baby's face or around the neck. A sleep sack goes on like clothing: the zipper or snaps keep it in place, and a properly designed neck opening is too small for the baby's head to slip inside. That is why AAP parent advice names "wearable blankets, like a sleeping sack" as a safer way to add warmth than a quilt or a loosely tucked covering.

It still has to fit. A sack that is far too big can ride up. A sack that is too tight at the chest or hips works against comfort and, if it straightens the legs, against healthy hip position. The IHDI's advice for commercial "sleepsacks specifically designed for swaddling" is that they should have a loose pouch for the legs and feet; the same idea applies to an ordinary arms-free sack — the baby should be able to flex and move the hips.

## Sleep sack vs swaddle

These two get mixed up because some products do both jobs.

| | Swaddle or swaddle sack | Arms-free sleep sack |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Limits arm movement to reduce startle in newborns | Adds warmth like a blanket, with arms free |
| When it is appropriate | Only before the baby starts trying to roll | Before and after rolling, if it fits |
| Hip position | Must stay loose at the hips | Must stay loose at the hips |
| Weighted versions | Do not use | Do not use |

If the product holds the arms against the body, treat it as a swaddle and follow the stop-at-rolling rule. If the arms are free, you can keep using it after rolling starts, which is the point of switching from a wrap to a sack.

## Fit, fabric, and what "TOG" can and cannot tell you

Useful fit checks, drawn from AAP and IHDI principles plus ordinary product-safety sense:

- The neck opening cannot slip down over the baby's mouth or up over the face.
- Armholes are snug enough that the baby cannot slip down inside the sack.
- There is room for a diapered baby to frog-leg, not a tight tube around the thighs.
- The fabric is light enough for your room (see the dressing article for the one-extra-layer rule and the 16–20°C vs 68–72°F temperature disagreement).
- There is no hood, no loose ribbon, and no attached toy or pacifier clip.

**TOG** is a lab measure of thermal insulation used on many UK and European sacks. Manufacturer charts often pair 0.5, 1.0, 2.5 TOG and so on with room temperatures. We opened AAP, NHS, and Lullaby Trust safer-sleep pages for this package and did not find an official TOG table, so any such chart should be treated as **unverified by those medical sources** — a label hint, not a clinical standard. Check the baby's chest, not only the number on the tag.

Replace a sack that is worn through, has a broken zipper, or that your baby has grown out of (when the neck opening looks wide or the hips look cramped). Follow the maker's size range, but do not "size up" a long way for longevity if that leaves a loose neck.

## When can a toddler use a regular blanket instead?

Sleep Foundation's page on blankets, and AAP safe-sleep advice, keep soft, loose objects out of the sleep space for the **first 12 months**. After the first birthday the suffocation risk is lower, but a small toddler can still get tangled in a heavy quilt. Many families keep a sleep sack well into the second or third year simply because it stays on. That choice is not something we found a hard AAP cut-off for. When you do introduce a blanket, start with a small, light one and keep the crib or bed otherwise uncluttered. Pillows are a separate, later question and are not needed for infant sleep.

## What a sleep sack will not do

It will not teach a baby to sleep through the night, fix [short naps](/short-naps), or replace [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) habits. It will not make an unsafe surface (a sofa, a lounger, a car seat in the house) safe. And a sack under a loose blanket puts you back where you started.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask if your baby has a hip condition, was born very early, or seems to overheat in every sack you try. Stop using a product and seek advice if the fabric covers the face, if breathing looks strained, or if the baby works their body down inside the sack.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are sleep sacks safe for newborns?**  
An arms-free sack can be, if it fits and you are not adding loose blankets. A swaddle sack is only for the pre-rolling stage. Newborns can also sleep in ordinary layers with no sack at all.

**Can my baby roll in a sleep sack?**  
In an arms-free sack, yes — and that is expected once rolling starts. That is why the arms need to be out. Practice supervised tummy time so rolling skill can develop.

**Do I need different sacks for summer and winter?**  
Often a lighter and a slightly warmer sack, or a single light sack with a change of clothes underneath, is enough. Follow the room and the baby's chest, not a rigid seasonal wardrobe.

**Can I use a sleep sack in a car seat or stroller?**  
A bulky sack can interfere with harness fit, which is a crash-safety issue, not a sleep-sack issue. Use clothing that allows a snug harness; do not put the harness over a thick bag if it leaves slack. Check the car-seat manual.

**Is a wearable blanket the same as a "sleep bag"?**  
Yes. "Sleep bag," "sleep sack," and "wearable blanket" are names for the same idea.

**What TOG rating should I buy?**  
TOG is a lab insulation number used on many UK and European bags, not an AAP, NHS, or Lullaby Trust medical standard. Use the maker’s chart only as a starting hint, then check the baby’s chest (see “Fit, fabric, and what TOG can and cannot tell you” above, and [how to dress a baby for sleep](/how-to-dress-baby-for-sleep)). Choose a bag that fits the baby’s **weight**, has no hood, and cannot ride up over the face. Do not “size up” so far that the neck opening is loose.
