# When Can a Baby Have a Lovey? After the First Birthday, Cautiously

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A "lovey" is a small comfort object — a thin comforter, a small soft toy, or another special item a child holds at bedtime. This page is that object. Loose blankets, pillows, and wearable sacks stay on [when can baby sleep with a blanket](/when-can-baby-sleep-with-a-blanket) and [sleep sacks](/sleep-sacks). We do not review brands.

## Short answer

**In the first year, keep comfort objects out of the sleep space.** The 2022 AAP safe-sleep recommendations, and the parent pages already used on our blanket article, treat stuffed toys and other soft objects the same way as loose blankets: they can cover a face or create a pocket, and they do not belong in a crib, bassinet, or play yard for an infant.

After **12 months**, U.S. public-health wording is more open, but it is not a guarantee. Michigan DHHS (an infant safe-sleep FAQ we opened for this page): babies should have nothing in the sleep area until they are **at least one year of age**. The same page adds that research has **not** shown when these objects are 100% safe, though **most experts agree they pose little risk to healthy babies after 12 months**. HealthyChildren.org's "A Lullaby for Good Health" (last updated 20 June 2013) similarly says that after one year of age, a child may pick a doll, blanket, stuffed animal, or other soft object as a bedtime companion. That is an older parent page; it still lines up with the 12-month line, not with a younger "starter lovey" in the crib.

**Red Nose Australia is not worded the same way.** It strongly recommends no soft toys or comforters from birth to **7 months**, corrected for prematurity — the period when most sudden unexpected deaths in infancy occur. It still encourages **nothing in the sleep space for the first 12 months**, and then says that from 7 months some families may *weigh risks and benefits* of a small item for separation anxiety. If one is used, it should be small, not pillow-like, and without a blanket skirt or long fabric attachments a baby can fold into the mouth; the face and head must stay uncovered; take it out if the baby buries their face in it, cannot move it away, uses it as a pillow, or chews it as a choke risk. We will not average 7 months and 12 months. For a U.S. reader, the AAP first-year bare-crib rule is the one to follow unless your own pediatrician has given you different, individual advice.

A lovey is not a SIDS-prevention tool, not a sleep-training method, and not a reason to add a pillow or a second stuffed zoo.

### Types, generically

| Kind of item | First year in the crib? | After 12 months, if you add one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small soft toy or thin comforter | No | Prefer small, simple, not pillow-like, no loose strings or long floppy parts |
| A muslin square the baby has handled in play | Still a soft object — not for unsupervised sleep in the first year | Only if it cannot drape over the face; this is not a loophole |
| A pillow, quilt, or "nook" cushion | No | Still a poor sleep-space choice; see the blanket page |
| A weighted comforter or weighted stuffed toy | No | The AAP recommends against weighted blankets, sleepers, and swaddles; a "weighted lovey" is not an exception we can bless |
| Several toys at once | No | One familiar item is enough; a pile is bedding |

You can still use a future lovey **during awake, supervised play** in the first year so the texture is familiar, then leave it out of the crib until the birthday line you and your pediatrician are following.

## How to introduce one later

Wait until your child is past 12 months, or later if they still mouth everything, have unusual tone or a breathing problem, or sleep in a space that is already crowded. Pick one small item. Add it at bedtime as part of the [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby), not as a midnight surprise. If it becomes the only way they will settle, it is now a [sleep association](/sleep-associations) — usually a manageable one, because the object can stay in the crib, unlike being rocked.

Keep a spare if you can, and wash it the way the label allows. Do not pin, tie, or clip it to clothing or to the crib. Check CPSC or your country's regulator for recalls.

## When to get advice

Ask your pediatrician before adding any soft object if your child was preterm, has a breathing or tone problem, or still gets into odd positions they cannot get out of. Urgent care is for a baby who cannot be woken, who is struggling to breathe, or whose colour changes suddenly.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can a baby have a lovey in the crib?**  
Under U.S. safe-sleep advice: not in the first year. After 12 months, a small item may be added for a healthy toddler, with the caveats above. Red Nose's 7-to-12-month wording is more conditional; we show that conflict rather than splitting the difference.

**Can I start earlier if I stay in the room?**  
A parent in the room does not turn a soft object into an approved infant sleep product. Supervised play is the right place to practice with it.

**Is a lovey safer than a blanket?**  
Both are soft objects. After 12 months, a small, light item is still a better starting point than a large quilt. See the blanket page for loose bedding.

**Will a lovey stop night waking?**  
It can become a useful cue for some toddlers, especially around [separation anxiety](/separation-anxiety-and-sleep). It is not a cure for hunger, illness, or an unsafe sleep space.
