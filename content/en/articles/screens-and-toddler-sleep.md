# Screens and Toddler Sleep: Keep the Bedroom and the Wind-Down Clear

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A tablet in the crib, a cartoon as the last step of the routine, a TV left on "for company" — this page is the **sleep** question, not a general lecture on every hour of media in the day. It is not a second [baby bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby) essay.

## Short answer

Pediatric sources link heavy or poorly timed screen use with **unhealthy sleep**, including in early childhood. HealthyChildren.org's 2026 explanation of AAP digital-ecosystem policy lists unhealthy sleep among the harms of misused digital media in the 0–5 year group, and it asks families to carve out **screen-free time**, starting with bedrooms and mealtimes — **especially an hour before bed**.

An AAP Center of Excellence Q&A on screens and sleep (the question was about a 12-year-old; the sleep tips are still AAP parent advice) says: **avoid using screens 1 hour before bed**, keep phones and screens **out of the bedroom at night**, and if there is evening screen time, aim to end it at least an hour before bedtime. We report that one-hour figure as AAP wording. We do not treat it as a toddler-only clinical trial, and we do not invent a different official clock for a 2-year-old.

For the youngest children, a 2016 HealthyChildren.org page still on the site (David L. Hill, MD, FAAP) says pediatricians think it is a bad idea for children to watch TV or use mobile apps **before age 18 months**, with **video-chat** with a distant loved one as the usual exception, and that early screen viewing contributes to **problems with sleep and attention**. That same 2016 page also caps "electronic entertainment" at **1 hour a day from 18 months to age five**. The 2026 AAP policy explanation, by contrast, asks families to move **beyond simple screen-time limits** and to focus on quality, context, and conversation. Those two parent-facing messages sit next to each other. We will not pretend only one of them exists.

## Why screens collide with sleep

The AAP Q&A notes that screen light can disrupt melatonin, and that intense content can keep a child alert. That mechanism is described for older children in that piece; it is a reason families also keep bright, exciting shows out of a toddler's wind-down, not a licence to diagnose a sleep disorder from one cartoon.

Background TV still counts as a screen in the 2016 parent page: even if "no one is watching," it can pull adult language and attention away from the child. A bedroom that stays a boring, dark, safe place to sleep is the practical overlap with [safe sleep for babies](/safe-sleep-for-babies) and [baby bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby).

## What to try instead of a show at lights-out

A bath, a book, a song, and the same short sequence every night does not need a screen to "work." If a show has become the only way they will get into pyjamas, shrink it earlier in the evening, keep it out of the sleep space, and put the last 30–60 minutes back on the routine page — that is the AAP one-hour idea used as a family experiment, not a second official clock.

This page is not a product review of "sleep" apps or YouTube channels.

## When screens are not the first problem

Illness, pain, a new sibling, or a bedtime that is simply at the wrong part of their day will not be fixed by deleting one app. If breathing, snoring, or unusual sleepiness worries you, see [baby snoring and mouth breathing](/baby-snoring-and-mouth-breathing) and [sick baby sleep](/sick-baby-sleep) and call.

## When to get clinical advice

Ask if sleep has collapsed *and* daytime behaviour or language concerns you, or if you cannot get devices out of the crib or bedroom and nights are unsafe. Urgent care is for a child who cannot be woken, who is struggling to breathe, or whose colour changes suddenly.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it OK to put a toddler to sleep with a cartoon?**  
AAP/HC advice is to keep screens out of the bedroom and to treat the time just before bed as screen-free, including about an hour. A show earlier in the evening, in a shared room, is a different choice from a tablet in the crib.

**What about a baby under 18 months?**  
The 2016 HealthyChildren.org page advises against TV and apps at that age, with video-chat as the usual exception. Sleep is one of the reasons, not the only one.

**Does the AAP still say one hour a day?**  
A 2016 parent page on the same site still does. The 2026 policy explanation asks families to go beyond a single daily cap. Ask your pediatrician how they apply current AAP advice to *your* child.

**Is a white-noise app a "screen"?**  
A glowing phone in the crib is still a device in the sleep space. If you use sound, keep the screen dark and follow volume guidance on [white noise for baby sleep](/white-noise-for-baby-sleep).
