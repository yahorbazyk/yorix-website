# Baby White Noise: Does It Help, and How to Use a Sound Machine Safely

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A steady hum from a fan, a shushing app, or a dedicated sound machine is one of the most common tools in a baby's room. It can mask a barking dog or a sibling's footsteps, and for some babies it becomes a familiar part of falling asleep. The catch is volume: sound machines can be loud enough to be a hearing concern if they are used carelessly. Here is what the evidence actually shows, and how to use one sensibly.

## Short answer

White noise may help some babies fall asleep. In a small 1990 trial, **80% of newborns fell asleep within 5 minutes** with white noise, compared with 25% without it. Volume is the main safety issue. A 2014 study in *Pediatrics* found that **all 14 infant sleep machines tested, at maximum volume, exceeded 50 dBA at 30 cm**, the noise limit recommended for hospital nurseries, and three exceeded 85 dBA. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises that if you use a sleep machine, you **place it as far from your baby as possible, keep the volume as low as possible, and limit how long it runs**.

## Does white noise help babies sleep?

The most quoted trial is small and old. Spencer and colleagues studied 40 newborns aged 2–7 days in a randomized trial. With white noise, 16 of 20 fell asleep within five minutes; without it, only 5 of 20 did. The authors concluded white noise "may help" parents settle unsettled babies.

What this means in practice:

- **It can help with settling,** especially in a noisy home.
- **It isn't proven to make babies sleep longer overall,** and the trial didn't test months of nightly use.
- **The AAP mentions possible benefits** of sleep machines alongside the hearing concern, which is why its advice is about safer use rather than avoidance.

## Is white noise safe for babies?

The concern is not the sound itself but **how loud and how close** it is, and for **how long**.

In the 2014 *Pediatrics* study, researchers played 14 infant sleep machines at full volume and measured them at three distances, adjusted for a 6-month-old's ear canal. At 30 cm (about a foot), every machine was louder than 50 dBA, the noise limit recommended for babies in hospital nurseries. Three exceeded 85 dBA, a level that, over more than 8 hours, would exceed adult workplace limits for accumulated noise exposure. The authors warned that these machines can produce sound levels that may damage infant hearing.

HealthyChildren.org (AAP) puts it simply: some machines can produce hazardous noise levels, so if you use one, keep it as far from your baby's head as possible and use it for a short time only. The AAP's environmental health guidance repeats the same three rules: far away, low volume, limited duration. The AAP's 2023 technical report on noise adds that infants cannot remove themselves from loud situations and depend on adults to do it for them.

## How loud should white noise be?

The sources we checked give a clear principle (as quiet as works), but the AAP pages we opened do not give a single decibel figure for home use. Here is how to read the numbers you will see online:

| Figure you may see | Where it comes from | How to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 dBA | The hospital nursery noise limit used as the benchmark in the 2014 *Pediatrics* study | A sensible upper reference point; quieter is better |
| 85 dBA | Adult occupational limit for long exposure, cited in the same study | Far too loud for a baby's room |
| "No louder than 50 dB and 7 feet (200 cm) away" | Widely quoted on parenting sites, including Huckleberry, which attributes it to the AAP | **Not verified:** we could not find these exact numbers in the AAP pages and abstracts we opened |

**A practical check (our suggestion, not an official rule):** stand where your baby's head will be. If you need to raise your voice to talk over the machine, it is too loud. A free sound-meter app can give a rough reading, though phone measurements are not precise.

## Where to put a sound machine

- **Across the room, not in or on the crib.** Distance lowers the volume that reaches your baby's ears (the 2014 study measured the effect at 30, 100, and 200 cm).
- **Cords out of reach.** KidsHealth warns that cords within reach of a crib, from a sitting or standing position, are a hazard.
- **Nothing added to the sleep space.** The AAP's safe sleep advice is a bare crib with only a fitted sheet. Plush toys with built-in sound don't belong in it.
- **Pointed away from the crib,** if the device has a speaker direction.

## How long should it run?

The AAP advises limiting the duration. Options families use:

- Running it only while your baby falls asleep, using a timer.
- Lowering it once your baby is in deep sleep.
- Keeping all-night use for genuinely noisy situations, such as traffic or a shared room, at the lowest volume that works.

## Types of noise: white, pink, brown, or nature sounds

You will see "pink noise" and "brown noise" marketed for babies. These are names for sounds with more energy at lower pitches, which many people find softer to listen to. In the sources we checked, we found **no study comparing noise colors for infant sleep**, so we can't recommend one over another. Whichever you pick, the same rules apply: steady sound, low volume, and distance. Avoid tracks that loop with sudden changes, music with lyrics, or a sound that suddenly cuts out.

## When to stop using white noise

There is no medical age at which white noise must stop. Families usually phase it out when:

- the home is quieter, or the baby no longer seems to rely on it to settle;
- the baby moves to a new room or bed;
- travel makes carrying a machine inconvenient.

To wean gently, lower the volume a little every few nights, or use it for falling asleep only and let a timer switch it off. These are practical suggestions, not clinical guidance.

## What can make it worse

- **Maximum volume, close to the crib.**
- **Using it to drown out a baby who seems unwell.** White noise should never replace checking on your baby.
- **Relying on it for every sleep, everywhere,** so your baby struggles without it.
- **A device inside the sleep space** or cords within reach.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Ask your pediatrician if your baby doesn't startle at loud sounds, doesn't turn toward voices as they get older, or if you have concerns about hearing. Also talk to them if your baby only sleeps with very loud noise, or if sleep problems persist despite good habits.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it safe to use white noise all night?**  
The AAP advises limiting duration. If you need it all night, keep it as far away and as quiet as possible.

**How far should a white noise machine be from a baby?**  
The AAP says as far away as possible. The often-quoted "7 feet" figure is attributed to the AAP online, but we could not verify it in AAP documents.

**What decibel is safe for babies?**  
The 2014 study used 50 dBA (the hospital nursery limit) as its benchmark. Aim for well below what you would need to talk over.

**Can babies become dependent on white noise?**  
Some may get used to it as a sleep cue. Gradually lowering the volume can help if you want to stop.

**Is pink noise better than white noise for babies?**  
We found no infant study comparing them.
