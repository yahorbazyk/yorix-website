# When Do Babies Sleep Through the Night — and What Does That Phrase Mean?

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Few baby-sleep questions are asked with more hope, or more confusion, than **when do babies sleep through the night**. The phrase itself is slippery. Some parents mean "from 7 p.m. to 7 a.m." Research often means something much shorter. And a baby who is growing well can still wake after the age when internet charts say they "should" be done. This page is about that milestone and its definitions. For getting through the hard nights, see [baby waking at night](/survive-night-wakings-new-parents). For hourly wakings, see [baby waking every hour](/baby-waking-every-hour).

## Short answer

Many babies begin to manage a longer stretch of night sleep around **4 to 6 months**, and Sleep Foundation, citing research, says most sleep "through the night" around **6 months**. In studies, that often means **at least six uninterrupted hours**, even if the baby stirs and resettles. It does not usually mean a 12-hour, no-waking night. A 2018 *Pediatrics* study (Pennestri and colleagues) found that a large share of infants were still not meeting that six-hour definition at 6 months, and a substantial share still were not at 12 months — and those babies were not developmentally behind. Cleveland Clinic says most babies start sleeping through the night by 6 months, which lines up with the "most," not "all," wording. Newborns are not expected to do this; they need frequent feeds.

## The definition problem

If you expect a 12-hour night and your 6-month-old sleeps from 7 p.m. to 2 a.m., you may think something is wrong. In the Pennestri study, that stretch would count. HealthyChildren.org, in a 2013 parent book extract, goes further for young babies: a "good sleeper" at that age is a child who wakes often but can get back to sleep, **not** a child who sleeps 10 hours without waking. The same page argues that frequent waking is developmentally appropriate and lets a baby rouse if they are not getting enough oxygen. "Sleeping undisturbed for prolonged periods at this age is not healthy" is their wording for early infancy — which is a very different message from "your 8-week-old should be sleeping through."

So when a friend says their baby "slept through" at 10 weeks, ask what they mean. A five-hour stretch after a late evening feed is not the same as dropping all night care.

## What the first year actually looks like

**Newborns (0–3 months).** Sleep is spread across day and night in short bursts. NHS is frank: some babies sleep much more than others, and the pattern will not line up with your need for sleep. Helping the body clock along — light and ordinary noise by day, dim and boring nights — is useful, but it does not produce a full night yet. See [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule).

**Around 4 to 6 months.** Sleep cycles lengthen, many babies start to put more sleep into the night, and a stretch of five to eight hours becomes realistic for some, according to Sleep Foundation and MedlinePlus material it cites. This is also when the [4 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/4-months) can make nights look worse before they look better, because sleep is becoming more like an older infant's, with more brief awakenings between cycles.

**6 to 12 months.** "Most" is not "everyone." The Pennestri figures above are worth sitting with: if roughly one in three 6-month-olds in that study were not yet doing six-hour stretches, a regularly waking 6-month-old is not an outlier by default. By 9 to 12 months, Sleep Foundation says most infants can go longer at night without a feed, and many families see eight or nine hours. Separation anxiety, teething, and standing in the crib can still interrupt that progress. Our month guides from [6 months](/6-month-old-sleep-schedule) to [12 months](/12-month-old-sleep-schedule) cover those bumps without expecting a perfect night.

## What helps — and what is just a hope

Habits that have some support:

- A predictable [bedtime routine](/bedtime-routine-for-baby). Mindell's work, discussed on that page, shows routines can help children fall asleep sooner and wake less.
- A calm, dark, safe sleep space. Bed-sharing is not a shortcut to longer sleep; Sleep Foundation, citing Merck Manual, notes it may hinder sleeping through and raises SIDS risk. See [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies).
- Putting the baby down drowsy but awake, once they are past the newborn weeks, so they have practice falling asleep in the crib (HealthyChildren.org; Sleep Foundation).
- Keeping night care boring: dim light, little talk, no play, as NHS and HealthyChildren.org both suggest.

Two behavioral methods that have been studied in older infants — bedtime fading and graduated extinction (a form of check-in or interval waiting) — are summarized by Sleep Foundation and belong with a conversation about [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training) and [sleep training methods](/sleep-training-methods-explained). They are not for newborns.

Things that do not reliably "make" a baby sleep through the night: starting solids early (HealthyChildren.org notes that solids do not increase sleeping through the night for breastfed infants), or keeping the baby up late so they are "extra tired." An [overtired baby](/overtired-baby) often sleeps worse.

Hunger is still a real reason to wake, especially in younger babies and in babies who are not yet gaining steadily. Dropping feeds is a separate decision; see [night weaning](/night-weaning) and talk to your pediatrician before you cut night calories.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

If your baby is not gaining, has fewer wet diapers than expected, or seems unwell, nights are a feeding or health issue first. HealthyChildren.org lists steady growth, enough daytime feeds (about 8–12 breastfeeds or 5–8 bottle feeds, depending on age), at least four wet diapers, and regular stools as signs that a healthy baby often does not need to be woken for a feed — that is about waking them, not about whether they will still wake you. If snoring, long breathing pauses, very sweaty sleep, or extreme daytime sleepiness show up, ask about those too. And if you are not coping, that is a valid reason to call, even if the baby's pattern is "normal."

## Frequently asked questions

**Do newborns sleep through the night?**  
No, not in the 8- or 12-hour sense. Short stretches and night feeds are expected.

**Is a 6-hour stretch "sleeping through"?**  
In much of the research, yes. In everyday conversation, maybe not. Both uses are common, which is why the phrase causes so many arguments.

**My 12-month-old still wakes. Is that a problem?**  
Not by itself. Pennestri's study found that not meeting a 6-hour definition at 12 months was still fairly common and was not linked to poorer development in that paper. Look at total sleep, mood, and feeding, and see our [12 month old sleep schedule](/12-month-old-sleep-schedule) and [12 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/12-months).

**Will a dream feed make my baby sleep through?**  
Sometimes it shifts the first waking later; it does not rewrite biology. See [dream feed](/dream-feed).

**Should I let my baby "cry it out" so they will sleep through?**  
That is a method choice with a right age and family fit, not a requirement for the milestone. See [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training) and [cry it out](/cry-it-out-method).

**Can a 6 month old (or 4, 8, 12 month old) sleep through the night without feeding?**
“Through the night” at 1–3 months often means about **5–6 hours** (KidsHealth), not a 12-hour stretch. Whether a later baby can go longer without a night feed is a weight-gain and feeding question for your pediatrician, not a search-result rule. Use this page, [night weaning](/night-weaning), and [should you wake a sleeping baby](/should-you-wake-a-sleeping-baby). Do not drop a needed night feed because a blog said “6 months.”
