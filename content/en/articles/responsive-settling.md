# Responsive Settling: Help That Can Fade, Not a Second Methods Table

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

This page is the **named approach** used in Australian and UK parent guidance: you read the baby's cues, you offer comfort, and, as they grow, you gradually offer less hands-on help. Pregnancy, Birth and Baby (PBB) calls it a gentle, supportive way to help a baby go to sleep or return to sleep. Raising Children Network describes the same idea for 0–6 months and, later, for reducing help between 6 and 18 months.

It is not [sleep training methods explained](/sleep-training-methods-explained) (the side-by-side table). It is not [gentle sleep training](/gentle-sleep-training) (chair / camping out, bedtime fading, and pick up / put down in outline). It is not the [5 S's](/5-s-soothing-a-crying-baby). It is not [self-soothing](/self-soothing) (how the skill develops). Those pages stay where they are. PBB's "transition settling (camping out)" is the same *family* as the chair method already outlined on the gentle-training page — we will name it here and send you there, not copy the steps.

## Short answer

Responsive settling starts with **tired signs**, a comfortable baby (fed, dry, not overheated), and safe sleep: **on the back**, on a firm, flat surface, in a clear cot or crib (PBB; the full list is on [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies)). Then you match the help to the age and the moment.

PBB groups the practical help like this:

- **Settling in arms** — hold and gently rock until the baby is calm or asleep, then place them on their back in the cot. Most useful in the first weeks, and often up to around 3 to 6 months. If they protest in the cot, comfort them; if they become very distressed, pick them up and start again.
- **Hands-on settling** — place the baby in the cot **awake**, on their back, and stay with them. If they are calm, give them a chance. If they cry, a hand on their body, a quiet voice, gentle "shh," or rhythmic patting of the thigh, shoulder, tummy, or mattress can help. PBB says this can suit many ages, and that younger babies up to about 6 months may respond best. You can sit by the cot and pat through the rails. If you or the baby are not getting calmer, it is all right to stop and try something else.
- **Comfort settling** — generally for **about 6 months or older**: a short comfort in the cot, then you leave when they are calm. Brief fussing is common. If the cry escalates, go back. This is not "cry it out"; if it is not helping, PBB says to try again another time, or return to hands-on or in-arms help.
- **Transition settling ("camping out")** — also usually from about 6 months: you stay nearby and, over about 1 to 4 weeks, sit or lie farther away, without doing the physical work of putting them to sleep. The how-to already lives on [gentle sleep training](/gentle-sleep-training).

Raising Children Network's 6–18 month advice is the same direction in different words: wait until one way of settling is working easily before you move to a less intensive way (for example, from feeding all the way to sleep, to rocking until drowsy, to hands-on help).

There is no official AAP protocol that uses the name "responsive settling." The steps above are public-health parent guidance from AU sources, labeled as such.

## A few practical limits

Learn your baby's early tired signs rather than waiting for a full melt-down (PBB lists yawning, jerky movements, rubbing eyes, and, in older babies, clumsiness or extra clinginess; our cues page has the sourced list). A short pre-sleep pattern — nappy, a story, a cuddle — helps the later steps (PBB). Wrapping or a sleep bag depends on age and safe-sleep rules; see [how to swaddle](/how-to-swaddle-a-baby) and [sleep sacks](/sleep-sacks).

PBB notes a true point that also sits on [sleep associations](/sleep-associations): if a baby *always* falls asleep in the same way (nursed, rocked, held), they may look for that same condition after a night waking. Responsive settling does not require you to withdraw all comfort from a young baby. It does mean thinking, as they get older, about how much help you still want to give at the start of sleep.

## When to talk to your pediatrician or child-health nurse

Ask if your baby is not settling at all, if you are unsure they are old enough for less hands-on help, or if feeding, growth, or breathing worries you. In Australia, Pregnancy, Birth and Baby and your local child-health nurse are the usual first calls alongside your doctor.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is responsive settling the same as sleep training?**  
It is a settling approach that can, over time, reduce how much help you give. It is not a new official method in the AAP table, and it is not cry it out.

**Can I use it with a newborn?**  
Settling in arms is written for the early weeks. Hands-on settling can start when it suits, with the baby on their back in a safe cot. You cannot spoil a young baby by holding them (Caring for Kids, on a related healthy-sleep page). Newborns are not ready for a formal independent-sleep program — see [when to start sleep training](/when-to-start-sleep-training).

**What about camping out?**  
PBB lists it as a later, "transition" form of responsive settling. Our step outline is on the gentle-training page, so we do not repeat it here.
