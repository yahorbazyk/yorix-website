# 3 Month Sleep Regression: Is It Real, and What Is Actually Going On?

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

Your baby was finally giving you a longer stretch at night, and then, at around 12 weeks, the night falls apart again. Searching for answers turns up the "3 month sleep regression." This page doesn't repeat our full [4 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/4-months) guide or the [3 month old sleep schedule](/3-month-old-sleep-schedule). It answers three narrower questions: is a 3-month regression a real thing, how does it relate to the better-known 4-month one, and what should you do this week?

## Short answer

There is **no research describing a separate "3 month sleep regression."** What parents notice at 12–14 weeks is most likely the **early start of the same sleep change usually called the 4 month regression**. Sleep Foundation says babies begin to have four sleep stages at around three months, and that some babies have trouble sleeping a little earlier or later than 4 months, or have no noticeable regression at all. Before calling it a regression, check two things. Was sleep really better before? Is something else going on, such as illness, hunger, or a baby who is starting to roll? Then keep nights calm and offer some chances to fall asleep in the crib. Hold off on formal sleep training for now.

## Is the 3 month sleep regression real?

The night waking is real. Whether it deserves its own label is a different question.

- **We found no study that defines a regression at 3 months.** In slides from a webinar published by Public Health Ontario, nursing researcher Elizabeth Keys (University of British Columbia Okanagan) lists "sleep regression" as a popularized term that does not exist in the scientific literature. Cleveland Clinic makes a similar point: research has not shown regressions arriving on a schedule at one age for every baby.
- **A biological shift is under way.** Sleep Foundation explains that newborns have two sleep stages (REM and non-REM), while babies over about three months begin to show the four stages adults have. Most babies move toward a more adult-like pattern somewhere between three months and one year.
- **The timing is loose.** Sleep Foundation's 4-month regression page says some babies have trouble a few months before or after 4 months, and some show no detectable regression at all.
- **App example (not clinical guidance):** Huckleberry describes the "3 month regression" as the same maturing of sleep that people call the 4 month regression, which can simply show up earlier.

So "3 month sleep regression" is best read as a **search term**, not a diagnosis. It describes a real, temporary rough patch that some babies hit as their sleep matures.

## 3 month vs 4 month regression: what's the difference?

| | "3 month regression" | "4 month regression" |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is behind it | The same maturing of sleep stages, starting early | The maturing of sleep stages |
| Research behind the label | None for a separate event | Also no fixed-age evidence, but widely described by Sleep Foundation and Cleveland Clinic |
| What still counts as normal at this age | Short, uneven naps; one or more night feeds; "through the night" = 5–6 hours | Night waking that often returns around 4–5 months (NHS services) |
| Sleep training? | Too early: readiness is usually placed at 4–6 months (Sleep Foundation) | Some experts suggest waiting until 6 months (Sleep Foundation) |
| Main job for parents | Rule out other causes; protect the night; low-pressure practice | Same, plus a steadier daytime rhythm |

In short, they are not two regressions. If your baby has a rough patch at 3 months, you are unlikely to face a second, separate one a few weeks later. You may simply be in the same transition for a little longer.

## Regression, or just normal 3-month sleep?

Many "regressions" at this age turn out to be ordinary infant sleep. Ask yourself:

1. **Was sleep clearly better for a week or more before this?** If naps were always 30–45 minutes, short naps are not a new problem. In app data from 841 children, Mindell and colleagues found that in the first 3 months, sleep mostly came in blocks of less than 3.5 hours, around the clock. Huckleberry (app example) makes the same point: short naps that have always been short are not a regression.
2. **Is your expectation realistic?** KidsHealth says that at 1–3 months, "sleeping through the night" means only about 5 or 6 hours, and that the range of normal is still very wide.
3. **Is it sudden and broad?** A real shift tends to show up together as more night waking, shorter naps, and a fussier, more tired baby. Sleep Foundation describes this pattern for the 4-month change.

If you answered "yes, it was better", "yes, I expect 5–6 hours, not 11", and "yes, it's sudden", the early start of the 4-month shift is a reasonable explanation.

## Check these first

Cleveland Clinic's Dr. Heidi Szugye warns against assuming every bad night is a regression. A baby may wake for many reasons, including illness, a dirty diaper, or an upset stomach. Run through:

- **Illness.** A fever in a baby younger than 12 weeks needs a call to your doctor right away, and HealthyChildren.org says not to give fever medicine before your baby is seen. Also watch for poor feeding, fewer wet diapers, or unusual sleepiness.
- **Hunger.** Night feeds are still normal at this age. A baby who suddenly wants more feeds may simply need them.
- **Rolling.** KidsHealth notes that babies typically start trying to roll at about 3 to 4 months. A swaddled baby who is trying to roll is a safety issue, not just a sleep one: stop swaddling.
- **Temperature, a wet diaper, or a changed sleep space.** These are small things that wake light sleepers.

## What to do this week

**1. Keep nights boring and short.** Feed and comfort in low light, with little talk, and settle your baby back in their own sleep space. This does not mean leaving a 3-month-old to cry for long periods.

**2. Offer some sleeps "drowsy but awake."** KidsHealth explains that a baby rocked to sleep at bedtime may expect rocking after every night waking. Try putting your baby down sleepy but not fully asleep for one or two sleeps a day, often the first nap or bedtime. If it fails, help them to sleep and try again tomorrow.

**3. Consider moving the late feed.** If your baby wakes for a feed shortly after you have gone to bed, KidsHealth suggests waking them for the late-night feed at a time that fits your own sleep. It may take a few nights to settle in.

**4. Don't start formal sleep training yet.** Sleep Foundation explains that babies don't have a strong body clock at birth and that most families may not be able to sleep train until about six months.

**5. Share the night.** If there are two adults, split the night into shifts so each person gets one longer block of sleep *(practical suggestion)*.

## How long does it last?

No study has measured how long a "3 month regression" lasts, because none defines one. For the related 4-month shift, Sleep Foundation describes a few days to a few weeks, and Cleveland Clinic says regressions usually last a week or two. Huckleberry (app example) suggests that some babies need until about 5–6 months to get good at falling asleep on their own. If sleep is still very broken after several weeks, or is getting worse, talk to your pediatrician.

## What can make it worse

- **Treating a feed or illness as "just a regression"** and missing what your baby actually needs.
- **Switching strategies every night.** Babies cope better with a predictable response.
- **Continuing to swaddle** once your baby is trying to roll.
- **Unsafe sleep fixes:** a couch, an inclined sleeper, pillows, loose blankets, or weighted sleep products. KidsHealth warns against weighted blankets, sleepers, and swaddles.
- **Starting formal sleep training too early** out of exhaustion.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Call promptly for any fever before 12 weeks (HealthyChildren.org). Also call for poor feeding or fewer wet diapers, a baby who is unusually hard to wake, breathing that looks like hard work or sounds noisy during sleep, or crying that you cannot soothe and that seems different from usual. It's also worth raising if the disruption lasts for weeks, or if you are struggling to cope.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a 3 month sleep regression?**  
Not as a separate, research-defined event. Changes at 12–14 weeks are most likely the early start of the sleep maturing usually called the 4 month regression (Sleep Foundation).

**Will my baby have a 4 month regression too?**  
Probably not a second, separate one. It is the same transition, and some babies go through it earlier, later, or barely at all (Sleep Foundation).

**How long does the 3 month sleep regression last?**  
There is no data for 3 months specifically. For the 4-month change, sources describe a few days to a few weeks (Sleep Foundation) or usually a week or two (Cleveland Clinic).

**Why is my 3 month old suddenly waking every hour?**  
Rule out illness, hunger, and rolling while swaddled first. If none applies and sleep had been better, maturing sleep is a likely reason.

**Can I sleep train a 3 month old?**  
It's generally considered too early. Sleep Foundation places readiness later, around 4–6 months.

**Is there a 12 week sleep regression?**
Twelve weeks is the usual "3 months" mark. Use this page, not a separate weekly diagnosis. The week-12 snapshot — stretches, not a schedule — is [12 week old baby sleep](/12-week-old-baby-sleep). Short day pieces belong on [short naps](/short-naps). Early "4-month" changes can start before 16 weeks; that essay is [4 month sleep regression](/sleep-regression/4-months).
