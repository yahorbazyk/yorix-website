# Bedtime Routine for Baby: A Simple, Evidence-Backed Wind-Down

**By the Yorix team**  
**Published:** [Month DD, YYYY] · **Updated:** [Month DD, YYYY]

*This article is for general information and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for care from your baby's pediatrician or another qualified clinician. If you are worried about your baby's sleep, feeding, growth, or breathing, contact your pediatrician.*

A **bedtime routine for baby** is a short, predictable sequence you repeat before sleep: the same few steps, in the same order, most nights. It is one of the least flashy ideas in infant sleep, and one of the better studied. You do not need a perfect nursery or a 10-step ritual. You need something you can actually do when you are tired.

## Short answer

Start a simple routine as early as you like — NHS and Sleep Foundation both encourage one from the early months — and keep it to about **20 to 30 minutes**, or a little longer if a bath is included. A common pattern is feed, wash or change, pajamas, dim light, a book or lullaby, then into the crib. Research led by Jodi Mindell has found that a consistent nightly routine can help young children fall asleep faster, sleep longer, and wake less, with improvements sometimes seen in only a few nights, and that doing it more consistently is associated with better sleep. The AAP later promotes **Brush, Book, Bed** once teeth and books are part of the evening. Put your baby down **drowsy but awake** once you are past the early newborn weeks, so they get practice falling asleep in their own sleep space.

## What a bedtime routine is — and why it works

Sleep Foundation describes a routine as a consistent, repetitive set of activities carried out before bed every night. The repetition itself is the point. It gives the baby a cue that sleep is next, gives you a script when you are exhausted, and, in older babies, builds self-care and a sense of security.

Mindell and Williamson (2018), in *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, reviewed the wider benefits: better sleep, better parent–child connection, and skills that matter later, such as attention. An earlier trial (Mindell, Telofski, Wiegand, and Kurtz, 2009) found that a nightly routine improved infant and toddler sleep and maternal mood. A 2015 paper (Mindell, Li, Sadeh, Kwon, and Goh) found a **dose-dependent** pattern: more consistent use of a routine was associated with better sleep. That is a useful, unromantic finding. A routine you actually do five nights a week will beat a beautiful routine you only attempt on weekends.

## A simple routine you can scale by age

NHS suggests a bath, a change into night clothes and a fresh nappy, putting the baby down, a story, dim lights, a kiss, and a lullaby or a wind-up mobile. Sleep Foundation's list for children is similar: a feed or snack, a bath or diaper change, teeth and toilet (when relevant), a book, a song, a cuddle, a short chat about the day, then lights out.

You can grow into that, rather than start with all of it.

**Newborns.** Keep it short and quiet. A feed, a change, a dim room, and a few minutes of holding or a soft song is enough. NHS's day/night contrast matters more than a clock: ordinary light and noise by day, low light and little talking at night. Do not worry if bedtime moves around in the first weeks; see [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule).

**About 3 to 6 months.** Many families pick a slightly more stable "start of night." HealthyChildren.org suggests, from around 4 months, putting babies down drowsy rather than already asleep, so they can learn to fall asleep in their own crib. A bath does not have to happen every night to "count." If a bath is overstimulating, wash the face and hands and save the tub for earlier in the day. Do baths help every baby sleep? TCB and others discuss that; the honest answer is "sometimes," and it is not required.

**Older babies and toddlers.** Add toothbrushing when teeth are present. The AAP's Brush, Book, Bed is a tidy way to end the evening: brush, read, then bed, with no snacks or bottles after brushing. Sleep Foundation notes that a stuffed animal or comfort blanket can help some toddlers with separation — but only when it is age-safe; loose objects stay out of the crib in the first year. See [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) and [sleep sacks](/sleep-sacks).

A worked example, not a rule:

1. Close the kitchen, dim the main lights (5 minutes).
2. Bath or a simple wash, then pajamas (10 minutes).
3. Feed if a bedtime feed is still part of your plan (10–15 minutes). Try to finish the feed before the baby is deeply asleep if you are working on falling asleep in the crib.
4. Book or song in a dim room (5–10 minutes).
5. Into the crib, kiss, leave.

If this regularly lasts an hour, shorten it. Sleep Foundation warns that a drawn-out routine delays bedtime and is hard to keep up on a busy night — and an [overtired baby](/overtired-baby) is harder to settle, so starting before the yawns turn into a second wind matters.

## Dos and don'ts that actually have a basis

**Do** repeat the same order most nights, and share the routine between caregivers when you can, so it does not belong to only one parent. **Do** keep the bedroom dark, cool enough for your climate, and fairly quiet; a dim night light is fine if you need it for feeds. **Do** shift bedtime in small steps (Sleep Foundation suggests about 15 minutes) if you need to move it.

**Don't** start when your baby is already past the point of no return. **Don't** park a baby in front of a phone or TV to "wind down"; the AAP wants screens off at least an hour before bed and out of the bedroom. **Don't** let the last step be a bottle left in the crib. **Don't** use the routine as a time for rough play or a scary story. **Don't** treat weekend late nights as a full reset; Sleep Foundation cautions against shifting wake time by more than about an hour when you can help it, at least for older children. For babies, a slightly later weekend morning is common, but a wildly different schedule makes Monday harder.

## A routine is not a whole sleep plan

It will not, by itself, fix [hourly waking](/baby-waking-every-hour), [false starts](/false-starts), or a bedtime that is two hours too late. It works with [age-appropriate totals](/how-much-sleep-does-a-baby-need), a safe crib, and, if you choose, a settling method you have already read about. It is also not a failure if you skip the bath when you get home late. Do the last two or three steps and start again tomorrow.

A popular daytime slogan, **eat–play–sleep** (or feed–play–sleep), is the same idea in a different order of the day: a flexible, cue-led pattern, not a rigid law, and not a newborn schedule. The longer caveat lives on [newborn sleep schedule](/newborn-sleep-schedule).

The daytime pattern has its own guide: [eat, play, sleep](/eat-play-sleep). For the evening clock, see [what time should a baby go to bed](/what-time-should-baby-go-to-bed).

## When to talk to your pediatrician

If bedtime is a nightly battle despite a consistent, short routine, or if your baby seems in pain, has noisy breathing, or cannot settle in a way that feels new and extreme, ask for help. A routine is a habit, not a treatment for illness or a medical sleep disorder.

## Frequently asked questions

**When should I start a bedtime routine?**  
As early as you want a cue for "this is night." Even a two-step newborn version counts. It becomes more useful as sleep starts to organize in the first months.

**How long should a baby bedtime routine be?**  
About 20–30 minutes is a realistic target; a bit longer with a bath. If it regularly runs past 45–60 minutes, it is probably delaying sleep.

**What if my partner and I do it differently?**  
The same order matters more than the same singing voice. Agree on the steps and on who does what, then let each adult use their own style.

**Does a routine have to include a bath?**  
No. NHS includes a bath as an option, not a requirement.

**Can I use the same routine when we travel?**  
Yes, and that is one of its strengths. A book and the same song still work in a pack-and-play. Keep the [safe sleep](/safe-sleep-for-babies) rules wherever you are.

**What is a good bedtime for a 12 week old?**
There is no official AAP clock. The week-12 snapshot is on [12 week old baby sleep](/12-week-old-baby-sleep). A short, same-order routine matters more than a 7:00 p.m. target.

**How do we protect baby sleep when a parent works nights, or when there is only one adult?**
Keep the *baby’s* day and night the right way around — light and ordinary noise by day, a dim, boring night — even if a parent is on a night shift. Do not flip the baby onto the parent’s work clock. The same short bedtime routine can be done by whoever is on duty; the sequence matters more than which adult does it. See [bedtime routine for baby](/bedtime-routine-for-baby). Safe sleep does not relax because you are exhausted: back, firm, flat, clear, and never a sofa or armchair (the sofa risk is already on [bed-sharing and co-sleeping](/bed-sharing-and-co-sleeping)). If you are dangerously tired, ask for help for you as well — mental-health and exhaustion support still applies when there is no second adult to “take a shift.”
